Barsisa was a monk among the Children of Israel, a believer in one God, known in his village for piety.

Three brothers going out to fight needed someone to look after their sister. The villagers pointed them to Barsisa. He refused outright: *I seek refuge from the accursed Shaytan.* He could see the danger from a mile off.

So the whisper changed its angle. *If they cannot find anyone good and leave her with someone bad, would that not be your fault?* It came dressed as compassion, and he agreed.

He housed her opposite the church and left her food outside it. Then: *why not leave it closer, so people do not see her walking back and forth alone?* Agreed. Then: *why not set it on her table, so she is not seen going in and out?* Agreed. Then: *she is alone, with nobody to speak to, why not talk to her?* He spoke through a door. Then, since they had to shout to be heard, he went in.

From there it went as such things go. Fornication, a pregnancy, a child killed to hide it, the mother killed to hide that, a false grave, and a lie to her brothers.

And at the very end, facing execution, the whisperer appeared in person and offered one last deal: prostrate to me and I will save you. He did. And the answer he got is the verse:

> *Like Shaytan, when he says to man: disbelieve. But when he disbelieves, he says: I am free of you; indeed I fear Allah, Lord of the worlds.* (59:16)

Read the sequence again and notice the design. Had the first request been prostration, Barsisa would have refused instantly. He did refuse the first real request. What defeated him was that every step was small, reasonable, and an improvement on the last.

The countermeasure the Book prescribes is applied at the *whisper*, not at the sin.
