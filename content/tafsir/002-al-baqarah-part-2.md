*Revealed in Madinah · This part contains **Ayat al-Kursi** (2:255), the **longest ayah in the Qur'an** (2:282), and **2:281 — the last ayah of the Qur'an to be revealed***

---

## 1. "I Am Near" (2:186)

Placed deliberately between the fasting verses, and the only place in the Qur'an where Allah answers a question about Himself **without the intermediary "Say."**

> **"And when My servants ask you about Me — I am indeed near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls on Me."**

The Companions were once raising their voices in takbir on a march, and the Prophet ﷺ said: *"O people! Be merciful to yourselves — you are not calling a deaf or an absent one, but One Who is All-Hearer, All-Seer. **The One Whom you call is closer to one of you than the neck of his animal.**"* And the hadith Qudsi: **"I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him whenever he invokes Me."**

**How a supplication is answered.** No Muslim supplicates for something free of sin or severing kinship *"but Allah will grant him one of three: He hastens the response; or saves it for him in the Hereafter; or turns away from him an equivalent amount of evil."* The Companions said, *"Then we will make more du'a!"* — and he ﷺ said: **"There is more with Allah."**

**How it is prevented.** *"One's supplication is accepted as long as he does not get hasty and say, 'I have supplicated but it has not been accepted.'"* Explained: *"He says, 'I supplicated and supplicated but I do not see it being accepted.' He thus loses interest and abandons supplicating."*

**Three whose du'a is not rejected:** the just ruler; the fasting person until he breaks his fast; and the oppressed — *"for Allah raises it above the clouds, and the doors of heaven are opened for it, and Allah says: 'By My grace! I will certainly grant it for you, even if after a while.'"*

## 2. Completing the Fast (2:187)

The ayah is a **relief**. In the first years, eating, drinking and intimacy were forbidden from the moment one slept or prayed 'Isha until the following sunset. Qays bin Sirmah came home exhausted from his land, found no food ready, fell asleep waiting — and collapsed at midday the next day. 'Umar and others admitted having gone to their wives after 'Isha. Then came: **"Allah knows that you used to deceive yourselves, so He turned to you and forgave you."**

**"They are Libas for you and you are Libas for them"** — a garment, a covering, a resort. Ibn 'Abbas and the majority: *"Your wives are a resort for you and you for them."* Ar-Rabi': *"They are your cover and you are their cover."*

**The white and black thread.** Some men literally tied black and white threads to their legs and watched them all night. 'Adi bin Hatim put two strings under his pillow. The Prophet ﷺ told him: *"Your pillow is very wide, if the white and black threads are under it!"* — then explained plainly: **they are the blackness of the night and the whiteness of the day.**

**Suhur.** *"Eat the Suhur, for there is a blessing in it."* It is the very thing that distinguishes the Muslim fast: **"The distinction between our fast and the fast of the People of the Book is the meal of Suhur."** And: *"It is a blessed meal — do not abandon it, even if one just takes a sip of water. Indeed, Allah and His angels send blessings upon those who eat Suhur."* Zayd bin Thabit: between Suhur and the adhan was **the time it takes to recite fifty ayat.** And the practical rule: Bilal's adhan is at night; eat until you hear Ibn Umm Maktum's.

**Fasting while Junub is valid** — the Four Imams and the majority. The Prophet ﷺ himself would wake in that state, bathe, and fast. When a man protested *"you are not like us, Allah has forgiven you,"* he ﷺ replied: **"By Allah! I hope I have the most fear of Allah among you, and the best knowledge of what taqwa is."**

**Break the fast promptly.** *"If the night comes from the east and the day departs from the west, then the fasting person breaks his fast."* And the hadith Qudsi: **"The dearest of My servants to Me are those who hasten most in breaking the fast."**

**Al-Wisal (fasting through the night without breaking) is forbidden** — and Ibn Kathir notes it was a particular privilege of the Prophet ﷺ, who was *"given food and drink"* by his Lord — spiritual, not material, otherwise it would not have been wisal at all. He is permitted to continue until Suhur, but not beyond.

**I'tikaf.** No intimacy during it, whether inside the masjid or outside it. He may leave for necessity but not linger, kiss, embrace, or busy himself; he may ask after the sick in passing but not visit them. The Prophet ﷺ observed i'tikaf in the last ten nights until he died, and his wives after him.

The passage of Safiyyah belongs here, and it is one of the finest teaching moments in the sirah: the Prophet ﷺ walked his wife home at night from his i'tikaf; two Ansari men saw them and hurried past out of shyness. He called them back: **"Do not run away! She is Safiyyah bint Huyayy."** They said, *"Subhan Allah, O Messenger of Allah!"* And he ﷺ explained: *"Shaytan reaches everywhere in the human body that the blood reaches. I feared he might cast an evil thought into your hearts."* Ash-Shafi'i's comment: he ﷺ was teaching his Ummah **to eliminate an evil thought at its source**, so as never to fall into the prohibited.

*"These are the limits of Allah, so approach them not."*

## 3. Bribery, and the Limits of a Judge's Ruling (2:188)

*"Do not eat up one another's property unjustly, nor give bribery to the rulers, that you may knowingly eat up a part of others' property sinfully."* Ibn 'Abbas: this concerns the debtor who denies an undocumented loan and litigates, **knowing he is lying.**

The Prophet's ﷺ own disclaimer is the anchor: *"I am only human. You bring me your disputes, and some of you may be more eloquent in argument than others, so I might rule in his favor. **If I give a Muslim's right to another, I am giving him a piece of fire; so let him not take it.**"*

The principle Ibn Kathir extracts: **a court ruling does not change reality.** It cannot make the forbidden lawful nor the lawful forbidden. Qatadah: *"The judge only rules by his best judgment and the testimony before him — he is human and bound to err. Know that if a judge rules wrongly in someone's favor, the dispute will meet him again when both parties stand before Allah."*

## 4. Crescents, and the Back Door (2:189)

*"They ask you about the crescents. Say: these are signs to mark fixed periods for mankind and for the Hajj."* Ibn 'Abbas: so they may mark their worship, the 'iddah of their women, and the times of Hajj. Hence the practical ruling: *"Fast on seeing it and break the fast on seeing it; and if it is obscured, complete thirty days."*

Then, abruptly: **"It is not Birr that you enter houses from the back; but Birr is from Taqwa. So enter houses through their doors."** A Jahiliyyah custom — on assuming ihram (or on returning from an abandoned journey) they would not use the door, but climb the back wall. The literal correction carries the same lesson as 2:177: **righteousness is never in the outward gesture; it is in obeying what Allah actually commanded.**

## 5. The Legislation of Fighting (2:190–195)

**"Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but transgress not the limits."** Abu Al-'Aliyah: **this was the first ayah about fighting revealed in Madinah**, and from its revelation the Prophet ﷺ fought only those who fought him, and avoided non-combatants.

Ibn Kathir rejects the claim that it was abrogated by 9:5. The ayah is not a temporary permission but a **description of proportion**: *"Your energy should be spent on fighting them just as their energy is spent on fighting you"* — reciprocity, not aggression (as in 9:36, *"fight the polytheists collectively as they fight you collectively"*).

**The limits (i'tida') are spelled out.** Al-Hasan Al-Basri lists them, echoed by Ibn 'Abbas and 'Umar bin 'Abdul-'Aziz: **mutilating the dead; stealing from the spoils; killing women, children and the elderly who do not fight; killing priests and residents of houses of worship; burning trees; and killing animals without benefit.** And the hadith of Buraydah: *"Fight, but do not steal from the spoils, commit treachery, mutilate the dead, kill a child, or kill those in houses of worship."* When a woman was found dead on one campaign, the Prophet ﷺ forbade killing women and children outright.

**"And Al-Fitnah is worse than killing."** Fitnah here is **shirk** — the near-unanimous reading of Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Ad-Dahhak and others. The point is that Jihad involves the grave matter of shedding blood, and the ayah answers *why*: what is being resisted is a greater destruction than killing.

**Fighting at the Sacred Mosque** is forbidden *unless they attack you there.* Makkah's sanctity dates from the creation of the heavens and earth; *"fighting in it was made lawful for me only for an hour of one day"* — and even then, on the day of the conquest, the Prophet ﷺ declared: **"Whoever closed his door is safe. Whoever entered the Mosque is safe. Whoever entered the house of Abu Sufyan is safe."**

**"Fight them until there is no more Fitnah and the religion is for Allah."** Ibn Kathir attaches the definitive hadith of intention: asked which of three men — one fighting for bravery, one for tribe, one to show off — fights in Allah's cause, the Prophet ﷺ answered: **"He who fights so that Allah's Word is superior, he is in Allah's cause."**

And the terms of ending it: **"But if they cease, let there be no transgression except against the wrongdoers."** Ibn 'Umar's exchange during the fitnah of Ibn Az-Zubayr is preserved as the corrective. Two men urged him to fight, quoting this very ayah. He answered: *"We fought until there was no more Fitnah and the religion became for Allah alone. **You want to fight until there is Fitnah and the religion becomes for other than Allah.**"* Asked why he performed Hajj and 'Umrah but not Jihad, he listed the five pillars — and added that the ayah described the era *"when Islam was weak and a man was tried in his religion by killing or torture. When Islam became strong, there was no more Fitnah."*

**Sacred months (2:194)** — *"and for prohibited things there is the Law of equality."* Revealed after the Hudaybiyyah year, when the idolaters barred the Prophet ﷺ during Dhul-Qa'dah and he returned the following year in the same month. Jabir: *"The Messenger of Allah ﷺ would not engage in warfare during a Sacred Month unless he was first attacked."*

**"And spend in the cause of Allah, and do not throw yourselves into destruction" (2:195).** The most famous misreading of this ayah was corrected in person. At the siege of Constantinople, a Muslim charged alone into the Byzantine lines and people cried, *"He is throwing himself into destruction!"* **Abu Ayyub Al-Ansari** — in whose house the Prophet ﷺ had lodged — stood and said: *"O people! You explain this ayah the wrong way. **It was revealed about us, the Ansar.** When Allah gave victory to His religion, we said to each other: 'We have neglected our families and our estates; let us go back and attend to them.' Then this ayah was revealed. **The destruction is staying with our families and estates and abandoning Jihad.**"* Hudhayfah and the majority agree: **the ayah is about refusing to spend.**

## 6. Hajj and 'Umrah (2:196–203)

**"Complete Hajj and 'Umrah for Allah."** Because they must be *completed*, the scholars agree that **once ihram is assumed, finishing the rites becomes obligatory.**

**If prevented (Hasr).** Revealed in year 6 AH at Hudaybiyyah, when the polytheists barred the Prophet ﷺ from the House. They sacrificed the seventy camels they had brought and shaved outside the sanctuary. The Companions hesitated to shave, hoping the order would be lifted; when he ﷺ shaved first, they followed. Some only shortened, hence: *"May Allah have mercy on those who shaved"* — repeated, before he added, *"and on those who shortened."*

The scope of *"prevented"* is broader than an enemy: **"Whoever suffered a broken bone or a limb has ended his ihram and must perform Hajj again"** — and Ibn 'Abbas and Abu Hurayrah both confirmed the ruling. Ath-Thawri: *"anything that harms the person."* And Duba'ah bint Az-Zubayr, ill and wanting to go, was told: **"Perform Hajj and make the condition: 'My place is where You prevent me.'"**

**The Fidyah for shaving (2:196).** Ka'b bin 'Ujrah, lice falling onto his face, was told by the Prophet ﷺ: *"I never thought your struggle had reached this."* Then: shave, and either fast three days, feed six poor persons, or sacrifice a sheep. Ka'b's own comment is a model of legal reasoning: **"This was revealed concerning my case in particular, but it is for you in general."** Ibn Kathir notes the elegant difference in order: the Qur'an lists from **easiest to hardest** (fasting, feeding, sacrifice); the Prophet ﷺ advised Ka'b from **most rewarding to least**. *"Each text is suitable in its place."*

**Tamattu'** — combining 'Umrah with Hajj — requires a Hady, at minimum a sheep, or ten days of fasting (three during Hajj, seven on return). 'Imran bin Husayn: *"We performed Tamattu' with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and the Qur'an was revealed about it, and nothing came to forbid it, nor did he forbid it until he died — **and then a man said what he wished according to his own opinion.**"* Al-Bukhari notes he meant 'Umar. But Ibn Kathir is careful: **'Umar never declared it unlawful**; he discouraged it so that people would make more separate journeys to the House, as 'Umar himself explained. Residents of the Haram are excluded from Tamattu'.

**The months of Hajj** are Shawwal, Dhul-Qa'dah, and the first ten days of Dhul-Hijjah (Ibn 'Umar, and a long list of Companions and Tabi'in). Ibn Jarir's linguistic note: the Arabs call two months and part of a third "months," just as one says "I visited him this year."

**Three prohibitions in ihram (2:197):**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Rafath** | Sexual intercourse — and, per Ibn 'Umar, Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ata' and Tawus, even *speaking of it*: "when I end ihram I will do such with you" |
| **Fusuq** | Sin of every kind. Some specified cursing (*"cursing a Muslim is fusuq"*), or slaughtering for idols. Ibn Kathir: those who said **all disobedience** are correct |
| **Jidal** | Disputing — Ibn Mas'ud: *"to argue with your companion until you make him angry"* |

And the promise: **"Whoever performed Hajj to this House and did not commit Rafath or Fusuq, will return sinless, as the day his mother gave birth to him."**

**"And take provisions — but the best provision is Taqwa."** Two occasions of revelation, both correcting a false piety: the people of Yemen used to come to Hajj without supplies, saying *"we are the people of tawakkul,"* and then beg; and others would throw away their provisions on assuming ihram. Ibn Kathir's structural note: having directed them to the material provision, Allah then names the provision for the longer journey — as in 7:26, where the garment of taqwa is named after the garment of the body, *"and that is better."*

**Trading during Hajj is lawful (2:198).** They had thought business during the season was sinful. A man asked the Prophet ﷺ and received no answer until Jibril brought the ayah down; then he summoned him and said: **"You are pilgrims."** And 'Umar, asked whether he traded during Hajj, replied: *"Was their livelihood except during Hajj?"*

**'Arafat.** *"Hajj is 'Arafat"* — said three times. Whoever stood there before dawn has performed the Hajj. And to the exhausted man who arrived at Muzdalifah having climbed every mountain: **"Whoever performed this prayer with us, stood with us until we moved on, and had stood at 'Arafat before that, by day or night — his Hajj is complete and his rites fulfilled."** The name itself is traced to Ibrahim, whom Jibril taught the rites and who said at each place, *"Araftu — I know it."*

**"Then depart from where the people depart" (2:199).** Quraysh and their allies, the **Hums**, considered themselves too exalted to stand at 'Arafat with the rest of the Arabs, and stayed within the sanctuary at Muzdalifah, *"for we are the people of Allah's town."* Allah commanded His Prophet ﷺ to stand **where everybody else stood.** Jubayr bin Mut'im, searching for a lost camel, saw him at 'Arafat and thought: *"By Allah, he is one of the Hums — what has brought him here?"* The abolition of a spiritual class system, enacted by standing in the same dust.

**Rites end in remembrance (2:200–203).** In Jahiliyyah the closing ceremony was **boasting of one's fathers**: *"my father used to feed the poor, settle disputes, pay blood money."* Allah replaced it: *"remember Allah as you remember your forefathers — or with far more remembrance."*

And He divides the supplicants. Some Bedouins would stand at 'Arafat asking only: *"O Allah, make it a rainy year, a fertile year, a year of good child-bearing"* — and never mention the Hereafter. Of these: **"for such there is no portion in the Hereafter."** Against them stands the du'a Anas said the Prophet ﷺ used most often: **"Our Lord! Give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and save us from the torment of the Fire."**

Ibn Kathir unpacks it generously — the good of this world covers health, a spacious dwelling, a pleasing spouse, sufficient provision, beneficial knowledge, good work, and good repute; the good of the Hereafter is Paradise, safety from the terror of the gathering, and an easy reckoning. And Al-Qasim bin 'Abdur-Rahman distills it: *"Whoever is given **a grateful heart, a remembering tongue, and a patient body** has been given good in this life, good in the Hereafter, and safety from the Fire."*

The hadith that seals it: a man had become as frail as a small bird. The Prophet ﷺ asked whether he had been supplicating for something. He said: *"Yes — O Allah, whatever punishment You have saved for me in the Hereafter, give it to me in this life."* The Prophet ﷺ said: **"Subhan Allah! You cannot bear it. Why did you not say: Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter..."** The man recited it, and was cured.

**The days of Tashriq** are days of *"eating, drinking and remembrance of Allah"* — the 'Id of the Muslims, on which fasting is forbidden.

## 7. Two Kinds of Man (2:204–207)

Ibn Kathir treats these four ayat as a single portrait diptych.

**The first man** (2:204–206) — *"whose speech may please you in this worldly life, and he calls Allah to witness as to what is in his heart, yet he is the most quarrelsome of opponents."*

Nawf Al-Bikali, who read the earlier Books, said he found a description of certain men of this Ummah in them: **"They use the religion to gain material benefit. Their tongues are sweeter than honey but their hearts more bitter than aloe. They show the people the appearance of sheep while their hearts hide the viciousness of wolves."** And he found the same description in the Qur'an — this very ayah.

*Aladd al-khisam* — the most obdurate disputant. The hadith: *"The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies; when he promises he breaks it; **and when you dispute with him, he is most quarrelsome**."* And: **"The most hated person to Allah is the one who is Aladd and Khasim."**

*"And when he turns away, his effort in the land is to cause corruption and destroy the crops and the cattle."* Ibn Kathir dwells on the verb *sa'a* — the same word used of Pharaoh (*"then he turned his back, striving hard"*) and of hastening to Jumu'ah — and notes its inversion: this man expends genuine effort, but at ruin. Mujahid: *"If the hypocrite strives for corruption, Allah withholds the rain, and crops and offspring perish."*

**The final mark:** *"And when it is said to him, 'Fear Allah,' he is led by arrogance to more crime."* The one thing he cannot tolerate is being advised.

**The second man** (2:207) — *"and of mankind is he who sells himself, seeking the pleasure of Allah."*

Revealed about **Suhayb bin Sinan Ar-Rumi**. When he embraced Islam in Makkah and wanted to migrate, the Quraysh told him he could go only if he left every dirham behind. **He left it all.** 'Umar and a group met him at the edge of Madinah and said, *"The trade has indeed been successful."* He said, *"You too — may Allah never let your trade fail. What is the matter?"* And they told him the ayah had come down about him. It is reported the Prophet ﷺ said: **"The trade has been profitable, O Suhayb!"**

Ibn Kathir notes the ayah is general: it includes every Mujahid, and every man who spends himself for Allah — as in 9:111, *"Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their properties, for theirs is Paradise."*

## 8. Enter Islam Entirely (2:208–213)

**"O you who believe! Enter As-Silm perfectly."** *Silm* is Islam; *kaffah* is **in its entirety**. Ibn 'Abbas notes this addresses in particular those from the People of the Book who believed: they were to embrace the whole of Muhammad's ﷺ Law and **not retain parts of the Tawrah alongside it.** Mujahid: perform all the good works, all the kinds of piety.

*"And follow not the footsteps of Shaytan."* The connection is direct: partial submission is a footstep of Shaytan, whose programme was already named at 2:169 — *evil, lewdness, and saying about Allah what you do not know.*

**Do not wait (2:210).** *"Do they wait for anything but that Allah should come to them in the shadows of the clouds, with the angels — and the case be judged?"* When the matter is decided, it is decided; deferral is not an option. The parallel is 89:21–23: *"and your Lord comes with the angels rank upon rank... On that Day man will remember — but how will remembrance avail him then?"*

**Changing Allah's favour into disbelief (2:211–212).** The Children of Israel saw everything: the hand, the parted sea, the struck rock, the shading cloud, the manna and quails — *"and whoever changes Allah's favour after it has come to him, then Allah is severe in punishment."* And 14:28 is quoted: those *"who changed the blessings of Allah into disbelief and caused their people to dwell in the house of destruction."*

*"Beautified is the life of this world for those who disbelieve, and they mock those who believe."* The disbelievers accumulate and withhold; the believers spend seeking Allah's Face and are laughed at for it. *"But those who have taqwa will be above them on the Day of Resurrection."*

Ibn Kathir gathers the hadiths on spending here, and their cumulative force is considerable: **"O son of Adam! Spend, and I will spend on you."** — *"O Bilal! Spend, and do not fear deprivation from the Owner of the Throne."* — *"Every day two angels descend; one says, 'O Allah, compensate everyone who spends,' and the other says, 'O Allah, destroy every miser.'"* — and the correction of what "my money" means: **"The son of Adam says, 'My money, my money!' Yet what is your money except what you eat and use up, what you wear and wear out, and what you give in charity and thus keep? Everything else is going away, and will be left for the people."**

**One community, then division (2:213).** Ibn 'Abbas: *"There were ten generations between Adam and Nuh, all on the religion of truth. Then they disputed — so Allah sent the Prophets."* Note the sequence Ibn Kathir highlights: the Books came **because** of the disagreement — and then *"only those to whom the Scripture was given differed concerning it, after clear proofs had come to them, through hatred of one another."* **The dispute did not arise from ignorance; it arose after the evidence, out of envy.**

*"Then Allah guided those who believed to the truth of that in which they differed, by His leave."* Zayd bin Aslam enumerates what this Ummah was guided to amid the disputes of others: the day of congregation (Friday, against Saturday and Sunday); the true Qiblah; the form of the prayer; the form of the fast; the truth about Ibrahim (neither Jew nor Christian but a Muslim *hanif*); and the truth about 'Isa — between the Jews who denied him and slandered his mother, and the Christians who made him a god. **Guidance here is not agreement with one party; it is the middle that both parties missed.**

Hence the Prophet's ﷺ own night-prayer opening: *"O Allah, Lord of Jibril, Mika'il and Israfil, Originator of the heavens and earth, Knower of the seen and the unseen — You judge between Your servants in what they differ. **Guide me to the truth in what is disputed, by Your leave.** Verily, You guide whom You will to a straight path."*

## 9. Paradise is Not Entered Untested (2:214)

**"Or do you think you will enter Paradise without such trials as came to those who passed away before you?"** Poverty (*ba'sa'*), illness (*darra'*), and shaking — *"until even the Messenger and those with him said: When will the help of Allah come?"*

The hadith of Khabbab bin Al-Aratt is placed here, and it is the answer given in the hardest hour. The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ to invoke Allah for relief. He said: *"A saw would be placed on the head of a man before you and he would be sawn in two, and he would be combed with iron combs between his skin and his bones — **and that would not make him leave his religion.**"* Then: *"By Allah, this matter will be completed until a traveler goes from San'a' to Hadramawt fearing none but Allah, and the wolf for his sheep. **But you are a hasty people.**"*

And Heraclius, hearing Abu Sufyan describe the alternating fortunes of the battles, gave the correct diagnosis without being a believer: **"Such is the case with Prophets — they are tested, but the final victory is theirs."**

**"Yes! Certainly, the help of Allah is near."** With 94:5–6 alongside: *"Verily, along with every hardship is relief"* — twice.

## 10. Six Questions (2:215–220)

A run of "they ask you" verses, each answered by redirecting the question.

**"What should we spend?" (2:215)** — the answer names not the amount but the **recipients**: parents, kindred, orphans, the poor, the wayfarer. Ibn 'Abbas and Mujahid note the question was really *how*, and the answer is *where*. Maymun bin Mihran, reciting it, remarked: *"These are the areas of spending. Allah did not mention among them drums, pipes, wooden pictures, or the curtains that cover walls."*

**Fighting is ordained though you dislike it (2:216)** — and then the general principle that outgrows its occasion: **"It may be that you dislike a thing which is good for you, and that you like a thing which is bad for you. And Allah knows, while you do not know."**

**"Fighting in the Sacred Month?" (2:217)** — the Nakhlah incident, given at length. The Prophet ﷺ sent 'Abdullah bin Jahsh with eight Muhajirun and sealed written orders not to be opened for two days, and with an explicit clause: **"do not compel any of your men to continue with you."** The orders said: camp at Nakhlah and *watch* the Quraysh caravan. 'Abdullah read them, said *"I hear and obey,"* read them aloud to his men and told them: *"whoever seeks martyrdom, march with me; whoever dislikes it, turn back."* None turned back.

They met a caravan on what turned out to be the **last day of Rajab**, a sacred month, and deliberated: *let them pass and they reach the sanctuary; strike and we violate the month.* They struck, killed 'Amr bin Al-Hadrami, and took two prisoners. The Prophet ﷺ refused the caravan and the prisoners, saying: **"I did not command you to fight during the Sacred Month."** The Quraysh publicized the scandal; the Muslims themselves criticized the party; the men believed themselves ruined.

Then the ayah came — and its structure is the point. It **concedes** the charge (*"fighting therein is a great transgression"*) and then weighs it: *"but a greater transgression with Allah is to hinder from the way of Allah, to disbelieve in Him, to prevent access to the Sacred Mosque, and to drive out its people — and Fitnah is worse than killing."* The Quraysh, prosecuting a violation of one month, had spent years on the greater crime. And the closing warning names what is actually at stake: *"they will never cease fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they can"* — with apostasy ending in the annulment of all deeds.

The party then asked whether it would count as a battle for them, and 2:218 came: **"Verily, those who believed, and those who emigrated and strove in the way of Allah — these hope for Allah's mercy."** Ibn Ishaq's comment: *"Allah greatly elevated their hopes to what they had wished for."*

**Wine and gambling (2:219)** — the famous gradual prohibition, traced through 'Umar's repeated prayer: **"O Allah! Give us a clear ruling regarding Al-Khamr."** First this ayah (*"in them is great sin, and some benefit for men, but their sin is greater than their benefit"*) — 'Umar heard it and repeated his prayer. Then 4:43 (*do not approach prayer while intoxicated*) — he repeated it again. Then 5:90–91, ending *"So will you not then abstain?"* — and 'Umar said: **"We have abstained, we have abstained."**

Ibn Kathir lists the "benefits" honestly, as the ayah does — digestion, warmth, sharpening, exhilaration, profit from sale, and gambling winnings spent on one's family — and notes that all of it is **outweighed by the harm to the mind and the religion.** 'Umar's definition: *khamr* is **whatever intoxicates the mind.**

**"What should we spend?" again (2:219)** — *"say: what is beyond your needs."* And the hadith of the man with successive dinars: *spend it on yourself → on your wife → on your child → "you have better knowledge"* (i.e. now use your judgement). And Jabir's version: **"Start with yourself. Then your family. Then your relatives. Then like this and like that."**

**Orphans (2:220).** After 6:152 and 4:10 came down, guardians became so afraid that they **separated the orphan's food and drink entirely from their own**, letting leftovers spoil rather than touch them. It became a hardship. So: *"Say: setting things right for them is best. And if you mix your affairs with theirs, **then they are your brothers.**"* And the warning that keeps the concession honest: *"Allah knows the one who means corruption from the one who means good."*

## 11. Marriage, Divorce, and Family (2:221–242)

The longest legal block in the surah. Ibn Kathir's treatment is dense; what follows preserves the rulings and the human occasions that produced them.

### Whom to marry (2:221)

*"Do not marry idolatresses until they believe."* The People of the Book are excluded from this prohibition by 5:5. But 'Umar, without declaring it unlawful, wrote to Hudhayfah — who had married a Jewish woman — *"Divorce her."* Hudhayfah asked, *"Do you claim she is unlawful for me?"* 'Umar said: **"No. But I fear that you might marry the loose women among them."** And 'Umar's rule of asymmetry: *"The Muslim man marries the Christian woman; the Christian man does not marry the Muslim woman."* Ibn 'Umar disliked it altogether, saying: *"I know of no greater shirk than her saying that Jesus is her Lord."*

**"A believing slave woman is better than an idolatress, though she please you."** Hence the criterion: *"A woman is married for four things — her wealth, her lineage, her beauty, and her religion. **Marry the religious one**, may your hands be filled with dust."* And: *"The world is a delight, and the best of its delights is a righteous wife."*

### Menstruation (2:222)

The Jews of Madinah would not eat with a menstruating woman or share a house with her. The ayah restricts the prohibition precisely — *"keep away from women during menses"* means **the place of intercourse only** — and the Prophet ﷺ said: **"Do everything you wish, except intercourse."** Hearing this, the Jews said: *"What is the matter with this man? He will not hear of any of our practices but he defies it."*

Two Companions then swung to the opposite error and asked whether they should have intercourse after all, to defy the Jews thoroughly. The Prophet's ﷺ face changed colour, and they left thinking he was angry. When milk was brought as a gift, he sent some after them — **and by that they knew he was not.**

The domestic picture is preserved in 'A'ishah's own words: he ﷺ would rest his head in her lap for her to comb his hair while she was menstruating; he would eat from the exact place on the bone she had eaten from, and drink from the spot on the cup where she had placed her mouth.

### "Your wives are a tilth" (2:223)

Another correction of a Jewish claim — that approaching a wife from behind (in the vagina) would produce cross-eyed children. The Ansar had adopted the belief; the Muhajirun had not; the resulting marital dispute reached the Prophet ﷺ through Umm Salamah. The ayah settled it, with the Prophet's ﷺ own clarifying words: **"From the front or from behind, as long as it is in one valve."**

Ibn Kathir then closes off the misuse of this ayah with unusual force, because it *was* misused. Nafi' was told people were quoting Ibn 'Umar as permitting anal intercourse: *"They have lied about me,"* and he told the real story. Ibn 'Umar himself, asked directly, replied simply: **"Does a Muslim do that?"** Malik, asked the same, said: *"You are not an Arab. Does intercourse occur except in the place of pregnancy? ... They lie about me, they lie about me."* And the hadiths: **"Allah does not shy from the truth"** — said three times — *"do not have anal intercourse with women."* Ibn Kathir records that the great majority of the Salaf rebuked the practice and many called it kufr.

*"And send forth for yourselves beforehand"* — 'Ata' and Ibn 'Abbas: say *Bismillah*. And the hadith: *"If one of you says on approaching his wife: 'In the Name of Allah. O Allah, protect us from Shaytan and protect what You grant us from Shaytan' — and a child is decreed for them, Shaytan will never harm him."*

### Oaths, Ila' and the waiting periods (2:224–235)

**Do not use Allah's name as an obstacle to good** (2:224). If you swore not to do something righteous — **break the oath, pay the expiation, and do the good deed.** *"By Allah! It is more sinful to Allah that a man persist in his oath regarding his relatives than that he break it and pay the expiation."* And the Prophet's ﷺ own practice: *"By Allah, I do not vow to do a thing and then see something better, but that I do what is better and expiate my oath."*

**Laghw oaths** — the reflexive "no, by Allah" and "yes, by Allah" of ordinary speech — carry no liability. The illustration is exquisite: new Muslims whose tongues were still habituated to swearing by Al-Lat were told, *"whoever swore and mentioned Al-Lat and Al-'Uzza, let him then say: There is no deity but Allah"* — **the word of tawhid deliberately uttered to erase the word of shirk uttered by habit.**

**Ila'** (2:226–227) — the oath to abstain from one's wife. Four months is the outer limit. After it he must either return or divorce; if he refuses, the authorities compel him, *"so that the wife is not harmed."* And the four months' passing does **not** itself constitute divorce — Ibn 'Umar, and twelve Companions asked separately, all said the same.

**The 'iddah of the divorced woman: three *quru'*** — the menstrual periods, per an overwhelming list from Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Uthman, 'Ali, Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn 'Abbas and dozens of the Tabi'in.

**"It is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs."** Ibn Kathir's note on the legal architecture here is important: verification of pregnancy or menses is impossible from outside, **so Allah left the determination with the woman herself** — and secured it not with a mechanism but with a warning: *"if they believe in Allah and the Last Day."* Her testimony about her own body is accepted; the check on it is her faith.

**Mutual rights (2:228).** *"They have rights similar to those over them, in kindness."* Ibn 'Abbas's personal application is the finest gloss on it: **"I like to take care of my appearance for my wife, just as I like her to take care of her appearance for me — because Allah said: *and they have rights similar to those over them.*"**

The Prophet's ﷺ farewell instruction sits here: *"Fear Allah regarding your women, for you took them by Allah's covenant and were made lawful to them by Allah's Word... **They have the right to be fed as you eat, clothed as you are clothed**, and not to be struck on the face, cursed, or abandoned except in the house."*

**Divorce is twice (2:229).** Previously a man could divorce and reclaim indefinitely — a hundred times — leaving the woman in permanent limbo. One man told his wife outright: *"I will neither divorce you nor keep you; I will divorce you and take you back just as your 'iddah ends."* She went to the Prophet ﷺ, and the ayah came. **The count of three is a protection, not a permission.**

*"Either retain in kindness or release with kindness."* Ibn 'Abbas: *"When he has divorced twice, let him fear Allah about the third. Let him keep her with kindness or let her go with kindness, **without infringing any of her rights.**"*

**Khul'** — the wife's release. She may return the dowry when she can no longer live with him. The occasion is preserved twice. Habibah bint Sahl was found standing at the Prophet's ﷺ door before Fajr in the dark; he asked who it was, and she named her husband Thabit bin Qays. When Thabit came, the Prophet ﷺ said: **"This is Habibah bint Sahl. She said what Allah permitted her to say."** In Al-Bukhari's version, the wife of Thabit said: *"O Messenger of Allah! I do not criticize his religion or his character. **But I hate to commit kufr in Islam**"* — meaning by failing his rights. He ﷺ asked: *"Will you return his garden?"* — *"Yes."* — **"Take back the garden, and divorce her once."**

Balanced against this: *"Any woman who asks her husband for divorce without justification — the scent of Paradise is forbidden to her."*

**Three divorces at once.** Mahmud bin Labid reports that when the Prophet ﷺ was told of a man who pronounced three at one time, **he stood up angry** and said: *"Is the Book of Allah being made a jest while I am still among you?"* — and a man rose and offered to kill the offender.

**After the third divorce**, she is not lawful to him until she marries another *"husband"* — genuinely. Hence the case of Rifa'ah's wife, and the ruling: **"No, not until he tastes her 'usaylah and she tastes his."** And hence the curse on **Tahlil** — the arranged sham marriage: *"Allah's Messenger ﷺ cursed the one who performs Tahlil and the one for whom it is done"* — the same hadith that curses the consumer and payer of riba. Ibn 'Umar, asked about a brother who secretly married a woman to make her lawful again: *"No — unless it is a marriage of desire. **We used to consider this adultery in the time of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.**"* And 'Umar: *"If the participants of Tahlil are brought to me, I will have them stoned."*

**Do not take her back to harm her (2:231).** A man would divorce, wait until the 'iddah was nearly done, take her back only to divorce again — prolonging her captivity indefinitely and preventing her remarriage. *"And do not take Allah's verses as a jest."* The Prophet ﷺ, angry with a man of the Ash'ariyyin: *"One of you says 'I divorced her,' then 'I took her back!' **This is not how Muslims conduct divorce.** Divorce the woman when she has fulfilled the prescribed period."*

**And do not prevent her remarrying her ex-husband (2:232).** The occasion is Ma'qil bin Yasar, whose sister was divorced and whose husband then wanted her back. She wanted it too. Ma'qil refused: **"O ungrateful one! I honoured you and married her to you, and you divorced her. By Allah, she will never return to you."** *"But Allah knew his need for his wife and her need for her husband"* — and the ayah came down. Ma'qil heard it and said: **"I hear and obey my Lord,"** summoned the man, and remarried them.

The ayah also yields the ruling that **a woman requires a Wali for marriage**: *"The woman does not give another woman in marriage, nor does she give herself in marriage"* and *"no marriage is valid except with a mature Wali and two trustworthy witnesses."*

**Suckling (2:233).** Two full years for those who wish to complete it. Suckling establishes the prohibitions of kinship **only within those two years**. The father bears the mother's food and clothing *"in what is reasonable"* — within his means (65:7). Then the ayah's central legal phrase:

> **"No mother shall be harmed on account of her child, nor a father on account of his child."**

Ibn Kathir applies it in both directions: **the mother may not refuse to nurse in order to hurt the father**, and **the father may not take the child in order to hurt the mother.** Weaning early requires **mutual consultation and consent** — neither parent may decide alone. His comment: *"The method of mutual consultation protects the child's interests. It is a mercy from Allah."*

**The widow's 'iddah: four months and ten (2:234)** — regardless of whether the marriage was consummated, and ending at delivery if she is pregnant (65:4, and the case of Subay'ah Al-Aslamiyyah, who gave birth days after her husband's death and was told her 'iddah was over).

The wisdom, per Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib and Abu Al-'Aliyah: the period suffices for a pregnancy to become evident — mapped onto the hadith of the forty-day stages of formation.

And the mourning is required — but note what it replaced. Zaynab bint Umm Salamah described Jahiliyyah: **the widow went into seclusion for a full year** in her worst clothes, no perfume, no adornment; then emerged, was given dung to throw, and an animal was brought and bled until it usually died. Against that, the Prophet ﷺ said, correcting a mother who asked to treat her widowed daughter's sore eye with kohl: **"It is four months and ten! During Jahiliyyah one of you would mourn for a whole year."**

**Indirect proposals during 'iddah are permitted (2:235)** — *"I wish to marry," "I desire a wife," "I wish Allah would grant me a good wife"* — but no secret promise, and no contract until the term ends. *"And know that Allah knows what is in your minds, so beware of Him"* — and Ibn Kathir reads the closing *"and know that Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Forbearing"* as deliberately placed so that men would not despair over the thoughts they cannot help.

**Divorce before consummation (2:236–237).** If no dowry was fixed: a **Mut'ah**, a parting gift, *"the rich according to his means and the poor according to his means"* — and Ibn Kathir notes the Prophet ﷺ himself did this, providing for Umaymah bint Sharahil along with a gift of two garments. If a dowry *was* fixed: **half of it**, unless one side remits.

And then the line Ibn Kathir clearly loves: *"And to remit is nearer to taqwa. **And do not forget liberality between yourselves.**"* Whichever party gives up their right is the one nearer to piety.

### The prayers, and the widow's year (2:238–242)

**"Guard strictly the prayers, especially the Middle Prayer, and stand before Allah devoutly."** Ibn Kathir marshals an overwhelming weight of evidence that the **Middle Prayer is 'Asr** — the position of the majority of the Companions, the Tabi'in, and the scholars of hadith — anchored in the Prophet's ﷺ words at the Battle of the Confederates when he was prevented from praying it: *"They have busied us from the Middle Prayer, the 'Asr prayer — may Allah fill their hearts and houses with fire."* And the warning: *"Whoever misses the 'Asr prayer is like one who has lost his family and his wealth."*

*"And stand before Allah Qanitin"* — with humility, **and in silence**. Zayd bin Arqam: *"A man used to speak to his companion about his affairs during the prayer. Then this ayah was revealed, and we were ordered to be silent."* And: *"Ordinary speech is not appropriate in the prayer; the prayer is only tasbih, takbir and the recitation of the Qur'an."*

**The prayer of fear (2:239).** *"And if you fear, then on foot or riding."* Ibn 'Umar: *"If the fear is intense, pray on foot, riding, facing the Qiblah or not."* Ibn 'Abbas: *"Allah ordained by the tongue of your Prophet ﷺ four rak'ah at residence, two while traveling, and **one during fear**."* Al-Awza'i: if they cannot even nod, they delay; Anas describes the assault on Tustar, when fighting raged through the dawn and they prayed only after full daylight — *"and I would not have been pleased to gain this world and all it contains instead of that prayer."*

And when safety returns: *"remember Allah as He has taught you, what you did not know."*

**The widow's year (2:240)** was abrogated by the four months and ten of 2:234 — and Ibn Az-Zubayr asked 'Uthman why it remained in the *mushaf* at all if superseded. **'Uthman's answer is one of the most important statements on the compilation of the Qur'an:** *"O my nephew! I shall not change any part of the Qur'an from its place."* The order is a matter of revelation, not of editorial judgement.

**"And for divorced women, a provision in kindness — a duty upon the God-fearing" (2:241).** When 2:236 had said a gift was *"a duty upon the doers of good,"* a man had said: *"If I want to be excellent I will, and if I don't, I won't."* So this ayah came — binding it to **taqwa** instead. Sa'id bin Jubayr and Ibn Jarir held that on its basis a parting gift is due to **every** divorced woman.

## 12. Death Does Not Come Early or Late (2:243–245)

**"Did you not consider those who left their homes in thousands, fearing death? Allah said to them: Die. Then He restored them to life."**

They fled a plague — several thousand of them — believing they could outrun it. Allah killed them where they stopped. Long afterwards, a Prophet (identified as Hizqil) passed their scattered bones and asked for them to be revived: *"O rotted bones, Allah commands you to come together"* — then flesh, nerves and skin — then *"O souls, return each to the body you inhabited."* They rose and said: **"All praise is to You, and there is no deity but You."**

Two lessons are drawn, and they are drawn together:

1. **Physical resurrection is real** — this is another of the surah's demonstrations.
2. **No caution averts destiny.** *"They departed fleeing an epidemic, seeking long life. What they earned was the opposite."*

And immediately, the balancing hadith so the lesson is not turned into recklessness — 'Umar at Sargh, turning back from a plague-stricken Sham, and 'Abdur-Rahman bin 'Awf's report: **"If it breaks out in a land you are in, do not leave to escape it. If you hear of it in a land, do not enter it."** *Taking precaution is commanded; imagining that precaution defeats decree is the error.*

Hence the next ayah: **"And fight in the way of Allah"** — because abandoning Jihad no more delays death than fleeing delays it (3:168, 4:77–78). Ibn Kathir closes with **Khalid bin Al-Walid**, dying in bed: *"I have participated in so many battles; there is not a part of my body but bears the mark of an arrow, a spear or a sword. **Yet here I am, dying on my bed as the camel dies. May the eyes of the cowards never taste sleep.**"*

**"Who is he that will lend to Allah a goodly loan?" (2:245)** — and the hadith Qudsi: **"Who would give a loan to He Who is neither poor nor unjust?"**

## 13. Talut and Jalut (2:246–252)

The Children of Israel, defeated and dispossessed, their Tabut taken and the Tawrah mostly lost, asked their Prophet (Shamwil/Samuel) for **a king to fight under**. He asked the obvious question: *"Would you then refrain from fighting if fighting were prescribed for you?"* They protested that of course they would fight — *"why should we not, when we have been driven from our homes and our children?"*

**"But when fighting was ordered for them, they turned away, all except a few of them."**

**Talut's appointment** produced instant objection: *"How can he be king over us when we are fitter for kingship, and he has not been given abundant wealth?"* — he was a soldier, not of the royal line, and poor (a water-carrier, or a tanner, by different reports). The answer names the actual qualifications: **"Allah has chosen him over you, and increased him abundantly in knowledge and stature."** Ibn Kathir's gloss: he has more knowledge and more strength; *"the king should have sufficient knowledge, be sound in appearance, and strong in soul and body."* And the closing corrective: **"And Allah grants His kingdom to whom He wills."**

**The sign** was the return of the Tabut, carried by the angels, containing *Sakinah* (tranquility, mercy, grace) *"and a remnant of what the family of Musa and Harun left"* — Musa's staff and fragments of the Tablets, per Ibn 'Abbas and Qatadah; some added the pot of manna.

**The river.** Talut marched with some eighty thousand and tested them: *"whoever drinks from it is not of me; and whoever does not taste it is of me — except one who takes a handful."* **"Yet they drank from it, all except a few of them."** Ibn 'Abbas notes the mercy inside the test: those who took a handful were satisfied; those who drank freely were left thirsty.

Al-Bara' bin 'Azib's remark ties the story to Badr with startling precision: **"We used to say that the Companions who were with Muhammad ﷺ at Badr numbered just over three hundred and ten — exactly the number who crossed the river with Talut."**

Even after the river, the few said: *"We have no power today against Jalut and his hosts."* And the answer came from **those with certainty**, not those with numbers: **"How often has a small group overcome a mighty host by Allah's leave! And Allah is with the patient."**

The du'a at the meeting of the armies is worth memorizing whole: **"Our Lord! Pour forth on us patience, and set firm our feet, and give us victory over the disbelieving people."** Pour patience *first*; the feet cannot be firm without it.

Then Dawud — a youth in the ranks — killed Jalut, and *"Allah gave him the kingdom and wisdom, and taught him of what He willed."*

The verse Ibn Kathir treats as the historical philosophy of the whole passage: **"And if Allah did not check one set of people by means of another, the earth would be full of corruption."** Paired with 22:40: were it not for this checking, *"monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques, in which the Name of Allah is much mentioned, would surely have been pulled down."*

## 14. The Messengers, Ranked and Not Ranked (2:253)

*"Those Messengers — We preferred some of them above others."* Some Allah spoke to directly (Musa, and per a hadith Adam and Muhammad ﷺ); others He raised in degrees; and 'Isa was given clear proofs and supported by **Ruh al-Qudus** (Jibril).

But ranking has a boundary, and Ibn Kathir addresses the apparent conflict head-on. A Muslim and a Jew argued; the Jew swore *"by Him who gave Musa superiority over all mankind,"* and the Muslim slapped him. The Prophet ﷺ said: **"Do not give me superiority over the Prophets"** — describing how on the Day of Resurrection he would find Musa already holding the leg of the Throne, not knowing whether Musa was exempted from the swoon or rose before him.

**The resolution:** the ayah states a fact known to Allah; the hadith forbids *using* that fact **as ammunition in a quarrel**. *"It is not up to creation to decide which Prophet is better; this is Allah's decision, and creation is only required to submit to it."*

*"And if Allah had willed, those after them would not have fought one another after the clear proofs came to them. But they differed — some believed and some disbelieved... but Allah does what He wills."*

## 15. Ayat al-Kursi (2:255)

**"The greatest ayah in the Book of Allah."** The Prophet ﷺ asked Ubayy bin Ka'b which ayah was greatest; Ubayy deflected twice, then said: *"Ayat al-Kursi."* And the Prophet ﷺ struck him on the chest and said: **"Congratulations on your knowledge, O Abu Al-Mundhir! By He in Whose Hand is my soul, this ayah has a tongue and two lips with which it glorifies the King beside the leg of the Throne."**

**The story of the thief.** Abu Hurayrah was set to guard the Sadaqah of Ramadan. Three nights running a man came and took handfuls, and three nights running he begged off and was released — the third time offering, in exchange for freedom, something of value: *"When you go to bed, recite Ayat al-Kursi to its end. A guardian from Allah will remain with you, and no devil will come near you until morning."* The Prophet ﷺ said: **"He told you the truth — though he is a liar. Do you know whom you were talking to these three nights? That was Shaytan."*

Asma' bint Yazid narrated that Allah's **Greatest Name** is in two ayat: this one, and the opening of Al 'Imran.

Ibn Kathir then divides the ayah into **ten complete sentences**, each an independent assertion:

| # | Statement | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *"Allah — none has the right to be worshipped but He"* | His exclusive divinity |
| 2 | *"Al-Hayy, Al-Qayyum"* | The Ever-Living Who never dies; the Self-Subsisting on Whom everything depends while He needs nothing |
| 3 | *"Neither slumber nor sleep overtakes Him"* | No inattention, no lapse. Note the ascent: slumber first, then the stronger *sleep* |
| 4 | *"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth"* | All are servants within His dominion (19:93–95) |
| 5 | *"Who can intercede with Him except by His permission?"* | Intercession exists but is wholly leased, never owned |
| 6 | *"He knows what is before them and what is behind them"* | Perfect knowledge of past and future |
| 7 | *"They compass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills"* | No creature acquires knowledge of Him except what He conveys |
| 8 | *"His Kursi extends over the heavens and the earth"* | Ibn 'Abbas: *"If the seven heavens and seven earths were flattened side by side, they would be like a ring thrown in a desert compared to the Kursi"* |
| 9 | *"And He feels no fatigue in guarding them"* | Sustaining all existence costs Him nothing |
| 10 | *"And He is the Most High, the Most Great"* | — |

On sentence 3, Ibn Kathir places Abu Musa's hadith: *"Allah does not sleep, nor does it befit His majesty to sleep. He lowers the scales and raises them. The deeds of the night are raised to Him before the day, and the deeds of the day before the night. **His veil is light** — were He to remove it, the glory of His Face would burn everything His sight reaches of His creation."*

His methodological note at the end applies to the whole subject of the divine attributes: these must be treated **as the Salaf treated them — accepting their apparent meanings without equating them with created attributes and without distorting them.**

## 16. No Compulsion (2:256–257)

**"There is no compulsion in religion. Verily, the right path has become distinct from the wrong path."**

Ibn Kathir's reading is not a concession but an argument from sufficiency: *"Do not force anyone to become Muslim, for **Islam is plain and clear, and its proofs and evidence are plain and clear.** Therefore there is no need to force anyone. Whoever Allah guides, opens his heart and enlightens his mind will embrace it with certainty. **And whoever Allah has blinded — he will not benefit from being forced.**"*

**The occasion.** Ansari women who had lost children in infancy would vow that if a child lived they would raise him as a Jew. When Banu An-Nadir were evacuated from Madinah, some Ansari children were among them, and the Ansar said: *"We will not abandon our children."* The ayah came down. *"Its indication is general in meaning."*

**"Whoever disbelieves in Taghut and believes in Allah has grasped the most trustworthy handhold that will never break."** *Taghut*, per 'Umar, is Shaytan — and Ibn Kathir accepts this as the broadest reading, covering *"every evil the people of Jahiliyyah fell into: worshipping idols, referring to them for judgment, and invoking them for victory."* The handhold is faith, or Islam.

The vision of 'Abdullah bin Salam is placed here as the ayah's picture: a green garden with an iron pole reaching the sky, a handle at its tip, and a helper raising him until he grasped it — **and he woke with the handle still in his hand.** The Prophet ﷺ interpreted: *"The garden is Islam, the pole is the pillar of Islam, and the handle is the most trustworthy handhold. **You shall remain a Muslim until you die.**"*

**Light and darknesses (2:257).** Allah brings the believers *"out of darknesses into light"*; the Taghut brings the disbelievers *"out of light into darknesses."* Ibn Kathir stops on the grammar, and it is one of his best observations: **light is always singular in the Qur'an; darkness is always plural** — *"because the truth is one, while disbelief comes in many types, all of them false."*

## 17. Three Encounters with Resurrection (2:258–260)

Ibn Kathir reads these as a deliberate triad — a **denial**, a **demonstration**, and a **request.**

### Ibrahim and Nimrod (2:258)

The disputant is Nimrod, whom Mujahid counts among the four kings who ruled the whole earth — two believers (Sulayman and Dhul-Qarnayn) and two disbelievers (Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar). *"What made him commit this transgression was his tyranny and the fact that he ruled for a long time."*

Ibrahim's first proof: **"My Lord is He who gives life and causes death"** — things come into existence from nothing and perish, which cannot happen without a Creator who wills.

Nimrod's answer — *"I give life and cause death"* — is usually read as the trick of pardoning one prisoner and executing another. **Ibn Kathir rejects that reading** and takes him at his word: Nimrod did not deny a Creator existed; he **claimed to be one**, exactly as Pharaoh later did (*"I know not that you have a god other than me"*). This is why Ibrahim's second statement is not a change of subject but a tightening of the same argument:

> **"Verily, Allah brings the sun from the east; so bring it from the west."**

*"He who brings life and death controls existence and its planets and their movements. So if you are god as you claimed — bring the sun from the west."* **"So the disbeliever was utterly defeated"** — silenced, with nothing to say.

Ibn Kathir explicitly prefers this to the philosophers' claim that Ibrahim moved to a "clearer" argument: **Ibrahim refuted both of Nimrod's claims, not one.**

### 'Uzayr and the Ruined Town (2:259)

A man passed a town destroyed to its roofs — Jerusalem after Nebuchadnezzar, per Mujahid — and asked, out of sheer implausibility: **"How will Allah ever bring this to life after its death?"**

Allah caused him to die a hundred years, then raised him — **and the scholars note that his eyes were restored first, so that he could watch his own body being reassembled.** Asked how long he had stayed, he said *"a day, or part of a day"* — because he had died in the morning and was raised in the afternoon, and thought the sun was the same sun.

Then the three proofs, ascending: **his food and drink unspoiled** after a century (figs not bitter, grapes not rotted, juice not turned); **his donkey**, whose scattered bones he watched being gathered, joined, clothed in flesh and given breath until it brayed; and the town itself, rebuilt and repopulated around him.

**"When it became clear to him, he said: I know that Allah is able to do all things."** Ibn Kathir's gloss: *"I know it — and I witnessed it with my own eyes. I am therefore the most knowledgeable of my time in this matter."*

### Ibrahim and the Four Birds (2:260)

**"My Lord! Show me how You give life to the dead."** — *"Do you not believe?"* — **"Yes, but that my heart may be at rest."**

Ibn Kathir uses the Prophet's ﷺ own comment to protect Ibrahim's station and console everyone else at once: **"We are more liable to doubt than Ibrahim"** — meaning, as he explains, *"we are more entitled to seek certainty."*

Ibrahim took four birds, slaughtered them, mixed the pieces together, and placed portions on separate hills, keeping the heads in his hand. He called, and — in Ibn 'Abbas's account — the feathers and flesh and blood flew to each other, each part to its own body, and each bird came **walking rapidly** toward him to collect its own head. *"And if he offered a bird another head, it refused it."*

And Ibn 'Abbas's remarkable verdict on the whole ayah, given when Ibn 'Amr offered 39:53 (*"despair not of Allah's mercy"*) as the most hopeful verse: **"But I say it is this one"** — because Allah accepted Ibrahim's faith on the strength of the single word *"Yes."* *"This ayah refers to the doubts that attack the heart and the thoughts that Shaytan inspires."*

## 18. The Ethics of Spending (2:261–274)

A sustained treatment of charity — its multiplication, its diseases, and its recipients.

### The multiplication (2:261)

**"The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is that of a grain that grows seven ears, in each ear a hundred grains."** Ibn Kathir notes the parable's rhetorical force: *"more impressive on the heart than merely mentioning the number seven hundred,"* and it shows that Allah **grows** good deeds as He grows a plant in fertile soil. A man gave a camel with its bridle in Allah's cause, and was told: **"On the Day of Resurrection you will have seven hundred camels, with their bridles."**

### The two diseases: reminders, and showing off (2:262–264)

*"Those who spend... and do not follow it with reminders of their generosity, or with injury."* And the threat: **three whom Allah will neither speak to, look at, nor purify — "he who reminds people of what he gave, he who trails his garment in pride, and he who sells his goods with a false oath."**

> **"A kind word and forgiveness are better than a charity followed by injury."**

**The showing-off parable** (2:264): a **smooth rock with a thin layer of dust on it**, struck by a downpour and left completely bare. *"Such is the case with the deeds of those who show off — they vanish, though people thought them as plentiful as the specks of dust."*

### The believer's parable (2:265)

Against it: **"a garden on high ground; heavy rain falls on it and it yields double; and if heavy rain does not fall, then light rain suffices it."** The point is the *floor*, not the ceiling: **the believer's deeds are never barren.** Heavy or light, the garden produces. And its condition is named at the front — *"seeking Allah's pleasure and confirming themselves,"* which Ibn Kathir maps to the fasting hadith: **"whoever fasts Ramadan out of faith and expectation"** — believing the command, and awaiting the reward.

### The parable of ruin (2:266)

'Umar once asked the Companions about whom this ayah was revealed. They said, "Allah knows best." He became angry: **"Say we know, or we do not know."** Ibn 'Abbas — the youngest present — said, *"I have an opinion."* 'Umar said: **"O my nephew! Say it, and do not belittle yourself."**

Ibn 'Abbas said: *"It is set for a deed. For a wealthy man who works in Allah's pleasure, and then Allah sends Shaytan to him, and he works in disobedience until he annuls all his good works."*

The image is devastating precisely in its timing: a man with a garden of palms and vines and rivers and every fruit — **who is now old, whose children are still weak** — and a fiery whirlwind takes it. He has neither the strength to replant nor children able to help. *"So he will be deprived of his reward when he most needs it."* Hence the Prophet's ﷺ du'a placed here: **"O Allah! Make Your greatest provision for me when I am old, and at the ending of my life."**

### Spend the good, not the leftovers (2:267–269)

*"Spend of the good things you have earned... and do not aim at the bad to spend from it, when you would not take it yourselves except with closed eyes."*

The occasion: at harvest the Ansar would hang branches of dates in the Prophet's ﷺ masjid for the poor Muhajirun — **and some would slip inferior branches in among the good ones.** Ibn 'Abbas's rendering of the reproach is the sharpest line in the passage: *"If someone owed you and paid you less than you gave, you would not accept it. **So how do you approve for Me what you do not approve for yourselves?**"*

**"Shaytan threatens you with poverty and orders you to lewdness; Allah promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty."** The two whisperings are named as a pair in Ibn Mas'ud's hadith: **the touch of Shaytan is the threat of evil consequences and the denial of truth; the touch of the angel is the promise of good and the affirmation of truth.** *"Whoever finds the latter, let him know it is from Allah and thank Him. Whoever finds the former, let him seek refuge from Shaytan."* Note the exact opposition: he threatens **poverty**, Allah promises **bounty**; he orders **sin**, Allah promises **forgiveness**.

**"He grants Hikmah to whom He wills, and whoever is granted Hikmah has been granted abundant good."** Ibn 'Abbas: knowledge of the Qur'an — its abrogating and abrogated, its clear and unclear, its lawful and unlawful, its parables. And the one permitted envy: *"There is no envy except in two: a man whom Allah gave wealth and he spends it rightly, and **a man whom Allah gave Hikmah and he judges by it and teaches it.**"*

### Openly or secretly (2:270–274)

*"If you disclose your charities, it is well; but if you conceal them and give them to the poor, that is better for you."* Concealment is better because it protects from ostentation — **unless disclosure serves a visible good**, such as encouraging others to imitate it. *"He who recites the Qur'an aloud is like he who gives charity openly; he who conceals his recitation is like he who conceals his charity."*

And the hadith of the seven whom Allah shades on a day of no shade — the just ruler; the youth raised in worship; two who love each other for Allah; the man whose heart is attached to the masjid; the man who remembers Allah alone and weeps; the man who refuses a noble and beautiful woman saying *"I fear Allah"*; **and the man who gives charity so secretly that his left hand does not know what his right hand gave.**

**"Not upon you is their guidance" (2:272)** — revealed because Muslims disliked giving charity to their **polytheist relatives**. They were permitted to. And the principle drawn: charity given for Allah's sake is rewarded by Allah **regardless of who received it**, since the giver is judged by his intention.

Which is illustrated by the story of the man who gave charity three nights running and each morning heard where it had landed: *to an adulteress; to a wealthy man; to a thief.* Each morning he said, **"All praise is to Allah — to an adulteress! ... to a wealthy man! ... to a thief!"** and gave again. Then he was told in a dream: **"Your charities were accepted. The adulteress may abstain from adultery because of it; the wealthy man may take heed and spend from what Allah gave him; the thief may abstain from stealing."**

**Who deserves it (2:273):** *"the poor who are restricted in Allah's cause, unable to travel in the land"* — the Muhajirun in Madinah who could not go out to trade. *"The ignorant one thinks them rich because of their restraint. You will know them by their mark. **They do not beg of people persistently.**"* And the Prophet's ﷺ definition, again: *"The Miskin is not the one who is turned away with a date or two. The Miskin is he who has nothing sufficient, whose situation is unknown so no one gives him, and who does not ask."*

Abu Sa'id's story closes it. His mother sent him to ask the Prophet ﷺ for help. He sat down without speaking, and the Prophet ﷺ turned to him and said: **"Whoever is content, Allah enriches him. Whoever is modest, Allah makes him decent. Whoever is satisfied, Allah suffices him. And whoever asks people while having a small amount has begged."** Abu Sa'id said: *"I thought to myself — I have a camel, Al-Yaqutah, and it is worth more than a small amount."* **And he went home without asking.**

## 19. Riba (2:275–281)

**The image.** *"Those who consume Riba will not stand except as one stands who is beaten by Shaytan into insanity."* Ibn 'Abbas: on the Day of Resurrection they will rise from their graves like the possessed. And Samurah's long dream-hadith: a man swimming in a river of blood, and a man on the bank with a pile of stones who throws a stone into his mouth each time he surfaces — **"the man in the river was the consumer of Riba."**

**The argument they made.** *"That is because they said: trading is only like Riba."* Ibn Kathir notes the exact inversion in their phrasing: they did not say *"riba is trade"* — they said **"trade is only like riba,"** i.e. they took riba as the standard and asked why trade was treated differently. It was not a comparison but a challenge to the ruling. And the answer: **"Allah has permitted trade and forbidden Riba."**

**The amnesty.** *"Whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists — his past is his, and his case rests with Allah."* At the conquest of Makkah the Prophet ﷺ declared: **"All the Riba of Jahiliyyah is annulled and under my feet — and the first Riba I annul is the Riba of Al-'Abbas."** No interest already collected was recovered; what was cancelled was what remained outstanding.

**The threat.** *"If you do not — then take notice of a war from Allah and His Messenger."* Ibn 'Abbas: on the Day of Resurrection those who consume riba will be told, *"Take up arms for war."* And the ruling reported: if a man persists after being required to repent, *"the Muslim leader should cut off his head."* This came down over the outstanding riba between Thaqif and Banu Al-Mughirah after both accepted Islam — and Thaqif said: **"We repent to Allah, and abandon what remains of our riba."** All of them abandoned it.

**The scope.** *"Riba is seventy types, the least of which is like a man having intercourse with his mother."* And the curse: **on the one who consumes it, the one who pays it, the two witnesses, and the scribe who records it.** Ibn Kathir's comment on the paperwork is pointed: *"They have witnesses and a scribe only so it appears to be a legitimate agreement — but it is still invalid, because the ruling applies to the agreement itself, not the form it appears in. **Verily, deeds are judged by intentions.**"*

**Doubtful cases.** This is where Ibn Kathir is most careful. **'Umar himself said:** *"I wished the Messenger of Allah ﷺ had made three matters clearer for us: the grandfather's inheritance, the Kalalah, and **some types of Riba.**"* And elsewhere: *"The ayah of Riba was among the last revealed, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ died before he explained it to us. **So leave what makes you doubt for what does not make you doubt.**"*

The governing principle Ibn Kathir names: **whatever leads to the unlawful is itself unlawful** — hence the prohibition of *Muzabanah*, *Muhaqalah* and *Mukhabarah*, transactions whose eventual equivalence cannot be known at contract time. And the hadith of An-Nu'man bin Bashir: *"The lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between them are doubtful matters. **Whoever avoids the doubtful protects his religion and his honour; whoever falls into them is like a shepherd grazing at the edge of a private pasture — at any moment he may enter it.**"*

**The counter-image (2:276).** *"Allah destroys Riba and gives increase to charity."* Ibn Mas'ud: *"Riba ends up with less, even if it was substantial."* And the growth of charity is given a physical image: *"Whoever gives in charity what equals a date, from honest earnings — and Allah accepts only what is good — Allah accepts it in His right Hand and **raises it for its giver as one of you raises his foal, until it becomes as big as a mountain.**"*

**Debtors in hardship (2:280).** *"And if the debtor is in difficulty, then grant him time until ease; and that you remit it as charity is better for you, if you only knew."* In Jahiliyyah, the arrival of the term meant: *"pay now, or the debt increases."* Against that:

- *"Whoever gives time to a debtor in hardship earns the charity of the debt's equal for each day."* Then Buraydah heard him ﷺ say **double** for each day, and asked about the difference. He ﷺ explained: **equal for each day before the term falls due; double for each day after.**
- Abu Qatadah, chasing a debtor who was hiding, found him and heard him say he had nothing. Abu Qatadah wept and said: *"I heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say — **whoever gives his debtor time, or forgives the debt, will be in the shade of the Throne on the Day of Resurrection.**"*
- And the man summoned before Allah who could name no deed worth an atom, until the third asking, when he said: *"O Lord, You gave me wealth and I was a merchant. **I used to be lenient — giving easy terms to the well-off, and time to those in hardship.**"* And Allah said: **"I am more worthy of giving easy terms than you. Enter Paradise."**

**2:281 — the last ayah revealed.** *"And fear a Day when you will be returned to Allah. Then every soul will be paid in full what it earned, and they will not be wronged."* An-Nasa'i and Ad-Dahhak from Ibn 'Abbas: **the final ayah of the Qur'an.** Placed, deliberately, at the end of the passage on money.

## 20. The Debt Verse (2:282–283)

**The longest ayah in the Qur'an** — and Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib was told it was the last revealed from above the Throne.

**"O you who believe! When you contract a debt for a fixed term, write it down."** Its stated purpose is given inside the ayah itself: **"that is more just with Allah, more supportive for testimony, and more likely to prevent doubt among yourselves."** Ibn Kathir's note on the middle clause is practical: a written record helps *the witnesses themselves*, who may forget, and who will recognize their own hand later.

The requirements, in order:

- **A scribe who writes in justice** — adding nothing, deleting nothing, favouring neither party.
- **The scribe may not refuse.** *"Let not the scribe refuse to write, as Allah has taught him."* Let it be a charity from the literate to the unlettered, *"just as Allah taught him what he did not know."* And: **"It is a charity to help a worker, and to do something for a feeble person."**
- **The debtor dictates**, not the creditor — *"and let him fear Allah his Lord, and not diminish anything of it."* If the debtor is of poor understanding, weak, or unable, **his guardian dictates in justice.**
- **Two witnesses; or one man and two women.**
- **Witnesses may not refuse when called.** The majority read this as referring to *giving testimony to what one actually witnessed* — obligatory when called, unless others have already discharged it.
- **Write it whether the amount is small or large** — *"do not become weary of writing it."*
- **Exception:** an on-the-spot transaction need not be recorded.
- **"Let neither scribe nor witness be harmed"** — and Al-Hasan and Qatadah read it both ways: neither may they be harmed, nor may they cause harm by writing other than what was dictated or testifying to other than what was heard.

**Travel and pledges (2:283).** If no scribe can be found, **a pledge (rahn)** is taken. Ibn 'Abbas adds: even if a scribe is found but there is no paper, ink or pen. And the fact that anchors it: **the Prophet ﷺ died with his shield mortgaged to a Jew of Madinah for thirty measures of barley**, bought on credit as provision for his family.

*"But if one of you entrusts the other, let the trusted one discharge his trust, and let him fear Allah his Lord."* Abu Sa'id and Ash-Sha'bi held this abrogates the obligation to write where the parties genuinely trust each other. And the debtor's warning: **"The hand that took is responsible for what it took, until it returns it."**

*"And do not conceal the testimony. Whoever conceals it — his heart is sinful."* Ibn 'Abbas: *"False testimony is among the worst of the major sins — and so is concealing the true one."*

## 21. The Closing Two Ayat (2:284–286)

**The alarm.** *"To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. Whether you disclose what is in yourselves or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it."*

This ayah frightened the Companions more than any other. They came to the Prophet ﷺ and **fell to their knees**, saying: *"O Messenger of Allah! We were charged with deeds we can bear — prayer, fasting, jihad, charity. But this ayah has been revealed to you, and we cannot bear it."*

His answer was not reassurance but **instruction in how to receive a command**:

> **"Do you want to say what the People of the Two Scriptures before you said — 'We hear and we disobey'? Rather say: 'We hear and we obey, and we seek Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the return.'"**

They said it. Their tongues recited it. **And only then** did Allah send down the answer they had been unable to ask for — **2:285–286**. Ibn Kathir preserves both the order and its lesson: *"When they did that, Allah abrogated it."*

Ibn 'Abbas's account adds the detail: they had said, *"We know we are accountable for our speech and our actions, but as for what occurs in our hearts, **we do not control what is in them.**"* And the concession came: **"Allah has pardoned my Ummah for what they say to themselves, as long as they do not utter it or act on it."** With the accounting weighted permanently in their favour: an intended evil not done is not written; done, it is written once. An intended good not done **is written as one good deed**; done, it is written as ten.

### 2:285 — The Creed

**"The Messenger believes in what was sent down to him from his Lord, and so do the believers. Each believes in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His Messengers — 'We make no distinction between any of His Messengers' — and they say: We hear and we obey. Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the return."**

Ibn Kathir: each believer holds Allah alone as Lord; believes in every Prophet and every revealed Book; and **does not divide the Messengers into some accepted and some rejected** — *"all of them truthful, righteous, guided, even when some brought what abrogated the law of others by Allah's leave."*

### 2:286 — The Answered Du'a

**"Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity."** This is the ayah that lifted the weight. *"He will only hold accountable for what one is able to guard himself from. As for what one cannot — such as what passes through the mind — there is no punishment for it."* And Ibn Kathir adds the fine point: **to dislike the evil thought that crosses the mind is itself a part of faith.**

*"It has what it earned, and against it what it earned."* Reward for the good; liability only for the evil actually acquired.

Then the five petitions — and Ibn Kathir records, after every single one, that Allah answered:

| The petition | The answer |
|---|---|
| *"Our Lord! Take us not to task if we forget or err."* | **"I have done so."** |
| *"Our Lord! Lay not on us a burden like that You laid on those before us."* | **"I have done so."** |
| *"Our Lord! Burden us not with what we have no strength to bear."* | **"I have done so."** |
| *"Pardon us, forgive us, have mercy on us."* | **"I have done so."** |
| *"You are our Protector; give us victory over the disbelieving people."* | **"I have done so."** |

Ibn Kathir separates the three requests of the fourth petition, because they are three different needs: **pardon** (*'afu*) for what is between the servant and Allah; **forgiveness** (*maghfirah*) for what is between him and other people, that his faults not be exposed to them; and **mercy** (*rahmah*) for what is still to come, that he not be left to fall again. *"They say that those who err need three things"* — and all three were granted.

**The virtues of these two ayat:**

- *"Whoever recites the last two ayat of Surah Al-Baqarah at night, **they will suffice him.**"*
- On the night of the Isra', at the Sidrat al-Muntaha, the Prophet ﷺ was given **three things**: the five daily prayers; the closing ayat of Al-Baqarah; and forgiveness for whoever of his Ummah dies without associating anything with Allah.
- And the hadith already met at the opening of Al-Fatihah — the door in heaven never before opened, and the angel descending with news of **two lights given to no Prophet before**: *the Opening of the Book, and the closing verses of Al-Baqarah.* **"You will not read a letter of them but you will be granted its benefit."**

Abu Ishaq reports that whenever **Mu'adh** finished this surah at *"and give us victory over the disbelieving people,"* he would say: **"Amin."**

## 22. In One Breath — The Whole Surah

Al-Baqarah opens by sorting humanity into believer, disbeliever and hypocrite, and closes with a prayer that the believers be spared the burdens the earlier nations carried. Everything between is the working out of that arc.

**Part I** builds the case: a nation given every conceivable favour — rescue, revelation, food from the sky, water from stone — whose hearts hardened past the responsiveness of rock, until they concealed the Prophet they had been praying for out of envy that he came from the other branch of the family. Against them stands Ibrahim: tested with everything, obedient in all of it, made an Imam — and told plainly that the covenant does not run through wrongdoers, however impeccable the lineage. The change of the Qiblah is the hinge: small as a ruling, enormous as a test — *who follows the Messenger, and who turns on his heels?*

**Part II** answers the question the first half raised: *if righteousness is obedience rather than direction, what does an obedient life actually look like?* And the answer is unglamorous and total. It looks like breaking your fast promptly and eating Suhur. Like not bribing a judge. Like entering the house by the door. Like fighting only those who fight you, and stopping when they stop. Like leaving the Sacred Month alone unless attacked in it. Like standing at 'Arafat with everybody else instead of above them. Like giving your wife what you would want given to you. Like not taking a woman back in order to trap her. Like consulting the mother before weaning the child. Like writing the loan down. Like giving the debtor time, and better, forgiving him. Like giving the good dates and not the bad ones. Like praying 'Asr.

The theological centre is **Ayat al-Kursi**, and the surah arranges itself around it: the God who neither slumbers nor sleeps, whose Kursi contains the heavens, who cannot be interceded with except by leave, is the same God who says *"I am near"* and answers the man who asks. And the surah's insistence on **resurrection** — the revival after the thunderbolt, the cow, the thousands who fled the plague, 'Uzayr's donkey, Ibrahim's four birds — is not decoration. It is the ground of every ruling in it: **a law about debts and dowries and dates only makes sense if the accounting is real.**

And the last word is not law but dependence. After the longest legal passage in the Qur'an, the surah ends with the believers on their knees admitting they cannot carry what they were shown, and being told to say **"we hear and we obey"** — and then being given the relief they could not ask for. Five petitions, five answers, all of them *"I have done so."*

*This completes Surah Al-Baqarah. All praise is due to Allah.*
