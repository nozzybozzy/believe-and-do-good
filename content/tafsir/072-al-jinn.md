*Revealed in Makkah · 28 ayat · "The Jinn" — named for the unseen creatures whose own testimony fills the first half of the surah*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-Jinn is unusual in the Qur'an: for fifteen verses, **the speaker is not Allah, not the Prophet ﷺ, and not the Quraysh — it is a delegation of jinn**, quoted at length, reporting their own conversion. Allah commands His Messenger to tell his people what happened: a group of them listened to the Qur'an, believed it, affirmed it, and held to it.

What makes this so effective as an argument to Makkah is *who* is testifying. The Arabs had spent generations seeking protection from these beings, fearing them, treating them as powers of the wilderness. Now those same beings are heard confessing that they cannot escape Allah on earth or by flight, that they have no idea what their Lord intends, that among them are the guided and the deviant just as among humans — and that they have just abandoned the doctrine the Quraysh are still defending.

The second half turns from the jinn's speech to a series of commands beginning **Say** — and each one *reduces* the Prophet ﷺ rather than elevating him. He cannot harm you. He cannot guide you. He cannot protect himself from Allah. He does not know when the Hour is. All he has is the conveying of the message. A surah that begins with unseen creatures crowding to hear him ends with him insisting that he is only a servant.

---

## 1. The Jinn Hear the Qur'an and Believe (72:1–3)

> *Say: "It has been revealed to me that a group of Jinn listened. They said: 'Verily, we have heard a wonderful Recitation! It guides to the right path'"*

Ibn Kathir reads "the right path" as **what is correct, and what leads to success**. He connects it directly to the parallel account:

> *And when We sent towards you a group of the Jinn listening to the Qur'an.* (46:29)

Their first doctrinal statement is a rejection of Shirk, and their second is about Allah's **Jadd** — a word with a spread of reported meanings:

| Authority | Meaning of *Jadd* |
|---|---|
| Ibn Abbas (via Ali bin Abi Talhah) | His actions, His commands, His power |
| Ibn Abbas (via Ad-Dahhak) | His blessings, His power, His favour upon creation |
| Mujahid, Ikrimah | The magnificence of our Lord |
| Qatadah | His magnificence, His greatness, His command |
| As-Suddi | The command of our Lord |
| Abu Ad-Darda', Mujahid, Ibn Jurayj | His remembrance (*Dhikr*) |

And the conclusion they draw from it: **He has taken neither a wife nor a son.** Ibn Kathir's note is that this is the *first thing* they professed once they accepted Islam — that Allah is far exalted above a mate and offspring.

---

## 2. "The Foolish Among Us" (72:4–5)

> *And that the foolish among us used to utter against Allah that which was an enormity in falsehood.*

Mujahid, Ikrimah, Qatadah and As-Suddi all identify **the foolish one (*As-Safih*) as Iblis**. *Shatat* — the enormity — is explained by Abu Malik (via As-Suddi) as **transgression**, and by Ibn Zayd as **a great injustice**.

But Ibn Kathir widens it: *Safih* covers **everyone in that category** — anyone at all who claims Allah has a spouse or a son. And the phrase "used to utter" means *before his acceptance of Islam*.

Then comes the admission that explains how they were misled for so long (72:5): *we did not think that humans and Jinn would join each other in lying about Allah.* They had assumed a falsehood that universal could not be a falsehood. Hearing the Qur'an is what broke the assumption.

---

## 3. Why Men Should Not Seek Refuge with Jinn (72:6)

> *And verily, there were men among mankind who took shelter with the males among the Jinn, but they increased them in Rahaq.*

This is the practical heart of the surah, and Ibn Kathir treats it as a piece of social history. **It was the custom of the Arabs in the days of ignorance**: a traveller settling for the night in a valley or open country would seek refuge with the greatest jinn of that place, the way a man entering enemy territory would put himself under the protection of a powerful local.

As-Suddi preserves the formula almost verbatim — *I seek refuge with the master of this valley from the Jinn, that no harm come to myself, my wealth, my child or my animals.*

What the jinn are confessing is what this did to **them**:

- **Qatadah:** the jinn were emboldened and increased in insolence; when men sought refuge with them instead of Allah, the jinn overcame them with harm because of it.
- **Ikrimah** (via Ibn Abi Hatim) — the most striking version: *the jinn used to fear humans just as humans fear them, or worse.* When people entered a valley, the jinn fled. Then they heard the human leader announce that he sought refuge with the master of the valley, and said among themselves: *these people are fleeing from us just as we flee from them.* **From that discovery, the jinn began drawing near to humans and afflicting them with insanity and madness.**

*Rahaq* itself is glossed as **fear, terror and fright** (Abu Aliyah, Ar-Rabi', Zayd bin Aslam), as **sin**, and as **transgression** (Mujahid).

The lesson is exact: fear directed at a creature does not buy safety from it. It teaches the creature what it can do to you.

## 4. The Sky Under Guard (72:7–10)

> *And we have sought to reach the heaven; but we found it filled with stern guards and flaming fires.*

The jinn had assumed no Messenger would ever be sent again — Al-Kalbi and Ibn Jarir: *not after so long a period*. Then the sky changed.

Ibn Kathir explains the mechanism as **protection of the Qur'an itself**: the devils were expelled from the stations where they had been eavesdropping so that they could not steal fragments of revelation and feed them to soothsayers, mixing truth with lies until no one could tell who was truthful. This was Allah's kindness to His creation and His guarding of His Book.

Shooting stars had occurred before — but rarely. **Ibn Abbas**: they were sitting with the Prophet ﷺ when one flashed, and he asked what they used to say about it. They answered that a great person had been born, or a great person had died. He told them: *it is not so — rather, when Allah decrees a matter in the heaven...*

Then the manners of the jinn, which Ibn Kathir singles out for admiration (72:10): *we know not whether evil is intended for those on the earth, or whether their Lord intends for them guidance.* They phrased it that way deliberately — **attributing evil to no one, and the good to Allah**. It matches the Sahih supplication: *and evil is not attributed to You.*

### The night the world seemed to end

Ibn Kathir gathers the narrative behind all this:

- **As-Suddi:** the sky was never guarded except when a Prophet was on earth, or when Allah's religion was becoming victorious and dominant.
- The devils, who had held their sitting-stations in the lowest heaven, were **pelted in a single night**.
- The people of **Ta'if** panicked, saying *the dwellers of the sky have been destroyed*, and began freeing their servants and abandoning their luxuries — until **Abd Yalayl bin Amr bin Umayr**, whose judgement they deferred to, told them to hold on to their wealth and simply *look at the guiding stars*: if the stars are still in place, the sky is intact and this is on account of Ibn Abi Kabshah (their derisive name for the Prophet ﷺ). The stars were still there. They kept their wealth.
- **Iblis**, alarmed, asked for a handful of dirt from every land so he could smell it. He smelled it and said: *it is your friend in Makkah.*
- He sent **seven jinn** to Makkah. They found the Prophet ﷺ standing in prayer in Al-Masjid Al-Haram, reciting. They pressed so close in their eagerness that their chests almost touched him — **and then they accepted Islam.**

## 5. Among Us Are the Righteous and Others (72:11–15)

> *There are among us some that are righteous, and some the contrary; we are groups having different ways.*

Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and others: **among us are believers and among us are disbelievers** — numerous differing paths, thoughts and opinions. (Ibn Kathir also records here, from Al-A'mash, a curious anecdote about a conversing jinn who confirmed that his kind, too, have their sectarian factions; Ibn Kathir notes that Al-Mizzi judged the chain authentic back to Al-A'mash.)

Three confessions follow, each worth holding on to:

**They cannot escape Him (72:12).** *We know that the power of Allah is decisive over us; even if we try to flee, He has complete control, and none of us can escape Him.*

**They believed as soon as they heard (72:13).** Ibn Kathir observes that **they were proud of this — and it is something to be proud of**, a great honour and a good characteristic. And the promise attached to it: whoever believes fears neither *a decrease in the reward of his good deeds* nor *an increase in the burden of his sins* (Ibn Abbas, Qatadah), matching:

> *Then he will have no fear of injustice, nor of any curtailment.* (20:112)

**They divide into Muslims and Qasitun (72:14–15).** The *Qasit* is the one who **behaves unjustly with the truth and deviates from it** — the exact opposite of the *Muqsit*, the one who is just. Note the asymmetry of outcome: whoever embraces Islam has *sought the right path*, meaning sought salvation for himself; the Qasitun become **firewood — fuel used to kindle the Fire**.

---

## 6. Abundant Water: Reward or Rope? (72:16–17)

> *If they had believed in Allah, and went on the way, We would surely have bestowed on them water in abundance — that We might try them thereby.*

Ibn Kathir lays out **two readings**, and the difference between them is the difference between two ways of understanding prosperity:

**First view — "the way" is the path of Islam.** If the deviant stood firmly and justly upon it, they would be given abundant rain and abundant provision. The trial is then, as Zayd bin Aslam put it, *to test who will remain upon guidance and who will turn back to sin.* Held by Ibn Abbas (via Al-Awfi), Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, Ata, As-Suddi, Muhammad bin Ka'b Al-Qurazi, Qatadah and Ad-Dahhak. Muqatil adds that the verse came down about the Quraysh when they were deprived of rain for seven years.

**Second view — "the way" is the path of misguidance.** Then We would have increased their provision **as a gradual respite** — *istidraj*. Held by Abu Mijlaz, Ibn Humayd, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Zayd bin Aslam, Al-Kalbi and Ibn Kaysan; Al-Baghawi appears to take it too, and Ibn Kathir notes it is supported by the closing clause *that We might try them thereby*. Its proof-texts:

> *So, when they forgot that with which they had been reminded, We opened for them the gates of everything, until in the midst of their enjoyment... We took them, and lo! They were plunged into destruction.* (6:44)

> *Do they think that in wealth and children with which We expand them, We hasten unto them with good things? Nay, but they perceive not.* (23:55–56)

And for the one who turns from the Reminder — a **Sa'ad torment**: harsh, severe, agonising, **with no relaxation in it** (Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Ikrimah, Qatadah, Ibn Zayd). Ibn Abbas also reports it as *a mountain in Hell*; Sa'id bin Jubayr, *a well in Hell*.

## 7. The Masjids Belong to Allah (72:18)

> *And the Masjids are for Allah, so invoke not anyone along with Allah.*

**Qatadah:** the Jews and Christians used to associate partners with Allah upon entering their churches and synagogues — so Allah commanded His Prophet ﷺ to tell them to single Him out alone.

**Sa'id bin Jubayr** (via Ibn Jarir) preserves a beautiful occasion: the jinn asked the Prophet ﷺ, *how can we come to the Masjid when we are so far from you, and how can we be present for the prayer at such a distance?* And this verse was revealed — the *place* is not the point; the exclusivity of the worship is.

---

## 8. "They Almost Crowded Over Him" (72:19)

> *And when the servant of Allah stood up invoking Him in prayer they just made round him a dense crowd as if sticking one over the other.*

Three interpretations, and Ibn Kathir does not leave them level:

1. **The jinn crowding to hear.** Ibn Abbas (via Al-Awfi): when they heard him reciting they almost mounted on top of him out of zeal, drawing very near — and he was unaware of them until Jibril came and revealed 72:1 to him. Also from Az-Zubayr bin Al-Awwam.
2. **The jinn describing the scene to their own people.** Ibn Abbas (via Ibn Jarir), and Sa'id bin Jubayr.
3. **Humans and jinn crowding together *against* him.** Qatadah: they combined to extinguish this matter — *however, Allah insisted upon helping it, supporting it, and making it victorious over those who opposed it.* Al-Hasan describes the Arabs nearly crowding over him when he stood and called people to their Lord. Also from Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr and Ibn Zayd; preferred by Ibn Jarir.

**Ibn Kathir takes the third as the most apparent meaning** — because of what immediately follows it. When they harmed him, denied him, and united to thwart the truth, his answer was not a counter-threat but a statement of worship: *I invoke only my Lord, and I associate none as partners along with Him* (72:20).

---

## 9. What the Messenger Cannot Do (72:21–24)

Four disclaimers in a row, and they are the spine of the surah's second half:

- **No power to harm or guide (72:21).** *I am only a man like you, and I have received revelation — a servant among the servants of Allah. I have no control over your guidance or your misguidance; all of that is referred to Allah.*
- **No protection from Allah for himself (72:22).** *If I disobey Allah, no one would be able to rescue me from His punishment* — and there is no place to escape to (Mujahid, Qatadah, As-Suddi).
- **Only conveyance (72:23).** Ibn Kathir reads this as the *exception* to the previous verse: nothing can save me from Him **except my conveying the message He has obligated me to carry.** Compare: *O Messenger! Proclaim what has been sent down to you from your Lord. And if you do not, then you have not conveyed His Message* (5:67).
- **And the reckoning of numbers is deferred (72:24).** When the idolaters — of jinn and men — finally see what was promised, they will learn *who is weaker in helpers and fewer in number*. The point, says Ibn Kathir, is that they have **no helper at all**, and are fewer than the soldiers of Allah. Which reverses exactly what a persecuted minority in Makkah appeared to be.

## 10. The Hour, and the Limits of the Unseen (72:25–28)

> *Say: "I know not whether that which you are promised is near or whether my Lord will appoint for it a distant term."*

Ibn Kathir uses this verse to demolish a rumour: the widely circulated claim that the Prophet ﷺ will not remain under the earth more than a thousand years (i.e. that the Hour must come before that) is **a baseless lie — not found in any of the books of Hadith**.

What the Prophet ﷺ actually said when asked:

- To **Jibril**, appearing as a Bedouin: *The one questioned about it knows no more than the questioner.*
- To a Bedouin shouting *when will the Hour be?*: **"Woe to you. Verily, it will occur — so what have you prepared for it?"** The man said he had not prepared much in prayer or fasting, *but I love Allah and His Messenger.* The reply: **"Then you will be with whomever you love."** Anas said the Muslims were never happier with anything than with this hadith.

**The unseen and its exception (72:26–27).** He reveals His Unseen to none — *except to a Messenger whom He has chosen*, which Ibn Kathir says includes **both angelic and human messengers**. Compare *they will never compass anything of His knowledge except that which He wills* (2:255). No creature attains any of it but what Allah allows.

And the chosen Messenger is escorted: **a band of watching guards marching before him and behind him** — additional guardian angels who protect the revelation travelling with him. Sa'id bin Jubayr specifies **four guardians among the angels, along with Jibril**.

**"Till he knows" (72:28).** Ibn Kathir lays out who the pronoun refers to:

- **The Prophet ﷺ** — so that he would know the Messengers before him had conveyed their Lord's messages and that the angels had protected and defended them (Sa'id bin Jubayr, Ad-Dahhak, As-Suddi, Yazid bin Abi Habib, Qatadah; **Ibn Jarir preferred this**).
- **The people** — on Ya'qub's recitation, *so that it may be known* (Al-Baghawi).
- **Allah** — mentioned by Ibn Al-Jawzi in *Zad Al-Masir*: He guards His Messengers through His angels so that they can convey, and guards the revelation, *so that He will know that they have indeed conveyed* — in the sense of 2:143 (*only that We know who followed the Messenger*) and 29:11 (*Allah knows those who believe, and knows the hypocrites*): knowledge made manifest in reality, not knowledge acquired.

Ibn Kathir closes the surah by removing any ambiguity: **from Allah's knowledge is that He knows all things before they occur — definite and certain.** Which is why the final words are *And He surrounds all that which is with them, and He keeps count of all things.*

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**A group of creatures the Arabs had spent centuries fearing sat down, listened once, and submitted** — and their testimony became the argument against the people who would not.

Three things they said are worth carrying:

1. **"We cannot escape Allah in the earth, nor can we escape Him by flight."** The unseen powers people appeal to are themselves cornered.
2. **"Whosoever believes in his Lord shall have no fear."** No reward decreased, no burden added beyond what is his own.
3. **"When we heard the Guidance, we believed therein."** They needed nothing but the hearing — and Ibn Kathir says they were right to be proud of it.

And the counterpart, from the human side: **the men who sought refuge with the jinn got *Rahaq* for it** — more fear, not less. Refuge sought anywhere but with Allah increases the very thing it was meant to remove. Which is precisely why the surah's practical command is the plainest one in it: **the Masjids are for Allah — so invoke no one along with Allah.**
