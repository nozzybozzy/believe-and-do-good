*Revealed in Makkah · 11 ayat · "The Chargers" — the warhorses sworn by in the opening verse*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-`Adiyat is built out of a single, deliberate contrast, and the whole surah turns on it.

It opens with **five verses of pure motion** — horses. Not described, but *heard*: the panting in their chests, hooves striking sparks off rock, the dawn raid, the dust thrown up, the plunge into the middle of the enemy. Five verses in which nothing is stated and everything is sensed. These are creatures giving the whole of themselves, in Allah's path, without reservation.

Then the oath lands on its subject, and the subject is **man**: *Verily, man is ungrateful to his Lord.* The horse spends itself for its master; the man, given far more, withholds. Ibn Kathir doesn't spell the contrast out in so many words — the surah's architecture does it for him.

From there the surah names the ingratitude precisely. Man **knows** he is like this (v.7). He is **violent in his love of wealth** (v.8) — the one thing he does spend himself on. And then the ground opens: the graves are emptied out, and what was hidden inside the chest is turned out alongside the body.

The surah ends on a word that closes the circle — **Khabir**, Well-Acquainted. The man who was a witness against himself is met by the One who was already acquainted with all of it.

## 1. The Oath: Five Verses of Cavalry (100:1–5)

> *By the `Adiyat (steeds), snorting. Striking sparks of fire. And scouring to the raid at dawn. And raise the dust in clouds the while. And penetrating forthwith as one into the midst.*

Ibn Kathir reads these as **horses of war, galloping in the path of Allah** — Jihad — and takes each image at its physical level:

| Verse | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Dabh** (snorting) | The panting sound heard from a horse when it runs hard |
| **Striking sparks** | Hooves striking rock, throwing off fire |
| **Raid at dawn** | The attack made in the early morning |
| **Raising dust** | The dust of the battlefield, stirred up by the charge |
| **Into the midst** | All of them together, in the middle of that spot |

Two clarifications from the early authorities:

- **Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Qatadah** on the dawn raid: this means *the invasion of the horses in the morning in the way of Allah*.
- **Al-Awfi from Ibn Abbas, with Ata, Ikrimah, Qatadah and Ad-Dahhak** on "into the midst": into the **midst of the disbelieving enemy**.

### The Prophet's ﷺ dawn practice

Ibn Kathir attaches a detail here that is worth pausing on. The dawn timing was the Prophet's ﷺ own habit — and he used the dawn for something other than tactics. **He would wait and listen for the Adhan.** If he heard the call to prayer coming from the people, he left them alone. If he heard nothing, he attacked.

The raid, in other words, was never against a place; it was against the absence of prayer in it.

## 2. The Verdict: *Kanud* (100:6)

> *Verily, man is ungrateful (Kanud) to his Lord.*

**This is the thing being sworn about** — the point all five oath-verses were driving toward. Man is ungrateful for his Lord's favours and rejects them.

On the word *Kanud* Ibn Kathir stacks up an unusually long chain of agreement — Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, Abu Al-Jawza, Abu Al-Aliyah, Abu Ad-Duha, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Muhammad bin Qays, Ad-Dahhak, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas, Ibn Zayd — all saying the same thing: **Al-Kanud means ungrateful.**

Then **Al-Hasan Al-Basri** gives the definition that makes it usable:

> **Al-Kanud is the one who counts the calamities that befall him — and forgets Allah's favours.**

That is the whole diagnosis in one line. Not a man who denies blessings, but a man whose *accounting* is one-sided: the losses are itemised and remembered, the gifts are not entered in the ledger at all.

## 3. The Witness (100:7)

> *And to that He bears witness.*

The pronoun is ambiguous in the Arabic, and Ibn Kathir preserves both readings:

- **Qatadah and Sufyan Ath-Thawri:** *Allah* is a witness to man's ingratitude.
- **Muhammad bin Ka'b Al-Qurazi:** the pronoun refers to *man* — **he is a witness against himself**. He knows what he is. It is obvious in his condition, apparent from his own statements and deeds.

For the second reading Ibn Kathir reaches for a parallel elsewhere in the Qur'an, where disbelief is something people testify to against themselves without meaning to:

> *It is not for the idolators to maintain the Masajid of Allah, while they witness disbelief against themselves.* (9:17)

Ingratitude, on this reading, is not a hidden condition. It is on public display in how a person talks and what he does — including to himself.

---

## 4. The One Thing He Does Love Fiercely (100:8)

> *And verily, he is violent in the love of wealth.*

"The good" here means **wealth**, and man's love of it is *severe*. Ibn Kathir notes two ways of taking it:

1. He is **intense in his love** of wealth; or
2. He is **covetous and stingy** because of that love.

His ruling: **both views are correct.** They are the same disease seen from two sides — the grasping inward and the refusal to let go outward.

Set beside verse 6, the picture is complete: the ingratitude isn't apathy. The capacity for devotion is fully intact. It has simply been pointed at wealth instead of at the One who gave it.

---

## 5. The Threat about the Hereafter (100:9–11)

Ibn Kathir marks the turn explicitly: **Allah now encourages abstinence from worldly things and striving for the Hereafter**, and tells man what is coming after the present condition — and what horrors he will face.

> *Knows he not that when the contents of the graves are poured forth,*

Meaning **the dead in them will be brought out.**

> *And that which is in the breasts shall be made known,*

**Ibn Abbas and others:** *what was in their souls would be exposed and made apparent.*

Notice the pairing, which is the surah's second great contrast. The graves give up **bodies**. The chests give up **contents** — motives, intentions, the private inner life that no one ever saw. Both are turned out on the same Day. The man who was already a witness against himself (v.7) now has nothing left to keep back.

> *Verily, that Day their Lord will be Well-Acquainted with them.*

Meaning: **He knows everything they used to do, and He will compensate them for it with the most deserving reward. He does not do even the slightest amount of injustice.**

The surah closes not on terror but on **exactness**. Nothing will be lost, nothing exaggerated, nothing shaved off.

---

## The Thread Running Through

Al-`Adiyat sets a warhorse against a man, and lets the comparison do the work.

The horse runs until its breath tears; it strikes fire out of stone; it goes into the middle of the enemy without hesitation — and it is not even the party that received the favours. The man received everything, and Ibn Kathir's authorities describe him **tallying his misfortunes while forgetting his gifts**, then spending the whole of his intensity on money.

Then the surah reminds him, in three verses, that both his body and his insides are going to be turned out and read by Someone already acquainted with all of it.

---

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**Al-Hasan's definition of *Kanud*: the one who counts the calamities and forgets the favours.**

Ingratitude, in this surah, is not a belief you hold. It is a habit of accounting — and it can be reversed the same way it was formed, by changing what you keep count of.
