*Revealed in Makkah · 112 ayat · "The Prophets" — sixteen of them named in a single sweep*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-Anbiya' opens with a warning that the reckoning is near while people look away, and closes with the Prophet ﷺ asking Allah to judge. Between those two points it does something no other surah does at quite this scale: it lines up **sixteen prophets, one after another**, each in a few verses, each rescued.

The logic is cumulative rather than argued. The Quraysh had objected that a *man* could not be a messenger. So the surah answers by parading the men — Ibrahim in the fire, Nuh in the flood, Yunus in the fish, Ayub in his ruin, Dawud with mountains echoing him — and after each one the same refrain sounds: *We answered, We saved, We delivered.* By the end the argument has been made without being stated. This is what happens to the people who carry the message, and this is what happens to the towns that mock them.

Ibn Kathir opens with Al-Bukhari's report from **Ibn Mas'ud**, who grouped this surah with Al-Isra', Al-Kahf, Maryam and Ta Ha and said of the five: *they are among the earliest and most beautiful Surahs, and they are my treasure.*

---

## 1. The Hour Is Close and No One Is Looking (21:1–6)

> *Draws near for mankind their reckoning, while they turn away in heedlessness.*

Ibn Kathir reads the heedlessness as practical, not merely mental: **they are not working for it or preparing for it.** An-Nasa'i's report from Abu Sa'id glosses "in heedlessness" as *in this world* — the distraction is the world itself.

Their reaction to revelation is to *listen while they play*, with hearts occupied elsewhere. Ibn Abbas turned this into a rebuke that still lands: why interrogate the People of the Book about scriptures that have been altered and added to, when your own Book is the most recently revealed, and **you read it pure and unadulterated**?

Their theories about the Qur'an are catalogued and are mutually incompatible — it is magic, it is poetry, it is jumbled dreams, it is invented. Ibn Kathir notes the incoherence itself is the tell: *See what examples they have put forward for you. So they have gone astray* (17:48).

They demand a sign like Salih's she-camel. The answer is historical: **not one town that was given its sign believed.** Signs did not save the destroyed nations, and these people witnessed clearer proofs at the hands of the Prophet ﷺ than any nation before.

---

## 2. Messengers Ate Food and Died (21:7–15)

The core objection — *is this more than a human being like you?* — gets a flat answer. Every messenger was a man. **Ask the people of the Reminder** — the Jews, Christians and other communities of knowledge — whether the messengers sent to them were angels or human beings.

Ibn Kathir turns the objection into a mercy: sending messengers *from among themselves* is precisely how people can receive and learn from the message. And they were fully human — *We did not place them in bodies that did not eat food, nor were they immortals.* They ate, walked in markets, earned livings, and died. What set them apart was revelation, nothing else.

On **"a Book in which there is Dhikrukum"**, Ibn Abbas gives one word: **"Honour for you."**

Then the destroyed towns. When the punishment became undeniable they tried to run — and were mocked as they fled: *return to your luxuries and your fine homes, in order that you may be questioned* — about whether you were grateful for them. **Qatadah:** "Mocking them." They confessed too late, and the confession continued *until We made them as a field that is reaped* — movement and voices cut off mid-sentence.

---

## 3. Creation Is Not a Game (21:16–20)

> *We created not the heavens and the earth and all that is between them for play.*

Created *in truth*, meaning with justice — so that evil is requited and good rewarded. **Mujahid**, on *had We intended to take a pastime*: if Allah had wanted amusement, "We would not have created Paradise or Hell or death or the resurrection or the Reckoning." Qatadah, As-Suddi and others read the verse's conditional as a flat negation: *We will not do that.* Mujahid's rule of thumb — wherever this particle of supposition appears in the Qur'an, it functions as a denial.

Then the image that gives the passage its force: **We fling the truth against the falsehood, so it destroys it, and behold, it disappears.** Truth is not merely stated; it is thrown, and falsehood is what breaks.

Set against the idolaters are the angels, who *are not too proud to worship Him, nor are they weary* — they glorify night and day and **never slacken**. Ibn Kathir: they do not get tired and they do not get bored.

---

## 4. Two Gods Would Have Wrecked It (21:21–29)

> *Had there been therein gods besides Allah, then verily, both would have been ruined.*

The argument from cosmic order: multiple gods would have meant division and rivalry — *each god would have taken away what he had created, and some would have tried to overcome others* (23:91). The universe holds together because one will governs it.

And then the verse that defines the relationship between Creator and creature: **He cannot be questioned as to what He does, while they will be questioned.** Ibn Kathir grounds this in attributes rather than raw power alone — His might, majesty, knowledge, wisdom, justice and subtlety together mean His rule cannot be overturned or objected to.

The angels are not daughters of Allah but **honoured servants**: *They speak not until He has spoken, and they act on His command.* They do not initiate before Him, they cannot intercede except for those with whom He is pleased, and they stand in awe out of fear of Him. Any claim to divinity, even hypothetically from among them, meets Hell — a conditional sentence, Ibn Kathir notes, whose condition need never occur.

---

## 5. Signs in the Fabric of Things (21:30–33)

Four signs, each a standing argument:

**The heavens and earth were joined, then parted.** One mass, piled together, then separated — seven heavens and seven earths, air placed between the earth and the lowest heaven, rain sent down and vegetation brought forth.

There is a lovely exchange here. Ibn Umar sent a questioner to "that old man" — Ibn Abbas — who answered that the heavens were joined and did not rain, the earth was joined and nothing grew, and only when living beings needed populating did rain come from one and vegetation from the other. When the answer came back, Ibn Umar said: **"I did not like the daring attitude of Ibn Abbas in his Tafsir of the Qur'an, but now I know that he has been given knowledge of the Qur'an."**

**Every living thing made from water** — the origin of all life. Abu Hurayrah asked the Prophet ﷺ to tell him about everything, and was told: *Everything was created from water.* He then asked for one deed that would bring him to Paradise, and was given four: **spread salam, feed others, uphold ties of kinship, and pray at night while people sleep.**

**Mountains as ballast**, keeping the earth steady, with passes cut through them so people can travel between lands. **The heaven as a guarded roof**, raised like a dome — and the indictment attached: *yet they turn away from its signs*, passing beneath it without ever thinking about how it was made.

**The night, the day, the sun and the moon,** each on its own path, its own light, its own allotted term, *each in an orbit floating*. **Ibn Abbas:** "They revolve like a spinning wheel, in a circle."

---

## 6. Everyone Tastes Death (21:34–47)

Immortality was granted to no one, including the Prophet ﷺ — *then if you die, would they live forever?* Ibn Kathir: they hope to outlast you, but everything passes.

On *We shall test you with evil and with good by way of trial*, **Ibn Abbas** unpacks the pairs: **difficulty and prosperity, health and sickness, wealth and poverty, the lawful and the unlawful, obedience and sin, guidance and misguidance.** The test is not one-sided; ease tests as surely as hardship does.

*Man is created of haste* — and they hurried the punishment on out of certainty it would never come. Ibn Kathir's answer is that if they knew what they were asking for, they would stop asking: fire from above them and beneath them, garments of tar, and no respite delayed even for an instant.

Consolation for the Prophet ﷺ: **messengers were mocked before you, and the scoffers were surrounded by the very thing they mocked.** Meanwhile Allah guards these same mockers night and day with an eye that never sleeps, and they turn away from the One protecting them.

Two warnings about being lulled. **The luxuries and the long lives** they enjoy have convinced them they are on to something good. And: *See they not that We gradually reduce the land from its outlying borders?* — **Al-Hasan Al-Basri: "This means the victory of Islam over disbelief."** The map is shrinking around them while they congratulate themselves.

### The Balance and the man who freed his slaves

> *And We shall set up Balances of justice on the Day of Resurrection... and if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it.*

Most scholars hold it is a single Balance, the plural reflecting the sheer number of deeds weighed in it.

Ahmad records that a Companion came to the Prophet ﷺ about two slaves who lied to him, betrayed him and disobeyed him, and whom he struck and insulted. The reply was an accounting: their misconduct will be measured against your punishment. If the two match, you break even. **If your punishment was less than they deserved, that counts in your favour. If it was more, Allah will take what is owed to them out of you.**

The man wept. The Prophet ﷺ asked whether he had not read this very verse. And the man said: I think there is nothing better than keeping away from these people — **I call upon you to bear witness that they are all free.**

The other side of the same scale, from the Two Sahihs: two phrases light on the tongue, heavy in the Balance, beloved to Ar-Rahman — **Subhan Allahi wa bi hamdihi, Subhan Allahil 'Azim.**

## 7. Ibrahim: An Argument, Then a Fire (21:48–70)

*We bestowed aforetime on Ibrahim his guidance* — Ibn Kathir stresses **aforetime**: he was inspired with truth and proof against his people from an early age.

His question is deceptively simple: *What are these images to which you are devoted?* Their entire case is inherited habit — *we found our fathers worshipping them* — and Ibrahim refuses the appeal to ancestry outright: **you and your fathers alike have been in manifest error.**

Then the plan. He swears, within earshot of some of them, to deal with the idols once they leave for their festival. When they invite him along he says *I am sick* — and Ibn Mas'ud's report places this the day after the oath. He smashes them all **except the biggest**, and (it was said) leaves the hammer in its hands, so the obvious inference is jealousy among gods.

Hauled before a crowd — which Ibn Kathir identifies as **exactly what Ibrahim wanted**, the largest possible audience — he says: *this one, the biggest of them, did it. Ask them, if they can speak!*

The trap closes. They turn on themselves, look at the ground, and admit: *you know well that these speak not.* Which is the whole case. **Qatadah:** they confessed their guilt and confusion. Ibrahim's reply needs no elaboration — *do you worship besides Allah things that can neither profit you nor harm you? Fie upon you!* **He defeated them in argument and left them with no way out.**

Having lost the argument they reach for force: **Burn him.** As-Suddi reports women vowing to bring firewood if they recovered from illness; a pit was dug and lit with flames unlike anything seen before; Ibrahim was catapulted in. As he was thrown he said **"Sufficient for me is Allah, and He is the best disposer of affairs"** — the same words, Ibn Kathir notes via Al-Bukhari from Ibn Abbas, that the Muslims said at 3:173.

> *O fire! Be you cool and safety for Ibrahim!*

Ibn Abbas: the angel of rain was waiting for the command, **but the command of Allah was swifter** — and no fire on earth was left burning. Ibn Abbas and Abu Al-'Aliyah add the precision of the wording: had Allah said only *cool* and not *and safety*, **the cold itself would have harmed him.**

*Ibrahim did not tell lies except on three occasions* — the hadith in the Two Sahihs, two of them for Allah's sake (this one, and *the big one did it*), and the third concerning Sarah before the tyrant, from which Hajar came into their household. When Abu Hurayrah narrated it he would say: *This is your mother, O sons of the water of the heaven.*

He was rescued to the blessed land with **Lut**, given Ishaq, and given Ya'qub as **Nafilatan** — a gift beyond what was asked. Ata' and Mujahid: "as a gift." Ibn Abbas and Qatadah: **"the gift of a son who has a son."** He asked for one, and was given the grandson too. And they were made *leaders, guiding by Our command* — who, Ibn Kathir notes pointedly, **did what they enjoined others to do.**

## 8. The Roll Call (21:76–91)

**Nuh** — answered, saved with his family and the believers *from the great distress*: difficulty, rejection and harm sustained across a thousand years less fifty, with generation after generation coaching the next to oppose him.

**Dawud and Sulayman** and the ruined field. Sheep strayed at night into a vineyard. Dawud awarded the sheep to the owner of the vines. Sulayman proposed instead: give the vines to the shepherd to tend until they are restored, and the sheep to the vine-owner to benefit from meanwhile — **then each returns to its owner**. Neither party loses capital; the loss is repaid out of usufruct.

Ibn Kathir uses this to settle an anxiety about judgeship. Al-Hasan Al-Basri found the judge Iyas bin Mu'awiyah weeping at his appointment, convinced that a judge who errs goes to Hell. Al-Hasan's answer: **Allah praised Sulayman but did not condemn Dawud.** And the hadith in Al-Bukhari: a judge who exerts himself and gets it right has two rewards; one who exerts himself and gets it wrong **still has one**. On the prophets specifically, Ibn Kathir holds they were infallible and divinely supported.

The parallel case, from Ahmad: two women, a wolf, one surviving child. Dawud awarded him to the elder. Sulayman called for a sword to divide the boy — and **the younger woman's refusal to let him be cut identified her as the mother.**

Dawud's other gifts: mountains and birds glorifying alongside him, which Ibn Kathir attributes to **the beauty of his voice** reciting the Zabur — birds hovering to repeat after him, mountains echoing. Hence the Prophet ﷺ, listening unseen to Abu Musa Al-Ash'ari recite at night: *This man has been given one of the wind instruments of the family of Dawud.* Abu Musa's reply: had I known you were listening, **I would have done my best for you.**

And the coats of mail. **Qatadah:** before Dawud they wore plated armour; he was the first to make chain mail — soft iron, rings balanced neither so loose that they rattle nor so tight that they cannot move. Ibn Kathir reads *are you then grateful?* as addressed to us: this was taught to him **for your sake**.

**Sulayman** was given the raging wind, carrying his entire encampment — horses, camels, tents, troops — shading him from the heat, a month's journey in a morning; and the shayatin who dived for pearls, wholly under his control, **and it was We Who guarded them** — so that none of them could harm him.

**Ayub**, who lost livestock, crops, children, houses, and finally his health, left at the edge of the city with no one but his wife, who is reported to have gone into service to support him. The framing hadith: **the people tested most severely are the Prophets, then the righteous, then the next best** — a man is tested in proportion to the strength of his religious commitment.

Yazid bin Maysarah preserves what Ayub said in the ruin: *there was no corner of my heart that was not filled with attachment to these worldly things, then You took all of that away and You emptied my heart, and there is nothing to stand between me and You.* **If my enemy Iblis knew of this, he would be jealous of me.**

And afterwards, the golden locusts raining down, Ayub gathering them into his garment. Asked whether he had not had enough, he said: **"O Lord, who can ever have enough of Your mercy!"**

On the restoration of his family, Mujahid's report: he was offered his family back in this world, or left in Paradise with replacements here. **He chose to leave them in Paradise.** And the purpose is stated outright — *a Reminder for all those who worship Us*: so that those under trial do not conclude that trial means they are uncared for.

**Isma'il, Idris and Dhul-Kifl** — all among the patient. On Dhul-Kifl, Ibn Kathir notes the company he keeps suggests prophethood, others held he was a righteous man and a just king; **Ibn Jarir declined to decide**, and Ibn Kathir leaves it there.

**Zakariyya**, who called *in secret, hiding it from his people*, asking not to be left single — and whose barren wife was cured for him. The description of that household is the model the surah offers: *they used to hasten on to do good deeds, and they used to call on Us with hope and fear, and they were Khashi'in before Us.* **Ath-Thawri** on hope and fear: hoping for what is with Him, fearing what is with Him.

**Maryam** — placed immediately after Zakariyya, Ibn Kathir explains, because the two are connected and escalating: first a child from an aged man and a barren woman, then **the more wondrous case — a child from a woman with no man at all.**

## 9. Yunus: The Prayer from Inside Three Darknesses (21:87–88)

He left his people in anger after threatening them with punishment. They, realising a prophet does not lie, went out into the desert — separating mothers from young so that **camels groaned, cows lowed and sheep bleated** alongside their pleading — and Allah spared them. They are the one town in the Qur'an whose faith arrived in time (10:98).

Yunus meanwhile boarded a ship, the lot fell to him three times, and he removed his garment and threw himself into the sea. The fish was inspired **not to devour his flesh or break his bones**: he is not food for you; your belly is a prison for him.

On **the darknesses**, Ibn Mas'ud: the darkness of the fish's belly, the darkness of the sea, the darkness of the night. Salim bin Abu Al-Ja'd reports an even bleaker version — a fish inside another fish inside the dark sea. Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn Abbas add that he was carried to the seabed, **where he heard the rocks glorifying Allah** — and it was then that he said it:

> *There is no God but You, Glorified be You! Truly, I have been of the wrongdoers.*

'Awf Al-A'rabi: when Yunus first realised where he was he thought he had died; then he moved his legs, and **prostrated where he was**, saying, *O Lord, I have taken a place of worship to You in a place which no other person has reached.*

The verse ends by generalising the rescue — *and thus We do deliver the believers* — and Ibn Kathir attaches the hadith preserved through the Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas / 'Uthman incident: **no Muslim ever prays to his Lord with these words for anything but He answers his prayer.**

## 10. One Ummah, Broken Up (21:92–94)

> *Truly, this, your Ummah is one, and I am your Lord, therefore worship Me.*

Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Qatadah: **"Your religion is one religion."** Al-Hasan: your path is one path.

The hadith Ibn Kathir cites is the key to it: **We Prophets are brothers from different mothers, and our religion is one.** The creed is identical throughout; the sacred laws differed — *to each among you We have prescribed a Law and a clear way* (5:48).

What broke was not the religion but the nations: *they have broken up and differed in their religion among themselves*, some believing in their messengers and some rejecting them. And the guarantee for whoever holds on — *his efforts will not be rejected. Verily, We record it for him* — meaning **nothing at all is lost.**

## 11. The End of the World (21:95–104)

A ban is laid on every destroyed town: **they shall not return** to this world before the Resurrection.

Then **Ya'juj and Ma'juj**, swooping *from every Hadab* — a raised portion of land — coming forth quickly to spread corruption. Ibn Abbas, watching boys tumbling and pouncing on one another, said: **this is how Ya'juj and Ma'juj will emerge.**

The hadith from Abu Sa'id describes them draining rivers dry, Muslims sealed inside strongholds, and one of them boasting *we have defeated the people of the earth; now the people of heaven are left* — hurling a spear into the sky which returns stained, as a test. Then a worm in their necks ends them all at once.

Ibn Kathir also gives An-Nawwas bin Sam'an's long hadith in full: the Dajjal with his curly hair and floating eye, emerging between Syria and Iraq, his forty days of which one is a year; the descent of **'Isa** at the white minaret east of Damascus, hands resting on the wings of two angels, killing him at the gate of Ludd; then Ya'juj and Ma'juj; then rain that washes the earth **until it looks like a mirror**; a pomegranate feeding a group who shelter in its skin; and finally a pleasant wind that takes the soul of every believer, leaving only the worst of people for the Hour to fall upon.

### The objection of Ibn Az-Zab'ari

*Certainly you and that which you are worshipping besides Allah are fuel for Hell* — and Abdullah bin Az-Zab'ari thought he had found the flaw. **If everything worshipped goes to Hell with its worshippers, then what of the angels, and 'Uzayr, and 'Isa?** Al-Walid and the others present were impressed.

The Prophet ﷺ answered that anyone **who likes to be worshipped** instead of Allah will be with those who worshipped him — because such a one is really worshipping Shaytan. And the following verses were revealed: *those for whom the good has preceded from Us, they will be removed far therefrom.*

Ibn Kathir's own verdict is blunter: the objection was **a serious mistake**, because the address was to the people of Makkah about **inanimate idols that cannot think**. It was never a general rule about everything ever worshipped, and it could not touch those who did righteous deeds and never accepted the worship offered them.

For the saved: *they shall not hear the slightest sound of it* — they will not feel its heat in their bodies — *while they abide in that which their own selves desire*, safe from what they feared and given all they love. **The greatest terror will not grieve them**, and the angels will meet them coming out of their graves with a greeting.

Then the closing image of creation: **the heaven rolled up like a scroll for books.** As-Suddi held As-Sijill was an angel keeping records, but Ibn Kathir prefers the view of Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Qatadah — it is simply **the written record**, this being the well-attested usage in Arabic. *As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it.* The hadith attached: **you will be gathered barefoot, naked and uncircumcised.**

## 12. Who Inherits, and Why He Was Sent (21:105–112)

> *We have written in Az-Zabur after Adh-Dhikr that My righteous servants shall inherit the land.*

The reports differ on the two books — Sa'id bin Jubayr took Az-Zabur to cover the Tawrah, Injil and Qur'an; Ibn Abbas and others took it as what was given to Dawud with Adh-Dhikr as the Tawrah; Mujahid took Adh-Dhikr as **Umm Al-Kitab with Allah**, and Ath-Thawri as Al-Lawh Al-Mahfuz. On **"the land"**, a striking consensus — Ibn Abbas, Abu Al-'Aliyah, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Ash-Sha'bi, Qatadah, As-Suddi, Ar-Rabi' and Ath-Thawri: **the land of Paradise.**

And then the verse the whole surah has been building toward:

> *And We have sent you not but as a mercy for the 'Alamin.*

Not a mercy to believers. A mercy **for all of them** — whoever accepts it is happy in both worlds, whoever rejects it loses in both. Muslim records that when the Prophet ﷺ was asked to curse the idolaters he said: **I was not sent as a curse; rather I was sent as a mercy.**

Ahmad preserves something even more personal, from Salman via Hudhayfah — the Prophet ﷺ saying that any man of his Ummah he insulted or cursed in anger (*for I am a man from among the sons of Adam, and I get angry just as you do*) will have **that anger turned into blessings for him on the Day of Resurrection.**

And to the question Ibn Kathir raises himself — what mercy do disbelievers get from him? — Ibn Abbas's answer: the believer receives mercy in this world and the next, while the one who does not believe is **spared what befell the earlier nations**, the earthquakes and the stoning.

The surah ends with the terms laid out plainly: *your God is only one God — will you then be Muslims?* If they turn away, then **notice given on equal terms**, so that both sides know exactly where they stand. As for when — *I know not whether that which you are promised is near or far.* The delay itself may be the trial.

And the last words are a prayer, one Qatadah says the prophets always made and the Prophet ﷺ was commanded to make, and which Zayd bin Aslam reports he said whenever he witnessed fighting:

> *My Lord! Judge You in truth! Our Lord is the Most Gracious, Whose help is to be sought against that which you attribute.*

---

## The Takeaway

Al-Anbiya' answers a sneer — *is this more than a human being like you?* — by showing that being a human being is exactly the point. Sixteen men, none immortal, all of whom ate food and walked in markets, and every one of them answered when he called.

The surah puts its own two closing lines in the right order. First **inheritance**: the righteous get the land, and the land is Paradise. Then **mercy**: the man they were mocking was sent as a mercy to everything that exists, and refused to curse them even when invited to.

Between the calamity of Ya'juj and Ma'juj and the sky rolled up like a scroll sits Yunus, at the bottom of the sea, inside three darknesses, praying. And the verse does not stop with him — *and thus We do deliver the believers*. Whatever the darkness, the prayer is already known and the rescue is already precedented.
