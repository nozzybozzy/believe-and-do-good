*Revealed in Makkah · 30 ayat · "The Dawn" — named for the oath in its first word*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-Fajr is a surah about **measurement** — about the fact that human beings are consistently wrong about what makes a life valuable, and about what Allah does to the things they think are permanent.

It opens with four oaths that Ibn Kathir reads as oaths on **times of worship** — dawn, the ten nights of Dhul-Hijjah, the even and the odd, the departing night. Then it swings hard into history and names three powers: **'Ad**, the tallest and strongest people ever created, living in pillared houses; **Thamud**, who carved their homes out of solid rock; **Fir'awn**, with his armies and his iron pegs. Height, engineering, force. Each one erased.

Then, without transition, the surah turns on the reader and diagnoses the misreading that makes all of this possible. A man is given money and says *my Lord has honoured me*. The same man is given less and says *my Lord has humiliated me*. Allah's answer is a single syllable — ***Kalla***, **no** — followed by the charge sheet that reveals what the money was actually doing to him: he does not honour the orphan, he does not push anyone to feed the poor, he eats inheritance greedily, and he loves wealth *Jamma* — in heaps.

The surah then flattens everything. *When the earth is levelled, Dakkan Dakka.* The people who built pillars "the like of which were not created in the land" end up on a plain with no features at all, and Hell is dragged in on seventy thousand leashes. Man finally remembers — too late to matter.

And then, in four of the gentlest ayat in the Qur'an, the surah shows what honour actually looked like the whole time: not property, but a **soul at rest**, called home, pleased and pleasing.

Note the shape: it begins at **dawn** and ends with a soul arriving. The name of the surah is the hour of return.

## 1. Before the Surah: Mu'adh and the Long Prayer

Ibn Kathir opens not with the oaths but with an incident about **this surah being recited in prayer** (An-Nasa'i, from Jabir).

Mu'adh led a prayer and made it long. A man joined, could not sustain it, slipped out and prayed alone at the side of the Masjid, then left. Mu'adh's verdict: *"He is a hypocrite."*

The Prophet ﷺ questioned the young man, who explained himself plainly — the prayer was too long for him, and he had a she-camel to feed. The Prophet ﷺ then turned to Mu'adh:

> **"Are you causing trouble, Mu'adh? Why don't you recite** *Glorify the Name of your Lord the Most High* (87), *By the sun and its brightness* (91), *By the dawn* (89), *and By the night as it envelops* (92)?"

Al-Fajr is named in a hadith about **not making religion heavier than people can carry**. It is worth holding that in mind while reading a surah that then spends fourteen verses on annihilated civilisations: the severity is aimed at arrogance, not at the tired man with a camel to feed.

## 2. The Four Oaths (89:1–4)

> *By the dawn; and by the ten nights; and by the even and the odd; and by the night when it departs.*

**Al-Fajr** — the morning. This is the well-known position, held by **'Ali, Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ikrimah, Mujahid and As-Suddi**. A minority view from **Masruq and Muhammad bin Ka'b** narrows it to the **Day of Sacrifice** (Yawm An-Nahr) specifically, the day that closes the ten.

**The ten nights** — the first ten days of **Dhul-Hijjah**. Ibn 'Abbas, Ibn Az-Zubayr, Mujahid and others, early and late, agree.

Ibn Kathir anchors this with the hadith in **Sahih Al-Bukhari** from Ibn 'Abbas:

> *"There are no days in which righteous deeds are more beloved to Allah than these days."*

The Companions pressed him: **not even Jihad in the way of Allah?** He replied:

> *"Not even Jihad in the way of Allah — except for a man who goes out with his self and his wealth, and returns with none of it."*

The only thing that outweighs ordinary good deeds in these ten days is a man who leaves and does not come back.

**The night when it departs** — glossed three ways, all converging: *when it goes away* (Ibn 'Abbas), *as some parts of it remove other parts* ('Abdullah bin Az-Zubayr), *when it moves along* (Mujahid, Abu Al-'Aliyah, Qatadah, Malik from Zayd bin Aslam, Ibn Zayd).

**What the oaths are for.** Ibn Kathir's summary is the key to the surah's opening: the oath concerns **the times of worship and the acts of worship themselves** — Hajj, Salah, and everything the humble and God-fearing do to draw near to Him. Allah swears by the calendar of devotion. Immediately after, He will describe people who had no such calendar.

## 3. *Hijr*: Why the Intellect Is Called "Restraint" (89:5)

> *Is there (not) in them sufficient proof for men of Hijr?*

Meaning: for one who possesses **intellect, sound reasoning, understanding and religious discernment**.

Ibn Kathir then gives a small lesson in Arabic that carries real moral weight. Intellect is called **Hijr** because *it prevents* a person from what does not befit him in word and deed. The same root runs through:

- **Hijr al-Bayt** — the enclosure that prevents the one making Tawaf from clinging to the wall facing Ash-Sham.
- **Hijr al-Yamamah** — the pigeon's cage.
- *"Hajara al-Hakim so-and-so"* — the judge issued an interdiction preventing a man from freely disposing of his wealth.
- And the cry of the criminals on the Day of Judgement: ***Hijran Mahjura*** — "a forbidding ban" (25:22).

Different cases, one meaning. **Reason is a restraint.** A mind that does not stop you from anything is not functioning as a mind. That is the standard against which the next nine verses are measured.

## 4. 'Ad, Iram of the Pillars (89:6–8)

These were **the first people of 'Ad** — descendants of 'Ad bin Iram bin 'Aws bin Sam bin Nuh (Ibn Ishaq). Allah sent them **Hud**; they rejected him; He saved Hud and the believers and destroyed the rest with a furious wind — *seven nights and eight days in succession*, leaving them like **hollow date-palm trunks** (69:7–8).

**"Of the pillars."** They lived in trellised houses raised on firm columns. They were the **strongest people of their time in physical stature** and the mightiest in power — and Hud's argument to them was precisely that: this strength is a gift, so spend it in obedience to the One who made you this way (7:69). Their reply is recorded elsewhere and is one of the most self-defeating sentences in the Qur'an:

> *"Who is mightier than us in strength?"* — **See they not that Allah, Who created them, was mightier in strength than them?** (41:15)

**A disputed pronoun, and why it matters.** *"The like of which were not created in the land"* — what is "which"?

- **Ibn Zayd**: the *pillars*. They built columns among the hills unlike anything built before.
- **Qatadah and Ibn Jarir**: the *tribe*. No people like them had been created in the land, in their time.

Ibn Kathir sides with the second and gives a clean grammatical reason: had Allah meant buildings, He would have said *"the like of which were not **produced**"*. He said **created**. Buildings are produced; peoples are created.

On **Iram** itself: **Mujahid** says it was an ancient nation, the first of 'Ad; **Qatadah bin Di'amah and As-Suddi** say it refers to the **royal house of 'Ad** — a reading Ibn Kathir calls "good and strong."

---

## 5. Thamud, and Fir'awn of the Pegs (89:9–14)

**Thamud** *hewed (Jabu) the rocks in the valley* — they cut and carved their dwellings out of stone (Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, Qatadah, Ad-Dahhak, Ibn Zayd). Ibn Kathir notes the word's family: *Jabu* is used for tearing open a leopard's hide, or slitting a garment — and **Jayb**, the opening at the neck of a shirt, comes from the same root. Compare 26:149: *you hew houses out of the mountains with great skill.*

**Fir'awn with Al-Awtad** — "the stakes." Two readings:

- **Ibn 'Abbas** (via Al-'Awfi): the **armies** that enforced his commands and propped up his rule.
- **Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, As-Suddi**: literal **iron pegs**, which he used to nail people's hands and feet before hanging them.

All three — 'Ad, Thamud, Fir'awn — *transgressed beyond bounds in the lands and made therein much mischief*, and the response was the same: a torment sent down from the sky that could not be repelled.

And the verse that closes the section is the hinge of the whole surah:

> ***Verily, your Lord is Ever Watchful.***

**Ibn 'Abbas: "He hears and He sees."** He watches His creation in what they do, and will recompense each in this life and the next, judging with justice, far removed from injustice and tyranny. The three empires were not accidents. They were verdicts.

## 6. The Great Misreading: Wealth and Poverty (89:15–16)

> *As for man, when his Lord tries him by giving him honour and bounties, he says: "My Lord has honoured me." But when He tries him by restricting his provision, he says: "My Lord has humiliated me!"*

Ibn Kathir's comment is a direct refutation. Man believes that abundance is a certificate of divine approval. **It is not — it is a test.** And he believes constraint is a mark of divine contempt. **It is not — that is a test too.**

> *Do they think that in the wealth and children with which We enlarge them, We hasten unto them with good things? Nay, but they perceive not.* (23:55–56)

Then the correction, in one word: ***Kalla*** — **"No!"** — meaning, as Ibn Kathir puts it, *the matter is not as he claims, neither in this nor in that.*

The rule beneath it is stated with unusual bluntness:

> **Allah gives wealth to those He loves and to those He does not love. He withholds from those He loves and from those He does not love.**

Prosperity carries no information about your standing. The variable is not the circumstance but the response: **if wealthy, be grateful; if poor, be patient.** Allah is to be obeyed in either condition.

---

## 7. The Charge Sheet (89:17–20)

Having denied that money is honour, the surah shows what money actually did:

**"You do not honour the orphan."** Ibn Kathir reads this as a *command* to honour him, and pairs it with the hadith from **Sahl bin Sa'd** (Abu Dawud):

> *"The guardian of the orphan and I will be like these two in Paradise"* — and he put his middle and index fingers together.

**"And you urge not one another to feed the Miskin."** Not merely failing to give — failing to **create the culture of giving**. They neither command kindness to the poor and needy, nor encourage each other toward it. The sin is social, not just personal.

**"And you devour the *Turath* with greedy devouring."** *Turath* is **inheritance** — wealth that arrived without being earned. They take it however they can get it, **lawful or forbidden**, without distinguishing.

**"And you love wealth with love *Jamma*."** *Jamma* means **in abundance, heaped up**. Ibn Kathir's closing note is quiet and devastating: this love *increases some of them in their wickedness.* The wealth is not neutral in their hands. It compounds.

## 8. The Day the Pillars Come Down (89:21–26)

> *Kalla! When the earth is levelled, **Dakkan Dakka**.*

*Kalla* here means **truly**. The earth and the mountains are flattened, levelled, made even — and the creatures rise from their graves.

Read this against verse 7. A people who built columns *the like of which were not created in the land* end on ground with no elevation left anywhere on it.

**"And your Lord comes with the angels, rank upon rank."** For the session of Judgement. Ibn Kathir places the **Great Intercession** here: humanity goes from one great Messenger to another, and each says *"I cannot do that for you"* — until they reach **Muhammad ﷺ**, who says: ***"I will do it, I will do it."*** He goes and seeks permission, and it is granted. This is the first of the intercessions, and it is the **Maqam Mahmud** — the praiseworthy station — already discussed in Surah Al-Isra'. Then Allah comes for the Judgement as He wills, and the angels come in rows upon rows.

**"And Hell will be brought near that Day."** From **Ibn Mas'ud** in *Sahih Muslim*:

> *Hell will be brought that Day with seventy thousand reins, and with every rein seventy thousand angels dragging it.*

**"That Day will man remember — but how will remembrance avail him?"** He will remember his deeds, near and distant. The faculty finally switches on at the exact moment it becomes useless.

**"He will say: Alas! Would that I had sent forth for my life!"** Ibn Kathir notes it cuts both ways: the disobedient man regrets his sins; **the obedient man regrets that he did not do more.** And then the narration from **Muhammad bin Abi 'Amirah**, a Companion, recorded by Ahmad:

> If a servant fell on his face in prostration from the day he was born until the day he died an old man, in obedience to Allah, **he would scorn that act on the Day of Judgement** — and would wish to be returned to this life to earn more reward.

A whole lifetime of prostration, and his own verdict on it will be *not enough*.

**"None will punish as He will punish, and none will bind as He will bind."** No one is punished more severely than those Allah punishes for disobeying Him; no one is bound more severely than those bound by **Az-Zabaniyah**, the angels of Hell, for the disbelievers in their Lord.

---

## 9. The Soul at Rest (89:27–30)

The surah has spent twenty-six verses on the criminals. It now turns — Ibn Kathir marks the turn explicitly — to **the pure and tranquil soul, always at rest and abiding by the truth**:

> ***O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing. Enter then among My servants, and enter My Paradise.***

Each phrase, as Ibn Kathir glosses it:

- **Return to your Lord** — to His company, His reward, and what He has prepared for His servants in His Paradise.
- ***Radiyah*** (well-pleased) — **within itself**. Content.
- ***Mardiyyah*** (well-pleasing) — **pleased with Allah, and He pleased with it**, and He will gratify it.
- **Enter among My servants** — into their ranks.
- **Enter My Paradise** — and this, Ibn Kathir says, is spoken **twice**: at the moment of death, and again on the Day of Judgement. It is the same glad tiding the angels bring the believer as he dies and as he rises from the grave.

The surah's answer to *"my Lord has honoured me"* arrives here. The honour was never the money. It was this sentence, addressed to a soul.

**The last narration.** Ibn Abi Hatim, from Ibn 'Abbas: this ayah was revealed while **Abu Bakr** was sitting with the Prophet ﷺ. Abu Bakr said:

> *"O Messenger of Allah! There is nothing better than this!"*

And the Prophet ﷺ replied:

> ***"This will indeed be said to you."***

## If You Remember Nothing Else

1. **Allah swears by the times of worship** — dawn, the ten days of Dhul-Hijjah, the departing night — and only then talks about the people who had no such calendar. The contrast is the argument.
2. **Intellect is called *Hijr* because it restrains.** A mind that stops you from nothing is not doing its job.
3. **'Ad, Thamud and Fir'awn had height, engineering and armies.** All three were "watched," and all three were flattened. *Your Lord is Ever Watchful.*
4. **Wealth is not a certificate and poverty is not a demotion.** Allah gives to those He loves and those He does not; He withholds from both. **Rich → be grateful. Poor → be patient.**
5. **The test is failed at the orphan and the poor man**, not in the accounting ledger — and note that *not urging others to feed the needy* is listed as a sin in its own right.
6. **A lifetime of prostration will feel insufficient on that Day.** The Companion's narration is the most sobering line in the surah.
7. **The honour man was looking for in verse 15 arrives in verse 27** — and it is not a possession. It is being addressed as *O tranquil soul,* pleased and pleasing, and told to come home.
