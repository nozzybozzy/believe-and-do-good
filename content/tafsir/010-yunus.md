*Revealed in Makkah · 109 ayat · named for a prophet who is mentioned in a single verse (10:98)*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Yunus is a Makkan surah, and it reads like one: no legislation, no communal rules, no battles. It is a sustained argument with people who will not be argued out of anything. Three claims are pressed again and again from different angles — **that Allah alone is God, that Muhammad ﷺ is truly sent, and that the return to Allah is real**. Ibn Kathir's commentary follows that structure, so the summary below does too.

The surah's name comes from one verse near the end, but that verse is the emotional hinge of the whole thing: the one town in history that repented *in time*.

---

## 1. "Is It a Wonder?" — The Objection That a Man Could Be a Messenger

The surah opens with the disconnected letters (Ibn Kathir refers back to his discussion at the start of Al-Baqarah rather than repeating it), then goes straight to the Quraysh's central complaint.

Ad-Dahhak reported from Ibn Abbas that when the Prophet ﷺ was sent, most of the Arabs rejected him with the argument that Allah is too great to send a *human* messenger. Ibn Kathir shows this is not a new objection at all — it is the standing objection of every rejecting nation:

- Hud and Salih's peoples: *do you wonder that a reminder has come through a man from among you?* (7:63)
- Quraysh on tawhid: *has he made the gods into one God? This is a curious thing!* (38:5)
- And bluntly, from an earlier nation: *shall mere men guide us?* (64:6)

**On "qadama sidq" — the reward of good deeds laid up with their Lord (10:2)** — the Salaf differ, and Ibn Kathir records all three readings without collapsing them:

| Authority | Reading |
|---|---|
| Ibn Abbas (via Ali bin Abi Talhah) | Eternal happiness has already been written for them |
| Ibn Abbas (via Al-Awfi) | The good reward for what they did |
| Mujahid | Their actual good deeds — prayer, fasting, charity, glorification — *and* the Prophet's ﷺ intercession for them |

---

## 2. The Lord Who Manages Every Affair (10:3–6)

Six days, then the rising over the Throne — which Ibn Kathir describes as the greatest of created things, a ceiling over the rest. Then *tadbir al-amr*: He manages the affair.

The commentary here is essentially a meditation on the scale of that management. No affair distracts Him from another. No matter troubles Him. The persistent asking of creation does not weary Him. He governs the mountains, the oceans, the cities and the wastelands with equal ease — *not a leaf falls but He knows it* (6:59).

**A strange narration Ibn Kathir preserves:** Sa'd bin Ishaq related that when this ayah was revealed, a group met a large caravan they assumed to be Arabs. Asked who they were, they answered that they were Jinn who had left Al-Madinah because of this verse. (Recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim.)

**Intercession is capped at the outset.** *No intercessor except after His permission* — cross-referenced to 2:255, 53:26 and 34:23. This matters enormously later in the surah, because the idolaters' entire defence of their idols is that they intercede.

**Sun and moon are deliberately different** (10:5): the sun's light is *diya'* (radiance of its own), the moon's is *nur* (light). Made unlike each other so they could never be confused; the sun rules the day, the moon the night; the moon's phases give the months, the sun's motion gives the days. All of it *bil-haqq* — not for amusement, but with wisdom and purpose.

---

## 3. Two Kinds of People, Two Destinations (10:7–10)

The wretched are described not as violent but as **satisfied**: they did not hope for the meeting with their Lord, they were *pleased and at rest* with this world.

**Al-Hasan's gloss is the sharpest line in the section:** they adorned this world and praised it until they were content with it; they were heedless of Allah's signs in creation and did not reflect on them, and heedless of His laws and did not keep them.

Against them, the believers: *their Lord will guide them through their faith.* Two readings — that their faith in this world earns guidance to the straight path on the Day of Resurrection, or (Mujahid) that **their faith will itself be a light they walk in**.

Their life in Paradise is bracketed by two utterances — it opens with *Subhanaka Allahumma*, their greeting throughout is *Salam*, and it closes with *al-hamdu lillahi Rabb al-'alamin*. Ibn Kathir draws the point out: Allah is praised at the beginning of creation and at its end, at the opening of His Book and at the opening of its revelation. And in a hadith recorded by Muslim, the people of Paradise are inspired to glorify and praise Allah **as naturally and continuously as they breathe** — because the blessings never stop arriving.

---

## 4. The Mercy of Unanswered Prayers (10:11)

*Were Allah to hasten evil for mankind as He hastens good, their term would already be over.*

Ibn Kathir reads this as a statement about Allah's forbearance in a very specific, domestic sense: people curse themselves, their children and their wealth in moments of grief or rage. He knows they do not mean it — so He does not answer.

> The Prophet ﷺ warned: do not pray against yourselves, your children or your wealth, lest your supplication coincide with an hour in which Allah answers.
> *(Al-Bazzar; also Abu Dawud, from Jabir)*

Mujahid's illustration: the man who says of his son or his money in anger, "O Allah, do not bless him — curse him." Were that answered as readily as his good requests, he would be destroyed.

---

## 5. Man in Distress and Man in Ease (10:12, 10:21–23)

This is one of the surah's recurring portraits, and Ibn Kathir treats the two passages as one theme.

**In affliction**, man calls on Allah lying on his side, sitting, standing — every posture, long supplications (41:51). **When it lifts**, he walks on *as if he had never called on Us*.

**At sea**, the picture is at its most vivid: the ship sailing smoothly, the passengers glad; then the storm, the waves from every side, the certainty of being encircled — and in that moment nobody calls on an idol. Every one of them makes his religion purely for Allah and promises gratitude. Then He delivers them, and they rebel in the land (compare 17:67).

Two counterweights Ibn Kathir supplies:

- The believer's exception (Muslim): the believer's affair is entirely good — prosperity meets gratitude, adversity meets patience, and both are good for him.
- **Al-Bukhari's hadith of the morning after rain:** the Prophet ﷺ reported that Allah said some of His servants had woken as believers and some as disbelievers — the one who said the rain came by Allah's grace believes in Him and disbelieves in the stars; the one who credited the rising of a star has done the reverse.

On *"Allah is swifter in planning"*: Ibn Kathir explains this as *istidraj* — He seizes gradually, granting delay until the criminal supposes he will not be punished at all, while the recording angels write down everything.

---

## 6. "Bring Us a Different Qur'an" (10:15–20)

The demand is not for evidence, it is for **an edit**: bring a Qur'an other than this one, or change it. The Prophet ﷺ is instructed to answer that it is not his to change — he is a servant who receives commands and a messenger who conveys.

Then comes the argument from his own life: *I have stayed among you a lifetime before this. Have you then no sense?* Ibn Kathir gathers the testimony of hostile witnesses to make the point:

- **Heraclius** asked Abu Sufyan — then still the head of the idolaters — whether they had ever accused Muhammad ﷺ of lying before his claim. Abu Sufyan said no. Heraclius replied that he wondered how a man who would not lie about people could lie about Allah.
- **Ja'far bin Abi Talib** told An-Najashi that they knew this Messenger's truthfulness, lineage and honesty; he had lived among them forty years before prophethood.
- **Abdullah bin Salam**, on first seeing him in Madinah: he realised at once that this was not the face of a liar. The first words he heard from him were about spreading peace, feeding others, keeping ties of kinship, and praying at night while people sleep.
- **Dimam bin Tha'labah**, who swore him by the One who raised the sky and set the mountains, and left saying he would neither add to nor subtract from what he had been told.

**The contrast case: Musaylamah.** Ibn Kathir tells the story of Amr bin al-'As — not yet Muslim, and Musaylamah's friend — visiting him. Asked what had been revealed to Muhammad ﷺ lately, Amr recited Surah Al-'Asr. Musaylamah claimed something similar had come to him, and produced a piece of doggerel addressed to a hyrax about its ears and its hollow body. Amr's verdict: *by Allah, you know that I know you are a liar.* Ibn Kathir's point is that this judgment came from an idolater who knew both men — the difference between them was clearer than the difference between mid-morning and the blackest part of the night.

**Why no requested miracle?** (10:20) They wanted As-Safa turned to gold, the mountains of Makkah replaced with gardens. Allah is able — but His way with creation is that when a demanded sign is granted and still rejected, punishment is expedited. When the Prophet ﷺ was given the choice, he chose not to have their demand met. And they had in any case already seen the moon split in two before them.

---

## 7. The Parable of This World (10:24–25)

Rain falls, the earth's produce comes up intermingled, men and cattle eat from it. The land takes on its full adornment — flowering, colourful — and the people who planted it are certain they have it in hand. **Then the command comes, by night or by day**, and it is left mown flat, as though it had not flourished yesterday.

Qatadah: as if it had never flourished, as if it had never been blessed. Ibn Kathir generalises it — this is how everything is once it has passed: as if it never existed.

The hadith he pairs with it makes the same point about a whole human life:

> The man who lived the most luxurious life on earth is dipped once into the Fire and then asked whether he ever saw any good. He answers: no.
> The man who suffered most is dipped once into the bliss of Paradise and asked whether he ever saw hardship. He answers: no.

**And then the invitation** — *Allah calls to the Abode of Peace.* Ibn Kathir records Jabir's narration of the Prophet's ﷺ dream, in which Jibril and Mika'il gave him a parable: a king built a house on his land, prepared a banquet in it, and sent a messenger inviting people. Some came, some did not. The King is Allah, the land is Islam, the house is Paradise, and the messenger is Muhammad ﷺ.

Alongside it, from Abu Ad-Darda': two angels descend each sunrise calling out to all creation except jinn and men — *come to your Lord; little and sufficient is better than much and distracting.*

---

## 8. "Al-Husna wa Ziyadah" — The Best, and More (10:26)

For the doers of good, *al-husna* (Paradise) **and something extra**.

Ibn Kathir lists the *ziyadah* first in its lesser senses — deeds multiplied tenfold to seven hundredfold and beyond, the palaces, the companions, Allah's pleasure, what no eye has seen. Then he gives the interpretation held by the overwhelming weight of the Salaf: **the increase is the honour of looking at Allah's Noble Face.**

He names the chain of those who said so — Abu Bakr, Hudhayfah, Ibn Abbas, Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, Mujahid, Ikrimah, Ata', Ad-Dahhak, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, As-Suddi, Ibn Ishaq and others — and supports it with the hadith of Suhayb (Ahmad, Muslim):

> When the people of Paradise have entered Paradise, a caller announces that Allah has a promise still to fulfil for them. They ask what could remain — has He not weighted their scales, whitened their faces, saved them from the Fire? Then the veil is removed and they see Him. Nothing they had been given was dearer to them than that.

And they will not deserve it by their deeds; they receive it by grace.

**The mirror image (10:27):** evil is repaid *with its like* and no more — Allah's justice — but disgrace covers their faces, and there is no defender to shield them. Their faces are described as though patches of the night had been laid over them (compare 3:106–107, 80:38–40).

---

## 9. The Day the Idols Disown Their Worshippers (10:28–30)

The idolaters and their objects of worship are made to stand apart, then separated — the same separation described in 36:59, 30:14 and 30:43. The "partners" then repudiate them entirely: *it was not us you were worshipping.* They call Allah as witness that they never asked for it, never commanded it, and never accepted it (compare 19:82, 2:166, 46:5–6).

And there, every soul learns exactly what it sent forward.

---

## 10. The Argument From What They Already Admit (10:31–36)

The most concentrated logical passage in the surah. Ibn Kathir treats it as a single argument: **the idolaters already concede Allah's Lordship, and that concession convicts them.**

Who provides from sky and earth? Who owns hearing and sight? Who brings the living from the dead and the dead from the living? Who manages the affair?

> *They will say: Allah.*

They say it knowingly and admit it. So — *will you not then fear Him?* If He is the Creator, Sustainer, Owner of your faculties and Judge of all affairs, then **worship follows Lordship**, and *after the truth, what is there except error?*

The verdict on their actual position (10:36): they follow nothing but *zann* — conjecture — and conjecture avails nothing at all against the truth. They did not arrive at their religion by evidence; they inherited an assumption.

---

## 11. The Challenge (10:37–40)

The Qur'an could not have been produced by anyone besides Allah. Ibn Kathir's reasoning runs on two tracks: its eloquence, clarity and precision are not human speech; and its content — principles of benefit in this world and the next — could come from no other source. It confirms the earlier Books and exposes what was altered in them.

Then: *bring a surah like it, and call whomever you can besides Allah.*

**Ibn Kathir maps this as the third stage of a descending challenge:**

| Stage | Demand | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The whole Qur'an — even if men and jinn combine | 17:88 |
| 2 | Ten fabricated surahs like it | 11:13 |
| 3 | **One surah** | 10:38, and again in 2:23–24 |

And in Al-Baqarah the challenge closes with the prediction that they never will manage it.

Why this particular miracle for this particular people? Because eloquence was the field the Arabs owned — the Mu'allaqat were their summit — and what came down to them was in a mode none of them recognised and none could match. Those who believed did so **because they knew the field well enough to know what they were looking at.** Ibn Kathir draws the same pattern twice more: Fir'awn's magicians knew sorcery well enough to recognise that Musa's staff was not sorcery; 'Isa came in an age of advanced medicine and did what no medicine could do.

> *There was never a Prophet but he was given signs by which people would recognise him; what I was given is revelation — so I hope to have the most followers among them.* (Sahih)

---

## 12. "When Is This Promise?" (10:45–54)

The deniers demand a date, which Ibn Kathir notes is a question whose answer would not benefit them anyway. The response has three parts:

1. **The Prophet ﷺ does not own the timetable** — he has no power over harm or benefit to himself except as Allah wills; he was told the Hour is coming, not when.
2. **Every community has a term**, and when it arrives it cannot be delayed or advanced by an hour (compare 63:11).
3. **Belief at the moment of impact is worthless** — *is it now?* — the same principle as 40:84–85, and the principle that will govern Fir'awn's fate later in the surah.

**The oath (10:53).** Asked whether the resurrection is real, the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to swear by his Lord that it is. Ibn Kathir points out this happens in **only three places in the entire Qur'an** — here, 34:3, and 64:7 — each time answering the same denial. And the wrongdoer on that Day would give the whole earth in gold as ransom, and it would not be taken.

---

## 13. The Qur'an as Medicine (10:57–58)

Four things at once, and Ibn Kathir defines each briefly:

- **Mawi'zah** — admonition, a restraint from shameful deeds
- **Shifa' li-ma fi as-sudur** — a cure for what is *in the chests*: suspicion, doubt, the filth of shirk
- **Hudan** — guidance
- **Rahmah** — mercy

But note the qualifier: *for the believers*. He pairs it with 17:82 — it is healing and mercy to the believers and increases the wrongdoers in nothing but loss — and 41:44.

Then the command to rejoice: **rejoice in Allah's bounty and mercy, not in what you accumulate.** Guidance and the religion of truth are better than the world's vanishing bloom.

---

## 14. Who Gets to Declare Things Halal and Haram? (10:59–60)

Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Ad-Dahhak, Qatadah and others: this was revealed against the idolaters' self-made dietary and livestock taboos — the *Bahirah*, *Sa'ibah* and *Wasilah* (compare 6:136).

**The hadith Ahmad records from Malik bin Nadlah** is the memorable one. He came to the Prophet ﷺ in shabby clothes and was asked whether he had wealth; he said he had every kind — camels, horses, sheep, slaves. He was told that if Allah gives wealth, its effect should be visible on a man. Then the Prophet ﷺ challenged him about slitting his camels' ears and declaring them forbidden to his family, and told him plainly that what Allah gave him is lawful — **Allah's forearm is stronger than his forearm, and Allah's blade sharper than his blade.**

Ibn Kathir's own comment, in his own voice, on *Allah is full of bounty to mankind*: Ibn Jarir took the bounty to be the delay of punishment; Ibn Kathir suggests instead that **the bounty is the sheer breadth of what He permitted** — He forbade only what harms them in this world or the next. So people who prohibit what He gave have narrowed their own lives. The idolaters did it by legislating for themselves; the People of the Book did it by innovating in religion.

---

## 15. The Awliya' of Allah (10:62–64)

A short passage Ibn Kathir treats as a definition, and he is careful to keep it unglamorous:

> **The Awliya' of Allah are those who believe and have taqwa.** Every pious, God-fearing person is a friend of Allah.

No fear over what lies ahead of them, no grief over what they left behind.

Ibn Jarir records from Abu Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ described servants of Allah whom **the Prophets and the martyrs will consider fortunate** — asked who they were, he said they are people who loved one another purely for Allah's sake, with no tie of money or kinship between them; their faces will be light, upon platforms of light, unafraid when others fear and ungrieving when others grieve.

**What is the "good news in this world"?** 'Ubadah bin As-Samit asked exactly that — the Hereafter's good news is obviously Paradise, but what is the worldly one? The answer: **the righteous dream** that a believer sees or that is seen about him — a part of prophethood. Ibn Kathir notes the differing numbers in the narrations (one part of forty-four, of forty-six, of seventy). Abdullah bin Amr's version adds the etiquette: a good dream should be told; a disliked one is from Shaytan to grieve him, so he should blow three times to his left, say *Allahu Akbar*, and tell nobody.

Two further candidates for the "good news" that Ibn Kathir records: the praise of people for a believer's deeds (Abu Dharr — *the good news expedited for the believer*), and **the glad tidings the angels bring at death** — the passage in 41:30–32, and Al-Bara's hadith of angels with white faces and white garments coming to the believer, his soul leaving him as easily as a drop falls from a waterskin.

---

## 16. Nuh — and the Claim That Islam Is Older Than Islam (10:71–74)

Nuh's speech is defiance without aggression: if my staying among you and reminding you of Allah's signs is unbearable to you, I rely on Allah — **so gather your plot and your partners, leave nothing unclear between us, and give me no respite.** Ibn Kathir sets it beside Hud's near-identical challenge in 11:54–55.

Then Nuh's second point: *I have asked you no wage; my reward is with Allah; and I was commanded to be among the Muslims.*

This last phrase triggers what is arguably the most striking passage of the surah's tafsir. **Ibn Kathir demonstrates that "Islam" is the religion of every prophet**, by walking through the Qur'an's own vocabulary:

| Speaker | Words |
|---|---|
| Ibrahim | *I have submitted to the Lord of all that exists* — and enjoined on his sons: die not except as Muslims (2:131–132) |
| Yusuf | Cause me to die as a Muslim (12:101) |
| Musa | If you have believed in Allah, rely on Him — if you are Muslims (10:84) |
| Fir'awn's magicians | Pour patience on us, and cause us to die as Muslims (7:126) |
| Bilqis | I submit, with Sulayman, to the Lord of all that exists (27:44) |
| The Disciples of 'Isa | We believe — bear witness that we are Muslims (5:111) |
| Muhammad ﷺ | My prayer, my sacrifice, my life and my death are for Allah… I am the first of the Muslims (6:162–163) |

And the hadith that resolves it: **the Prophets are brothers of one father and different mothers — their religion is one**, though their laws differ (compare 5:48; Ibn Abbas glosses *shir'ah wa minhaj* as a way and a Sunnah).

Also from Ibn Abbas, twice in this surah: **there were ten generations between Adam and Nuh, and all of them were upon Islam.** Shirk is not humanity's original state. It is an innovation, introduced later, and Nuh was the first messenger sent to the people of the earth because of it.

## 17. Musa and Fir'awn (10:75–92)

Ibn Kathir notes he has already covered the magicians' contest in Surah Al-A'raf, and here draws out particular details.

**The strategy backfires.** Fir'awn wanted the magicians to overwhelm the plain truth in front of a public festival crowd. The result was the exact opposite: the magicians fell prostrate and declared faith in the Lord of Musa and Harun (7:120–122). Musa deliberately let them throw first — so the people would see the full extent of what human craft could do before the truth arrived to swallow it.

**Almost nobody believed (10:83).** Al-Awfi from Ibn Abbas: the ones who believed from Fir'awn's own people — apart from the Children of Israel — were very few. He names **Fir'awn's wife, the believer who concealed his faith, Fir'awn's treasurer, and the treasurer's wife.** The Children of Israel, by contrast, believed in Musa as a body; they knew his description from their own Books.

**Tawakkul (10:84–86).** Musa's instruction to rely on Allah prompts Ibn Kathir to gather the Qur'an's habit of binding worship and reliance together (11:123, 67:29, 73:9) — and to point out that Allah commanded the believers to say it repeatedly in every prayer: *You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.*

Their du'a is unusually precise: **do not make us a fitnah for the wrongdoing people** — meaning, per Mujahid and others, *do not give them power over us*, lest they take their victory as proof they were on the truth and we on falsehood.

**Praying in secret (10:87).** Ibn Abbas: the Children of Israel told Musa they could not pray openly in front of Fir'awn's people, so they were permitted to pray in their houses — and commanded to orient those houses toward the Qiblah. Qatadah and Ad-Dahhak say the same: when they feared being killed in their gathering-places, their homes became their places of worship and the prayer became a private act.

**Musa's imprecation (10:88–89).** He asks for their wealth to be destroyed and their hearts hardened, since he was certain no good remained in them — Ibn Kathir compares Nuh's prayer in 71:26–27, and notes that Harun's role was to say *Amin*. The answer comes: *your prayer is answered* — **followed immediately by a command: so stand firm, and do not follow the path of those who do not know.** Ibn Kathir's reading of that sequence is direct: *since I have answered you, remain steadfast on My command.*

**The crossing.** Ibn Kathir narrates it with unusual richness: Fir'awn emptying his kingdom of every man of authority to pursue them; the two hosts meeting at the shore; Musa's companions saying they were surely overtaken and Musa answering *my Lord is with me, He will guide me* (26:62); the sea struck and split into **twelve paths, one for each tribe**, with the walls of water like windows so each tribe could see the others and not fear they had perished; the wind drying the seabed.

Then Fir'awn's hesitation at the edge, Jibril's mare drawing his horse in, Mika'il driving the army from behind, and the sea closing.

**Fir'awn's last-second faith is refused** (10:90–91) — *now? when you disobeyed before?* — the same law stated in 40:84–85. Ibn Kathir includes the hadith from Ibn Abbas (At-Tirmidhi: *Hasan Gharib Sahih*) in which Jibril describes stuffing black sea-mud into Fir'awn's mouth, **out of fear that mercy might reach him.**

**The body (10:92).** Ibn Abbas and others among the Salaf: some of the Children of Israel doubted he was really dead, so the sea was commanded to cast up his body whole, in its recognisable armour, onto high ground — so they could see it. Hence *badanika*: your body, the physical thing.

And per Al-Bukhari from Ibn Abbas, the day was **'Ashura'** — which the Jews of Madinah were fasting when the Prophet ﷺ arrived, and which he said the Muslims had more right to than they did.

**Afterwards (10:93):** settled in an honourable dwelling and provided with good things — and then they differed, *after knowledge came to them*. Ibn Kathir's comment is pointed: knowledge had been given precisely so there would be nothing left to dispute. He attaches the hadith of the Jews splitting into seventy-one sects, the Christians into seventy-two, and this Ummah into seventy-three, with the saved group identified as those upon what the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions were upon.

## 18. The People of Yunus — The Surah's Namesake (10:98)

*Was there any town that believed, and its faith benefited it?* The question is rhetorical and the answer is almost entirely no — messenger after messenger was mocked (36:30, 51:52, 43:23); in the vision of the nations shown to the Prophet ﷺ, some prophets passed with a handful of followers and some with none at all.

**Except the people of Yunus.**

Ibn Kathir: between Musa and Yunus there was no nation that believed in its entirety except this one — the people of **Naynawa (Nineveh), in the land of Mosul** (Qatadah, Ibn Mas'ud, Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr).

And they believed *late*. They had already seen the signs of the punishment arriving. What saved them was that Allah cast repentance into their hearts before it landed. Qatadah's account of what they did:

> They put on coarse wool. They separated every animal from its young. And they cried out to Allah for forty nights.

When Allah saw the truth in their hearts and the sincerity of their regret, He lifted the torment and gave them respite in this life.

**This is the exception that proves the rule of the whole surah.** Every other passage insists that faith at the moment of punishment is worthless — Fir'awn's especially. Yunus's people are the single case where repentance arrived just early enough. That is why the surah carries their prophet's name.

---

## 19. Faith Cannot Be Compelled (10:99–100)

*Had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed — will you then compel people until they become believers?*

Ibn Kathir answers plainly: **that is not yours to do, and you were never commanded to do it.** He assembles the Qur'an's repeated relief of this burden from the Prophet's ﷺ shoulders:

- Do not destroy yourself in grief over them (35:8)
- Guidance is not upon you — Allah guides whom He wills (2:272)
- You may kill yourself with grief because they will not believe (26:3)
- You do not guide whom you love (28:56)
- Your duty is only to convey; the reckoning is Ours (13:40)
- Remind — you are only a reminder; you are not over them a controller (88:21–22)

And no soul believes *except by Allah's leave*.

---

## 20. The Closing Commands (10:104–109)

The surah ends by narrowing to a personal instruction, delivered in the second person to the Prophet ﷺ and through him to everyone:

- **Declare the position clearly.** I do not worship what you worship; I worship Allah, the One who takes your souls.
- **Direct your face to the *hanif* religion** — which Ibn Kathir defines as making one's intention in worship purely for Allah, turning away from all association of partners.
- **Do not call on what cannot benefit or harm you** — and the theological reason is given in the next breath: if Allah touches you with harm, none can remove it but He; if He intends good for you, none can turn it back. Benefit and harm are held in one Hand only, which is precisely why worship belongs there only.
- **He is the Pardoning, the Merciful** — and Ibn Kathir insists on the full reach of that: whoever turns to Him in repentance is forgiven **whatever the sin, even shirk itself**, if he repents from it.
- **Guidance is self-directed.** Whoever is guided is guided for himself; whoever strays, strays against himself. *And I am not a guardian over you.*
- **And finally, the two-word instruction that closes the surah:** follow what is revealed to you, **and be patient** — bear the opposition — until Allah judges. And He is the best of judges.

## The Thread

Read as a whole, Yunus makes one move over and over: it takes something the objector already concedes and shows him what it obliges. You already say Allah provides — then why worship what does not? You already know this man never lied for forty years — then why call him a liar now? You already call on Allah alone when the ship is going down — then why call on others on dry land? You already admit the world withers like a harvest — then why be *satisfied* with it?
