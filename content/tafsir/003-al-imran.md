## 1. Context: Why This Surah Was Sent Down

Ibn Kathir opens with a historical anchor. Al-'Imran is Madinan, and the evidence is that **the first eighty-three ayat were revealed concerning the Christian delegation from Najran** (in Yemen), who came to Madinah in the ninth year of Hijrah to argue that 'Isa was divine and the son of God. The whole opening movement of the surah — the nature of revelation, the story of Maryam and 'Isa, the challenge of the *Mubahalah* — is a sustained, reasoned answer to that delegation.

The second great block of the surah (from 3:121 onward) turns to a different kind of test: **the battle of Uhud**, fought in Shawwal of the third year of Hijrah, where seventy Muslims were killed and the Prophet ﷺ himself was wounded. Where the first half argues with the People of the Book about *truth*, the second half teaches the believers about *defeat* — how to read it, survive it, and be purified by it.

The surah's virtues were covered at the start of Al-Baqarah, since the two are paired in the hadith literature (*Az-Zahrawan*, "the two luminous ones").

## 3. The Book: Muhkam and Mutashabih (3:1–9)

After *Alif-Lam-Mim*, Allah names Himself **Al-Hayy Al-Qayyum** — the Ever-Living, the Sustainer. Ibn Kathir notes that the hadith reports **Allah's Greatest Name** to be contained in these two ayat and in Ayat al-Kursi.

He then makes an argument that will run through the whole surah: *He it is Who shapes you in the wombs as He wills* (3:6). If 'Isa was formed in a womb, stage after stage, **he is a creature like every other creature** — and creatures are not divine.

**The heart of this passage is 3:7.** The Qur'an contains:

- **Muhkamat** — verses entirely clear, "the foundations of the Book": what abrogates, what is lawful and forbidden, the limits and obligations. They cannot be twisted, and so they stand as unanswerable proof.
- **Mutashabihat** — verses not entirely clear to everyone: they carry several possible meanings, including parables, oaths, and things to be believed but not acted upon.

**The rule of interpretation is one sentence:** whoever refers the *mutashabih* back to the *muhkam* has found guidance; whoever does the reverse has lost it.

Ibn Kathir gives the Christian argument as his worked example: they cite that 'Isa is a *Word* from Allah and a *Spirit* from Him — the ambiguous — while ignoring the explicit: *He was nothing but a servant on whom We bestowed favor* (43:59), and *the likeness of 'Isa before Allah is the likeness of Adam* (3:59).

**'A'ishah's hadith** (in both Sahihs): the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse and then warned that when you see people arguing over the unclear parts of the Qur'an, **those are the ones Allah named — so beware of them.**

**On "none knows its Ta'wil except Allah":** Ibn Kathir presents the disagreement honestly, and it turns on where you stop reciting. *Ta'wil* has two senses in the Qur'an:

1. **The true reality of a thing** (as in 7:53, the reality of the Resurrection). On this sense, you *pause* at "except Allah" — only He knows how things truly are.
2. **Explanation or exposition** (as in 12:36). On this sense, you *continue*: "…except Allah **and those firmly grounded in knowledge**", since the scholars understand what they were addressed with, even if the ultimate reality is hidden from them.

Ibn 'Abbas — for whom the Prophet ﷺ prayed, *"O Allah, give him understanding in the religion and teach him the interpretation"* — placed himself among those grounded in knowledge. Ibn Kathir cites Ibn 'Abbas's fourfold division: Tafsir the Arabs know from their language; Tafsir no one is excused for not knowing; Tafsir known to scholars; and Tafsir known only to Allah.

A beautiful definition worth keeping — **Nafi' bin Yazid** on who is "firmly grounded in knowledge": *those who are humble for Allah's sake, seek His pleasure, and neither exaggerate about those above them nor belittle those beneath them.*

The passage closes with their prayer (3:8), which the Prophet ﷺ used to recite after his own supplication *"O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion"*: **Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us.** Note the sequence — a discussion of how people go astray through interpretation ends not with a rule but with a plea for protection.

## 4. What Avails, and What Does Not (3:10–20)

**Wealth and children will not save anyone** (3:10). They will be the fuel of the Fire, as the people of Fir'awn were before them. *Da'b* — in the phrase "like the *Da'b* of the people of Fir'awn" — means their practice, habit, custom; all these meanings hold.

**Badr as evidence (3:13).** After Badr, the Prophet ﷺ gathered the Jews in the marketplace of Banu Qaynuqa' and warned them. The sign was there for anyone who would read it: two armies met, one fighting for Allah and one in disbelief, and *"they saw them with their own eyes twice their number."* Ibn Kathir preserves the layered reading — before the clash, each side was made to see the other as *twice* its number (so the Muslims would turn to Allah, and the idolators would despair), and then at the moment of engagement each was made to look *fewer* in the other's eyes, so both would commit to battle and Allah's decree would come to pass (8:44, 8:42).

**Beautified for men is the love of what they covet (3:14).** Ibn Kathir works through the list rather than merely condemning it — the point is that each of these is neutral in itself and takes its ruling from the intention behind it:

- **Women** are named first because that test is the most tempting: *"I have not left behind a test more tempting to men than women."* But marriage for chastity and children is encouraged; the Prophet ﷺ said the best of this Ummah's men are those with the most wives, that the best delight of this life is a righteous wife, and that he was made to love women and perfume, with the comfort of his eye in prayer.
- **Children** — a temptation when sought for boasting; praiseworthy when sought to increase the worshippers of Allah.
- **Wealth** — blameworthy when sought to dominate the weak; praiseworthy when spent in obedience and kindness to kin. (A *Qintar* is simply a very large sum; Abu Hurayrah's estimate is that each of its units is better than everything between heaven and earth.)
- **Horses** — three types: kept for Allah's cause (rewarded), kept for arrogance and enmity (a burden), kept for lawful need while fulfilling Allah's right in them (a shield of sufficiency). *Musawwamah* means branded and beautiful — or, per Makhul, marked with a white face or white feet. Every Arabian horse is given two supplications at dawn: that it be made beloved to its owner.

Then the pivot: *Say — shall I inform you of what is better than all that?* Gardens, permanence, purified mates, and **the pleasure of Allah**, which is greater than the eternal delight itself (9:72).

**The Muttaqin described (3:17):** the patient, the truthful, the obedient, those who spend, and **those who seek forgiveness in the last hours of the night.** Ya'qub, when he promised to seek forgiveness for his sons, waited for the last part of the night. And every night, in the final third, the Lord descends to the lowest heaven asking: *Is there anyone asking, so I may give? Anyone calling, so I may answer? Anyone seeking forgiveness, so I may forgive?* Ibn 'Umar would ask his servant Nafi' whether the last part of the night had come, and then supplicate until dawn.

**The testimony of Tawhid (3:18).** Allah bears witness, then the angels, then **those who have knowledge** — Ibn Kathir stops here to note that this placement is an enormous statement of the rank of scholars.

**The religion with Allah is Islam (3:19–20).** Ibn Kathir's definition is precise: Islam means following every Messenger up to Muhammad ﷺ, who closed the commission — so after his sending, no other path is accepted. And the People of the Book did not differ out of ignorance but *out of rivalry, after knowledge had come to them*: envy and mutual hatred made them defy each other even where the other was right.

The Prophet's mission is universal — he wrote to the kings of the earth, Arab and non-Arab, and said: *"A Prophet used to be sent to his people; I was sent to all mankind."*

## 5. Sovereignty, Loyalty, and the Test of Love (3:21–32)

**On killing the Prophets and those who enjoin justice (3:21):** this, says Ibn Kathir, is arrogance in its purest form — and he quotes the Prophet's definition: **arrogance is rejecting the truth and looking down on people.** Because they rejected truth and despised creation, they were repaid with humiliation in this life and torment in the next.

**"The Fire shall not touch us but for a number of days" (3:24)** — the self-issued license. What emboldens people to defy the truth is a fiction they invented about their own safety, for which Allah gave them no authority.

**The du'a of sovereignty (3:26–27)** — *Say: O Allah, Owner of all sovereignty, You give power to whom You will and take it from whom You will…* Ibn Kathir reads this as a **teaching in gratitude**, revealed in the context of Prophethood being transferred from the Children of Israel to the unlettered Arab Prophet of Makkah. It is the direct answer to those who said, *"Why was this Qur'an not sent down to some great man of the two towns?"* — to which Allah replied, *Is it they who apportion the mercy of your Lord?* (43:31–32). Allah gives it to whom He wills, and **Allah knows best where to place His Message** (6:124).

The imagery of the following verse is entirely everyday: night entering day and day entering night across the turning seasons; the seed from the plant and the plant from the seed; the date from its stone and the stone from the date; the chicken from the egg and the egg from the chicken; **and the believer from the disbeliever, and the disbeliever from the believer.**

**Alliance with disbelievers (3:28).** The prohibition is on taking them as intimate allies and protectors *instead of* the believers, and Ibn Kathir gathers the parallel verses (60:1, 4:144, 5:51, 8:73). The exception is explicit and narrow: **unless you fear a danger from them.** In that case outward friendliness is permitted, **never inward loyalty.** Al-Bukhari records Abu Ad-Darda': *we smile in the faces of some people while our hearts curse them.* Al-Hasan: this allowance stands until the Day of Resurrection.

**The test of love (3:31)** — *If you love Allah, follow me, and Allah will love you.* Al-Hasan Al-Basri and others called this **the Ayah of the Test**: people claimed to love Allah, so Allah tested the claim. Ibn Kathir's comment is the sharpest line in the passage: **you will earn far more than what you sought — you sought to love Him, and He will love you.** And the standard is action: *"Whoever does a deed that is not in accordance with our matter, it is rejected."* To turn from the Messenger's way is not a lesser love; it is Kufr.

## 6. The House of 'Imran (3:33–63)

This is the narrative heart of the surah, and Ibn Kathir tells it as a continuous chain of chosen households — Adam, Nuh, the family of Ibrahim, the family of 'Imran — *offspring one of the other*.

### Hannah's vow and Maryam's birth (3:35–37)

'Imran's wife is **Hannah bint Faqudh**. Ibn Ishaq relates that she was childless, and one day watched a bird feeding its chick; the longing moved her to supplicate, and she conceived. She vowed the child to the service of Bayt Al-Maqdis — *"I have vowed to You what is in my womb."*

She had expected a boy. *"My Lord, I have given birth to a female"* — and Allah's parenthesis, **"and Allah knew better what she bore"**, hangs over the whole story. Her comment, *"the male is not like the female,"* is about physical strength and the ability to serve the Masjid — not about worth. The child she thought was the lesser gift became the woman chosen above the women of the worlds.

Several rulings and gems from this passage:

- **Naming a child on the day of birth is permitted** — *"I have named her Maryam"* — as the Prophet ﷺ did with his son Ibrahim, and with Anas's newborn brother 'Abdullah, whose mouth he touched with a chewed date. This does not conflict with the hadith of the 'Aqiqah on the seventh day.
- **Her final words are a prayer of protection:** *I seek refuge with You for her and her offspring from Shaytan the outcast* — and it was **answered**. The Prophet ﷺ said every newborn is touched by Shaytan at birth and cries from that touch, **except Maryam and her son**, and Abu Hurayrah recited this very verse as the reason.

**Zakariyya's guardianship (3:37).** Allah "made her grow in a good manner" — her conduct, her manner, her acceptance among people, and her company of the righteous. Zakariyya was her guardian, and the husband of her maternal aunt (hence the Prophet's ﷺ ruling giving Hamzah's daughter to her maternal aunt: **"the maternal aunt is like the mother"**).

**The provision in the Mihrab.** The Salaf are unanimous on the miracle: he would find with her **summer fruit in winter and winter fruit in summer**. He asked where it came from. Her answer is the theological key to the whole surah: *"This is from Allah. Verily, Allah provides for whom He wills without limit."*

### Zakariyya and Yahya (3:38–41)

Seeing that provision, Zakariyya wanted a son — though his bones were feeble, his head gray, and his wife barren. Ibn Kathir underlines that he called on Allah **in secret**.

The angels answered while he stood praying: glad tidings of **Yahya** — named, said Qatadah, because Allah gave his life to faith (*yahya*, "he lives"). Four descriptions:

- **Believing in a Word from Allah** — meaning believing in 'Isa son of Maryam.
- **Sayyid** — the Salaf gave a spread of meanings: wise; noble, wise and pious; the scholar and jurist; noble in conduct and piety; one not overcome by anger; honored by Allah.
- **Hasur** — chaste. Ibn Kathir corrects a common misreading: **it does not mean he abstained from women**, but that he was immune to unlawful relations. Zakariyya had asked for offspring who would themselves have offspring.
- **A Prophet from among the righteous** — Ibn Kathir notes this is better news than the birth itself.

The sign given was silence: three days speaking only by gesture, **though he had no physical defect** (19:10) — and in that silence, a command to remember Allah abundantly.

### The glad tidings to Maryam (3:42–51)

*Allah has chosen you, purified you, and chosen you above the women of the worlds.* Ibn Kathir gathers the hadiths: the best woman of her time was Maryam, and of the Prophet's time, Khadijah; and **many men reached perfection, but among women only Maryam and Asiyah the wife of Fir'awn**.

**The lottery of the pens (3:44).** Ibn Kathir dwells here because Allah does — *You were not with them when they cast lots with their pens.* Her mother brought her to the rabbis of the descendants of Harun, who kept Bayt Al-Maqdis, saying she could not take her home since a menstruating woman may not enter the Masjid. Each wanted her, since she was the daughter of their Imam. Zakariyya pressed his claim (her maternal aunt was his wife). So they cast their pens — the very pens with which they wrote the Tawrah — into the river Jordan, agreeing that whoever's pen stayed afloat would take her. **Every pen was taken under except Zakariyya's, which stayed floating in place.**

**'Isa announced (3:45–47).** *Al-Masih* — Ibn Kathir's explanation is that he was called this because when he **touched** (*mash*) the afflicted, they were healed by Allah's leave. He is named *son of Maryam* because he had no father; he is honored in this world and the next and among those brought near.

Maryam's question — *how, when no man has touched me* — is answered with a change of verb that Ibn Kathir treats as deliberate: to Zakariyya, Allah said *"Thus Allah **does** what He wills"*; to Maryam, *"Thus Allah **creates** what He wills"* — **using "create" precisely to close the door on any thought of divinity.** What He decrees comes to be at the word *Be*, faster than the blink of an eye (54:50).

**Miracles matched to their age (3:49).** This is one of the great passages of the tafsir. Allah sends each Prophet with a sign calibrated to what his people most prized:

- **Musa** came when magic was the height of skill, and brought what bewildered every magician — so the magicians, who understood the craft best, were the first to believe.
- **'Isa** came when medicine and natural knowledge were advancing — so he healed the man born blind and the leper and raised the dead. *What physician can breathe life into clay, or call back a man from the grave?*
- **Muhammad ﷺ** came among the most eloquent people and the most accomplished poets on earth — and brought a Book which, if mankind and jinn combined to imitate ten chapters of it, or one, they would fail.

'Isa also confirmed the Tawrah while **making lawful part of what had been forbidden** — a detail Ibn Kathir will use later as a proof that abrogation was always part of the divine law.

### The disciples, the plot, and the raising (3:52–58)

*"Who will be my helpers to Allah?"* Ibn Kathir places this next to the Prophet ﷺ in the Hajj seasons before Hijrah, asking who would shelter him so he could deliver his Lord's speech, until the Ansar answered. **Hawari** in Arabic means support. Every Prophet has a *Hawari*; the Prophet ﷺ said Az-Zubayr was his.

**The plot.** They accused 'Isa before the disbelieving king of misguiding people, dividing families and turning men from obedience — and added the slander about his birth. The king sent men to seize him. Allah raised him from the house, cast his likeness on another man present, and in the darkness they took that man, humiliated and crucified him and set thorns on his head — **while thinking they had achieved their aim** (4:157–158).

**"I will take you"** — Ibn Kathir's preferred reading is *while you sleep*, comparing 6:60 and 39:42, and Al-Hasan's statement that Allah raised him while asleep. He connects this to 'Isa's return before the Day of Resurrection, when every one of the People of the Book will believe in him, and he will abolish the Jizyah and accept only Islam.

**"And I will make those who follow you superior…"** Ibn Kathir traces what happened after the raising: the followers split — some held to the truth that he was a servant and Messenger and the son of Allah's female servant; some claimed he was the son of God; some that he was God; some that he was one of three. He then gives his account of Constantine as the point at which the religion of 'Isa was overwritten with the religion of the emperor — feast, fast, direction of prayer and law altered, and churches built in their thousands. **This is Ibn Kathir's own historical polemic, presented as such**, and its function in the tafsir is to explain how a message of pure Tawhid became a trinitarian creed.

### Adam, and the challenge (3:59–63)

**The argument in one sentence:** *The likeness of 'Isa before Allah is the likeness of Adam — He created him from dust, then said "Be", and he was.* If being fatherless makes one divine, then Adam — who had **neither** father nor mother — has by far the stronger claim. Since that conclusion is plainly false for Adam, it is more plainly false for 'Isa. Ibn Kathir adds the elegant completion: Allah displayed His power in four ways — Adam from neither male nor female, Hawwa' from a male without a female, **'Isa from a female without a male**, and the rest of humanity from both.

**The Mubahalah (3:61).** *Come, let us call our sons and your sons, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves, then invoke the curse of Allah upon the liars.*

Ibn Ishaq's account: sixty horsemen came from Najran, fourteen of them chiefs, three of these the decision-makers — **Al-'Aqib** ('Abdul-Masih), their leader and counsellor; **As-Sayyid** (Al-Ayham), their scholar and travel leader; and **Abu Harithah bin 'Alqamah**, their patriarch, an Arab of Bakr bin Wa'il whom the Romans had honored with churches and wealth. Ibn Kathir notes pointedly that **Abu Harithah knew the Prophet's ﷺ description from his own books, but his position among the Christians kept him where he was.**

They disagreed even among themselves — some saying 'Isa was God, some the son of God, some one of three. When the Prophet ﷺ called them to the Mubahalah, they asked for time and consulted Al-'Aqib, who told them plainly: *you know he is a Prophet, and no people ever entered a mubahalah with a Prophet and had their old men survive or their young men grow up.* They withdrew and asked instead for a trustworthy man to arbitrate their financial disputes.

Every Companion hoped to be chosen. The Prophet ﷺ said: **"Stand, Abu 'Ubaydah bin Al-Jarrah" — and then, "This is the trustee of this Ummah."**

## 7. The Common Word and the Religion of Ibrahim (3:64–99)

**3:64 — *Come to a word that is just between us and you*.** Ibn Kathir explains that "word" here means a whole sentence, and Allah then spells it out: that we worship none but Allah, associate nothing with Him, **and that none of us take others as lords besides Allah** — which Ibn Jurayj glosses simply as: *that we do not obey one another in disobedience to Allah.* No statue, cross, idol, Taghut or fire — this is the message of every Messenger (21:25, 16:36).

This is the verse the Prophet ﷺ wrote in his **letter to Heraclius**. Ibn Kathir pauses on a genuine chronological difficulty: Az-Zuhri says the people of Najran were the first to pay the Jizyah, yet the Jizyah verse (9:29) came after the conquest of Makkah, while the letter to Heraclius came before. His resolution: what Najran paid was in lieu of the Mubahalah, not Jizyah; and it is possible the Prophet ﷺ wrote these words before the verse was revealed, and **the Qur'an then came down agreeing with them word for word** — as happened repeatedly with 'Umar's statements (the captives of Badr, the Hijab, the station of Ibrahim, 66:5).

**Ibrahim was neither Jew nor Christian (3:65–68).** The rabbis of the Jews and the Christians of Najran each claimed him in the Prophet's ﷺ presence. The refutation is chronological and devastating: **the Tawrah and the Injil were not revealed until after him.** Ibn Kathir generalizes the rebuke — they argued about what they *did* know (their own law), which would at least have been defensible; instead they argued about what they had no knowledge of at all. *It is Allah Who knows, and you know not.*

Who then has the best claim to Ibrahim? **Those who followed him, and this Prophet, and those who believe.** The Prophet ﷺ said every Prophet has a *Wali* among the Prophets, and his is **Ibrahim**.

**The plots described (3:69–74).** Two are named:

1. **The morning-and-evening scheme** — a group would publicly believe at dawn, praying with the Muslims, and publicly reject at day's end, so that observers would conclude they had discovered a flaw in Islam after examining it from the inside.
2. **The knowledge embargo** — *believe no one except one who follows your religion* — that is, do not let the Muslims learn what is in your books about the description of the Prophet ﷺ, lest they use it against you.

Allah's reply to both cuts through: **guidance is Allah's guidance**, and *all bounty is in the Hand of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills.* Knowledge and understanding are not property to be withheld; they are gifts to be given.

**The question of trust (3:75–77).** Ibn Kathir reads the verse as a fair, discriminating judgment: **some** among them will return a fortune entrusted to them, and **some** will not return a single coin unless you stand over them demanding it. What licensed the second type was a doctrine — *"there is no blame on us regarding the unlettered ones"* — and Allah's response is flat: **they invent a lie against Allah while they know it.**

Ibn 'Abbas applied this immediately and practically: asked by a man whether it was fine to take chickens and sheep from the *Ahl Adh-Dhimmah* during a campaign, and told "we say there's no sin in it," he replied: **that is exactly what the People of the Book said.** If they pay the Jizyah, their property is not lawful to you except with their consent.

**Selling the covenant of Allah for a small price (3:77)** draws a cluster of hadiths, all on the same theme — that trading truth for gain forfeits the Hereafter entirely: three whom Allah will not speak to, look at, or purify (the one who trails his garment in arrogance, the one who swears falsely to sell merchandise, the one who gives charity then reminds people of it); and in another narration (one who withholds surplus water from a traveler, one who swears falsely after 'Asr to close a sale, one who pledges allegiance to a ruler only for what he receives). The verse itself was revealed in a dispute over land: Al-Ash'ath had no evidence, the Jewish man was about to swear falsely, and this came down. In another version, Imru' Al-Qays, hearing that forfeiting his claim would gain him Paradise, gave up the entire disputed land.

**Distorting the Book (3:78).** Ibn Kathir preserves a genuine disagreement about *what* was corrupted. Al-Bukhari reports from Ibn 'Abbas that they altered and added but could not remove Allah's words. **Wahb bin Munabbih** held that the Tawrah and Injil remain as revealed, and that people misguide others by additions and false interpretation using books they wrote themselves. Ibn Kathir accepts a distinction: **if Wahb meant the books in circulation, they have certainly been altered** (he adds that the Arabic translations of his day were full of error and misunderstanding); **if he meant Allah's Books as they are with Him, those are preserved and never changed.**

**No Prophet ever called people to worship him (3:79–80).** If this is not permitted to a Prophet, it is permitted to no one else. The rebuke targets the ignorant rabbis, priests and teachers of misguidance — not the Messengers and the sincere scholars who act on their knowledge. Instead the Prophet says: **be *Rabbaniyyun*** — which Ibn 'Abbas glossed as wise, learned and forbearing. Ad-Dahhak's remark on *"because you are teaching the Book and studying it"*: **whoever learns the Qur'an deserves to become a Faqih.**

**The covenant of the Prophets (3:81).** *Isr* means the ratified covenant. Allah took from every Prophet a binding pledge: whatever Book and Wisdom I give you, if a Messenger then comes confirming what is with you, **you must believe in him and support him.** 'Ali and Ibn 'Abbas: Allah never sent a Prophet without taking his pledge that if Muhammad ﷺ were sent in his lifetime, he would believe in and support him — and commanded each Prophet to take the same pledge from his nation. Tawus, Al-Hasan and Qatadah put it as a pledge that the Prophets would believe in one another, which Ibn Kathir sees as compatible.

The consequence: **Muhammad ﷺ is the Imam whom any Prophet alive in his time would be obliged to follow.** Hence he led the Prophets in prayer in Bayt Al-Maqdis on the night of Isra'; hence *Al-Maqam Al-Mahmud*, the station of intercession that the mighty Messengers will decline and he alone will assume.

**Only Islam is accepted (3:83–85).** Everything in the heavens and the earth has submitted, *willingly or unwillingly* — the believer submits in heart and body; the disbeliever submits in body alone, being under a power he cannot resist. And the Muslim creed is stated at its widest: we believe in what was revealed to us **and** to Ibrahim, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub, the tribes, Musa, 'Isa, and all the Prophets, making no distinction between them. Ibn Kathir's summary: **faithful Muslims believe in every Prophet Allah sent and every Book He revealed, and disbelieve in none of them.**

**Apostasy and the door left open (3:86–91).** The passage is severe — the curse of Allah, the angels and all mankind, with the torment neither lightened nor delayed — and then: ***except those who repent afterwards and do righteous deeds.*** Ibn Kathir records the occasion: an Ansari man apostatized, joined the idolators, then regretted it and sent to ask whether repentance was possible for him. This passage came down, and he returned to Islam.

But repentance has a horizon. Those who die as disbelievers will not be ransomed **by the earth's fill of gold** — Ibn Kathir stretches the image over all its mountains, sands, valleys and seas. Asked whether 'Abdullah bin Jud'an's famous generosity would benefit him, the Prophet ﷺ said no: **not one day of his life did he say, "My Lord, forgive me on the Day of Judgment."** And the hadith of the man in the Fire who would give the earth's fill of gold to escape, and is told: *I asked you for something easier than that, and you did not do it.*

**Al-Birr (3:92): *You will never attain Al-Birr until you spend of what you love.*** 'Amr bin Maymun said *Al-Birr* here means Paradise itself. The story is Abu Talhah's: the most beloved of his properties was the garden of **Bayruha'**, facing the Masjid, whose sweet water the Prophet ﷺ used to drink. When this verse came down he gave it away at once — and the Prophet ﷺ, twice repeating *"that is profitable property"*, directed him to give it to his relatives. 'Umar did the same with his share of Khaybar, and was told: **retain the land and give its fruits in Allah's cause.**

**The food question and the case for abrogation (3:93–95).** A delegation of Jews came testing the Prophet ﷺ with four questions only a Prophet would know — what food Israil forbade himself; the roles of the two discharges in determining a child's sex; the condition of the unlettered Prophet in sleep; and who his angelic *Wali* was. He answered all four, taking their oath first that they would follow him if he answered truly, and they confirmed each answer under oath. **Then, when he named Jibril, they refused** — and 2:97 was revealed about them.

Ibn Kathir gives two reasons this passage sits where it does:

1. It follows *"you will never attain Al-Birr…"* — Israil forbade himself the food and drink he loved most (camel meat and camel milk) for Allah's sake, which was permitted in his law. **What we are asked to do is spend from what we love, not forbid ourselves what Allah has permitted.**
2. It opens the **argument from abrogation**. The Jews denied that laws can be abrogated — but their own Tawrah records it repeatedly: after the ark, Nuh was permitted all animal meat; Israil then forbade camel meat and milk to himself and his children followed him, and the Tawrah later prohibited it outright along with more; Adam's sons married his daughters, later forbidden; Ibrahim's law permitted taking a female servant alongside a wife, as with Hajar and Sarah, later forbidden; Ya'qub married two sisters at once, later forbidden. **Abrogation is in their own book.** So why refuse it when it comes through 'Isa, and through Muhammad ﷺ?

**The First House (3:96–97).** *The first House appointed for mankind is the one at Bakkah.* Ibn Kathir's rebuke is quiet and pointed: **the Jews and Christians claim Ibrahim's religion, yet do not make Hajj to the House that Ibrahim built** by Allah's command and to which he called the people.

Abu Dharr asked which Masjid was built first: **Al-Masjid Al-Haram**; then Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa; **forty years between them** — and then the Prophet's ﷺ addition, that wherever prayer becomes due, pray, *for the whole earth was made a Masjid.*

**Bakkah** — from *Buka'*, either because it brings the tyrannical and arrogant to weeping and humility, or because people crowd and press around it. Among Makkah's other names: Al-Bayt Al-'Atiq, Al-Bayt Al-Haram, Al-Balad Al-Amin, Umm Rahm, Umm Al-Qura.

**The Maqam of Ibrahim** is where he stood to raise the walls higher while Isma'il handed him stones; Mujahid says the impression of his feet remains on it as a clear sign. It sat beside the House until **'Umar moved it eastward** so that those in Tawaf would not disturb those praying at it.

**The sanctuary.** Al-Hasan Al-Basri describes the pre-Islamic custom: a murderer would put wool around his neck and enter the Haram, and even the son of the man he had killed would not touch him until he left. The Prophet's ﷺ declaration on the day of the conquest: **Allah made this town sacred the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it is sacred by His decree until the Day of Resurrection.** Fighting in it was made lawful for him for a few hours of that one day and never again; its thorns are not uprooted, its game not hunted, its lost property not picked up except to announce it, its trees not cut — except, at Al-'Abbas's request, the lemongrass. Abu Shurayh's rule for anyone citing the conquest as precedent: **Allah permitted His Messenger and did not permit you.** And the Prophet's ﷺ farewell to Makkah at Al-Hazwarah: *you are the best of Allah's land and the most beloved of it to Allah, and had I not been driven out of you, I would not have left.*

**Hajj (3:97).** Obligatory once in a lifetime for whoever is able, by text and consensus. When a man asked whether it was every year, the Prophet ﷺ stayed silent until the third asking, then: **had I said yes, it would have become obligatory and you could not have borne it** — followed by the general principle, *leave me as I leave you; those before you were destroyed by their many questions and their disputing with their Prophets; what I command you, do of it what you can.* "Able to undertake the journey" is explained in the hadith as **provision and a mount.** And the closing rebuke — *whoever disbelieves, Allah is not in need of the worlds* — was read by Ibn 'Abbas and Mujahid as referring to **denying the obligation**, while 'Umar's own severe words are preserved: whoever could perform Hajj and did not, it makes no difference whether he dies a Jew or a Christian.

## 8. The Community That Holds Together (3:100–120)

**The Rope of Allah (3:103).** *Hold fast, all of you together, to the Rope of Allah, and do not be divided.* The Rope is Allah's covenant. The command has two halves — **cling** and **do not separate** — and Ibn Kathir treats the second as an order to stick to the *Jama'ah*.

The verse's occasion is **the Aws and the Khazraj**: two tribes locked in generations of war, hatred and vengeance, whom Islam made brothers. *If you had spent all that is on earth you could not have united their hearts, but Allah united them* (8:63). The Prophet ﷺ reminded the Ansar of exactly this when the spoils of Hunayn were divided and some were unhappy: **Were you not misguided, and Allah guided you through me? Divided, and He united you around me? Poor, and He enriched you through me?**

The hadith Ibn Kathir cites here is worth memorizing whole — three things please Allah and three displease Him. Pleasing: **that you worship Him alone, that you hold to His Rope together and not divide, and that you give sincere counsel to whoever is appointed over you.** Displeasing: *"it was said" and "so-and-so said"* (idle rumor-trading), excessive questioning, and wasting wealth.

**The calling community (3:104).** *Let there arise from among you a group calling to good, enjoining right and forbidding wrong.* Ibn Kathir's balance: this is a **communal obligation** — there must be a body of people dedicated to it — **and simultaneously an individual one, each according to his capacity.** Hence the hadith of the three levels: change it with your hand; if not, your tongue; if not, your heart, **and that is the weakest faith** — with the additional narration that beyond that there is not a mustard seed's weight of faith. And the warning: *you will enjoin good and forbid evil, or Allah will send a punishment upon you, and then you will supplicate and not be answered.*

**Division (3:105–107)** is what happened to those before, who split after clear proofs came, having abandoned this very duty. On the Day when faces whiten and blacken, Ibn 'Abbas said the whitened faces are those of **the people of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah**, and the darkened are those of innovation and division.

**"You are the best nation raised up for mankind" (3:110).** Ibn Kathir refuses to let this be read as an ethnic or automatic honor, and his evidence is textual:

- Abu Hurayrah's gloss, recorded by Al-Bukhari: **the best of people *for* the people** — you bring them in chains and they end up embracing Islam. The excellence is in what the Ummah does *for* others.
- The praise is **immediately conditioned** by the rest of the verse: *you enjoin right, forbid wrong, and believe in Allah.* Whoever of this Ummah has these qualities has a share in the praise; whoever does not is exactly like the People of the Book whom Allah condemned because *they did not forbid one another from the evil they committed* (5:79).
- **'Umar**, seeing people rushing during Hajj, recited this verse and said: *whoever wishes to be of this Ummah, let him fulfill the condition Allah set in it.*

Ibn Kathir then collects the hadiths of this Ummah's standing — seventy thousand entering Paradise with faces like the full moon (and 'Ukkashah beating the other man to the request); the hope that it will be a quarter, a third, then half of Paradise's people; that of a hundred and twenty rows, this Ummah takes eighty; and *"we are the last to come but the foremost on the Day of Resurrection"*, with Friday given to us and Saturday and Sunday coming after.

**"Not all of them are alike" (3:113–115).** Ibn Kathir is careful to mark this as the necessary counterweight to everything preceding: it was revealed about the People of the Book **who embraced Islam** — 'Abdullah bin Salam, Asad bin 'Ubayd, Tha'labah bin Sa'yah, Usayd bin Sa'yah and others. Among them is a party standing for the right, reciting Allah's verses in the watches of the night, prostrating; **and whatever good they do will not be denied them.**

**The parable of the frigid wind (3:117).** What the disbelievers spend is like a wind of *Sir* — bitter cold, or cold with snow, or (per one report) fire — striking a crop **just as it is ready to be harvested** and burning it. The tragedy is the timing: the loss lands at the moment of maximum need. Ibn Kathir reconciles the readings simply: intense frost burns produce exactly as fire does.

**The Bitanah (3:118).** *Bitanah* is the inner lining of a garment — the confidant with access to your most private affairs. The prohibition is on taking as intimate advisors those outside the community of faith, and the verse describes them precisely: they spare no effort to corrupt you; hatred has already shown in their speech, **and what their breasts conceal is worse.** 'Umar, told of a skilled young Christian scribe from Hirah who could be appointed, replied: **"Then I would have taken an advisor from among the disbelievers."**

*You love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all the Scriptures* — Ibn 'Abbas's reading is that the asymmetry is the point: **you believe in your Book and theirs and what came before, while they disbelieve in yours.** And the reply to their private rage: *Die in your rage.*

The passage closes with the safeguard the believers are actually given, which is not a strategy but a discipline: ***if you are patient and have Taqwa, their plotting will not harm you at all.***

## 9. Uhud (3:121–175)

Ibn Kathir devotes an enormous portion of the surah's tafsir to this battle, because the Qur'an does.

### The setting (3:121–123)

After Badr, the Quraysh spent the profits of Abu Sufyan's rescued caravan on war, mobilized three thousand men with the Ahabish tribes, and camped near Uhud facing Madinah. The Prophet ﷺ prayed Jumu'ah, performed a funeral prayer, then **consulted** the Muslims: fortify inside the city, or march out?

**'Abdullah bin Ubayy** advised staying — a siege would cost the attackers dearly, the men would fight in the streets and the women and children could throw stones from the roofs. But those who had missed Badr pressed to go out. The Prophet ﷺ went in, put on his armor, and came out. Seeing this, the Companions regretted pressuring him and offered to stay — and he said: **it is not for a Prophet to put on his armor and then lay it down before Allah decides.**

He marched with a thousand. At Shawt, **'Abdullah bin Ubayy turned back with a third of the army**, claiming his advice had been ignored. Seven hundred remained. The Prophet ﷺ camped with Mount Uhud at their backs, set **fifty archers under 'Abdullah bin Jubayr** on the hill, and gave them an order with no exceptions in it: *keep the horsemen off us; hold your position whether we win or lose — **even if you see us being carried off by birds, do not leave your places.***

Allah recalls first the two tribes — **Banu Harithah and Banu Salamah** — who nearly lost heart. Jabir's comment on the verse revealed about them is remarkable: *I would not wish it had not been revealed*, because of what Allah said next: ***but Allah was their protector.***

Then the reminder of Badr — three hundred and thirteen men, two horses, seventy camels, against nine hundred to a thousand fully equipped — *when you were a weak little force.* **The lesson is that victory never came from numbers**, which is why Allah pairs it elsewhere with Hunayn, *when your great numbers pleased you and availed you nothing* (9:25).

### The angels (3:124–129)

Ibn Kathir lays out both scholarly positions fairly.

**First view:** the promise of three thousand and then five thousand refers to **Badr**. Ash-Sha'bi's account: the Muslims heard that Kurz bin Jabir was coming to reinforce the idolators, and this promise came as reassurance; when Kurz heard of the idolators' defeat he never came, and so the five thousand were not sent. Ar-Rabi': Allah reinforced them with a thousand, then three, then five. The apparent conflict with 8:9 (*a thousand angels in succession*) dissolves — *in succession* implies more following behind.

**Second view:** the promise concerns **Uhud** — but then, Ibn Kathir notes, **the angels did not come**, because the promise was explicitly conditional: *if you are patient and have Taqwa.* The Muslims at Uhud were not patient; they fled; and not one angel came.

Either way, the verse states the purpose of the promise outright: *Allah made it only as good news for you and to reassure your hearts — **and there is no victory except from Allah.*** If He willed, He would have destroyed the enemy without you (47:4–6); He commands fighting to test some of you by others.

**"Not for you is the decision" (3:128).** This came down at a specific moment. The Prophet ﷺ, wounded at Uhud — his front tooth broken, his helmet driven into his head, blood running down his face — said: *how can a people succeed who have injured their Prophet, while he calls them to their Lord?* And in another narration he was cursing named men in the Qunut of Fajr — Al-Harith bin Hisham, Suhayl bin 'Amr, Safwan bin Umayyah. Then this verse came: **the decision is not yours; whether He turns to them in mercy or punishes them.** Ibn Kathir's closing note lands with full force: ***all of those men were later pardoned — they became Muslims.***

### The interlude: Riba, Paradise, and the Muttaqin (3:130–136)

Between the two halves of the Uhud narrative, the Qur'an pauses on character.

**Riba doubled and multiplied (3:130)** — the Jahiliyyah practice Ibn Kathir describes concretely: at the due date the creditor says *pay now or the debt increases*, and the increase compounds year on year until a small capital has multiplied many times over.

**Race to forgiveness (3:133)** — to a Paradise *as wide as the heavens and the earth*, which Ibn Kathir reads as an image of immensity, adding the hadith: **when you ask Allah for Paradise, ask for Al-Firdaws — the highest of it, from which the rivers of Paradise flow, and above it is the Throne.** Asked *"then where is the Fire?"*, the Prophet ﷺ answered with a question: when night comes and covers everything, where is the day? *Where Allah wills it to be.*

**The four qualities (3:134):**

1. **They spend in ease and in hardship** — in good times and hard, when energetic and when not, healthy or ill.
2. **They restrain rage.** The Prophet ﷺ: *the strong one is not he who overpowers people, but he who controls himself when angry.* And: **there is no dose a servant swallows better with Allah than a dose of rage he swallows** seeking Allah's Face — and when he does, Allah fills him inwardly with faith. And: whoever restrains rage while able to act on it, Allah will call him before all creation and let him choose.
3. **They pardon people** — forgiving those who wronged them and holding nothing in their hearts. Ibn Kathir calls this the most excellent conduct in the matter, and cites the oath-hadith: charity never decreases wealth; **whoever forgives, Allah increases his honor**; whoever humbles himself for Allah, Allah raises him.
4. **They return to Allah after sin (3:135)** — they do not persist. The hadith Qudsi of the man who sins four times and each time says *my Lord, I have sinned, forgive me*, and each time Allah says *My servant knew he has a Lord who forgives sins and punishes for them — I have forgiven My servant* — ending with **"do as you wish"** (that is, so long as you keep returning this way). Anas said that when this verse came down, **Iblis wept.**

### Reading the defeat (3:137–148)

*Many ways of life have passed before you; travel the earth and see the end of those who denied.* The previous nations suffered losses too — **and the good end was theirs.**

*Do not weaken, do not grieve; you will be uppermost if you are believers.* And: *if a wound has touched you, a like wound has touched them* — Ibn Kathir's plain reading: **you were injured and killed; so were they.** *And so are the days: We alternate them among people* — at times, in wisdom, the enemy is allowed the upper hand, so that Allah may distinguish the believers and **take martyrs from among you.**

*Do you think you will enter Paradise before Allah knows who among you struggled and who was patient?* (compare 2:214, 29:2). And the sober correction to those who had longed for battle: *you used to wish for death before you met it; now you have seen it with your own eyes* — Ibn Kathir describes the moment vividly: **you saw it when the swords came out, the blades were sharpened, the spears crossed, and men stood in ranks.** The hadith: do not wish to meet the enemy; ask Allah for well-being; **but if you meet them, be patient, and know that Paradise is under the shade of swords.**

**"Muhammad is only a Messenger" (3:144).** Shaytan shouted at Uhud that Muhammad had been killed; Ibn Qami'ah claimed the deed. Some believed it and their resolve collapsed. Ibn Kathir preserves the immortal answer of an unnamed Ansari, who said to a Muhajir bringing him the news while he lay bleeding: ***"Even if Muhammad has been killed, he has delivered the Message. So defend your religion."***

The verse's second life came nine years later. When the Prophet ﷺ died, **Abu Bakr** rode in from As-Sunh, entered, uncovered his face, kissed him, wept, and said: *Allah will not combine two deaths upon you; you have died the death that was written for you.* Then he came out to the people, silenced 'Umar, and said:

> **Whoever worshipped Muhammad — Muhammad is dead. Whoever worshipped Allah — Allah is alive and never dies.**

And he recited this verse. The narrator adds that it was **as though the people had never known Allah had revealed it** until Abu Bakr recited it that day, and then everyone was reciting it. 'Umar said: *by Allah, when I heard Abu Bakr recite it, my legs would not hold me, and I fell to the ground.*

**Death has an appointed term (3:145).** No one dies except by Allah's leave, at a time already fixed (35:11, 6:2). Ibn Kathir notes what this verse is *for*: **it is an encouragement to the fearful** — going out to battle does not shorten a life, and staying home does not lengthen one. Hujr bin 'Adi recited it, rode his horse into the Euphrates, and the army followed him across.

**The Ribbiyyun (3:146).** Ibn Kathir gives the readings — Ibn Mas'ud: thousands; the majority: large bands; Al-Hasan: many scholars, or patient and pious scholars. On the meaning of the verse he favors Ibn Ishaq's reading, defended vigorously by As-Suhayli: **many a Prophet was killed, and his companions did not weaken, did not falter, and did not submit to the enemy after him.** That is what patience is. And they said nothing except a prayer — *forgive us our sins and our excesses, make our feet firm, and give us victory* — **and this was what they kept repeating.** So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the next.

### What actually happened, and why (3:149–158)

**The cause of the defeat, in Al-Bara's own account** (Al-Bukhari): the archers held the hill; the idolators were routed; the Muslims saw their women fleeing up the mountain with their anklets showing. Then the archers said, *"The booty! The booty!"* 'Abdullah bin Jubayr reminded them of the order. **They left anyway** — and the rear was opened, Khalid's horsemen came round behind, and seventy Muslims were killed.

Az-Zubayr's testimony is the most painful line in the account: *by Allah, I saw the women of Hind fleeing, and there was nothing at all standing between us and capturing them — until the archers came down.*

Then Abu Sufyan's exchange from below: *Is Muhammad among you?* — "Do not answer him." *Is Ibn Abi Quhafah? Is Ibn Al-Khattab? They must be dead, or they would have answered.* 'Umar could not restrain himself: *You lie, enemy of Allah — the cause of your misery is still alive.* Abu Sufyan: *Be exalted, Hubal!* — "Answer him: **Allah is higher and more sublime.**" *We have Al-'Uzza and you have none* — "Answer him: **Allah is our protector and you have no protector.**"

Ibn Kathir names each element of the verse's diagnosis (3:152): **Allah did fulfill His promise** — you were killing them by His leave, at the start of the day — *until you lost courage (Fashiltum), disputed about the order, and disobeyed after He showed you what you love.* Some of you wanted this world (those who went for the spoils); some wanted the Hereafter. *Then He turned you back from them to test you.* **And then: *but He has pardoned you.***

**Talhah and the others.** Qays bin Abi Hazim: *I saw Talhah's hand — it was paralyzed, because he shielded the Prophet ﷺ with it.* At one point only Talhah and Sa'd remained beside him. To Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas the Prophet ﷺ handed arrows from his own quiver: **"Shoot — may my father and mother be sacrificed for you."** Sa'd also said he saw two men in white fighting fiercely at the Prophet's ﷺ right and left, whom he never saw before or after: **Jibril and Mika'il.**

**Anas bin An-Nadr**, who had missed Badr and vowed that Allah would see what he did if given a chance, said as the Muslims fell back: *O Allah, I excuse myself before You for what these have done, and disown what those have done* — then went forward, met Sa'd bin Mu'adh and said, ***"Paradise! By Allah, I can smell its fragrance from beyond Uhud"*** — and fought until he was killed. They counted more than eighty wounds on him, and only his sister could identify him, by his fingers.

**The Prophet's ﷺ injuries** were tended by Fatimah, who washed the blood while 'Ali poured water; when washing made the bleeding worse, she burned a piece of matting and pressed the ashes into the wound until it stopped.

**"Distress upon distress" (3:153).** Ibn Kathir records the range of views on what the two were: the defeat and then the rumor of the Prophet's ﷺ death (Ibn 'Abbas, 'Abdur-Rahman bin 'Awf — who said the rumor was worse to them than the defeat); or the rumor and then the casualties; or the lost booty and then the enemy gaining the height. The stated purpose is the same: **so that you would not grieve over what escaped you or what struck you.**

**The slumber (3:154).** After the distress, Allah sent down security — and **slumber overtook one group** while another group was consumed with themselves. Abu Talhah: *I was among those overtaken by drowsiness at Uhud; my sword kept falling from my hand and I kept picking it up.* And: *I raised my head and looked around, and every man's head was nodding.* Ibn Kathir explains why this is a mercy and not a lapse: **sleep in the face of danger is the outward sign of inward calm**, given to those certain that Allah would fulfil His promise (compare 8:11 at Badr).

The second group could not sleep — *thinking of Allah the thought of ignorance*, whispering *"had we had any say in the matter, none of us would have been killed here."* Az-Zubayr says he heard those exact words from **Mu'attib bin Qushayr** while half-dreaming, and Allah's Qur'an then quoted them back. The reply: ***even if you had stayed in your houses, those for whom death was decreed would have gone out to the place where they were to lie.*** The purpose of the whole ordeal, stated plainly: *that Allah might test what is in your breasts and purify what is in your hearts.*

**Those who fled (3:155).** Shaytan made them slip **because of something they had already earned** — and Ibn Kathir quotes the Salaf's principle here: *the reward of a good deed is being enabled to do another after it; the punishment of a sin is being drawn into another after it.* Then: ***but Allah has pardoned them.*** 'Uthman used this verse in his own defense when reproached for fleeing at Uhud: **how can he blame me for something Allah has already forgiven?**

### Mercy, consultation, and honesty (3:159–164)

**"By the mercy of Allah you were lenient with them" (3:159).** Ibn Kathir places this immediately after the account of the disaster, and that placement is the whole point: **the verse about the Prophet's ﷺ gentleness comes directly after the worst military failure of his life, and it is about how he treated the people who caused it.**

*Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have scattered from around you.* Qatadah: with Allah's mercy you became this gentle. 'Abdullah bin 'Amr said he found the Prophet ﷺ described in the earlier scriptures as **not harsh, not coarse, not loud in the markets, and not repaying evil with evil, but forgiving and pardoning.**

The three commands that follow are extraordinary given the circumstances: **pardon them, ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter.**

Ibn Kathir then lists the Prophet's ﷺ actual practice of *Shura*: before Badr, whether to intercept the caravan (and the Ansar's answer that they would follow him into the sea, and never say what Banu Isra'il said to Musa); where to camp at Badr; before Uhud, whether to fortify or march out — **and he followed the majority, which is exactly what had just cost them so dearly**; at the Trench, whether to buy off the Confederates with a third of Madinah's fruit (Sa'd bin 'Ubadah and Sa'd bin Mu'adh refused, and he went with them); at Hudaybiyyah, whether to fight (Abu Bakr disagreed, and he agreed with Abu Bakr); and during the slander against 'A'ishah. The hadith: **the one whose advice is sought is entrusted.**

And then the balance: *when you have resolved, put your trust in Allah.* **Consultation before the decision; reliance on Allah after it.**

**Ghulul (3:161).** Ibn 'Abbas: this was revealed about a red robe missing from the spoils of Badr, when some people said the Prophet ﷺ might have taken it. The verse clears him of every kind of treachery. Then the warnings: the worst *Ghulul* is a yard of a neighbor's land, for which a man will be collared with it from the seven earths; the Prophet's ﷺ furious sermon about the Zakah collector who said *"this is yours and this was given to me as a gift"* — **why did he not sit in his father's or mother's house and see whether he was given gifts?** — followed by the image of the man on the Day of Resurrection carrying a grunting camel, a mooing cow, a bleating sheep, fluttering cloth, or gold and silver on his neck, calling for intercession and being told: *I have no power to help you; I conveyed the Message to you.* And the man at Khaybar whom the Companions called a martyr, of whom the Prophet ﷺ said: **no — I saw him in the Fire over a robe he stole.**

**The greatest favor (3:164).** *Allah conferred a great favor on the believers when He sent among them a Messenger from among themselves.* Ibn Kathir's reading of *"from among themselves"*: **from their own kind, so that they can speak with him, question him, sit with him and learn from him.** The Messenger's work is then given in three verbs — he **recites** the verses, he **purifies** them (commanding good and forbidding evil, so the heart is cleansed of what filled it in ignorance), and he **teaches** them the Book and the Wisdom, that is, the Qur'an and the Sunnah.

### From yourselves, and the answer of the martyrs (3:165–175)

**"Say: it is from yourselves" (3:165).** Seventy fell at Uhud; at Badr the Muslims had killed seventy and captured seventy. Why, then?

Two explanations are preserved, and Ibn Kathir gives both:

- **'Umar's:** it was the ransom taken for the captives of Badr.
- **The majority's** (Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Jurayj, Ar-Rabi', As-Suddi): it was the **archers' disobedience** of the Messenger's order.

Either way, the verse teaches the discipline of looking inward first. Then it balances that immediately: *and what struck you was by Allah's leave, that He might know the believers* — **your fault and His decree are both true at once.**

**The hypocrites exposed (3:167–168).** Called to *"come and fight, or at least defend"*, they answered: *had we known there would be fighting, we would have followed you.* Ibn Kathir demolishes the excuse with the facts: **an army had come from a distant land in rage to avenge its dead — everyone knew there would be fighting.** *They were that day nearer to disbelief than to faith* — and he draws the general principle from the phrasing: **a person moves along a spectrum, at times nearer to faith and at times nearer to disbelief.** Their words about the dead — *had they obeyed us they would not have been killed* — is answered: *then avert death from yourselves, if you are truthful.*

**The martyrs are alive (3:169–171).** Ibn Mas'ud asked the Prophet ﷺ about this verse and was told: **their souls are in green birds with lamps hanging beneath the Throne; they roam Paradise wherever they wish and return to those lamps.** Allah asks them if they wish for anything; they say, *what could we wish for, when we go wherever we wish in Paradise?* He asks three times, and when they understand He will keep asking, they say: **"O Lord, we wish our souls returned to our bodies so we may be killed in Your cause again."**

And in another narration about the dead of Uhud specifically: when they tasted the delight of their food and drink and dwelling, they said they wished their brothers knew, **so that they would not hold back from Jihad** — and Allah said: *I will convey it for you*, and revealed these verses.

Jabir's father was one of them. The Prophet ﷺ told Jabir that Allah has never spoken to anyone except from behind a veil — **but He spoke to your father directly**, and told him to ask. He asked to be returned to life to be killed again, and was told the word had already gone forth that they are not returned. So he asked instead: *then convey the news to those I left behind.*

For every believer, not only the martyr, Ibn Kathir cites a hadith with a chain running through three of the four Imams (Ahmad from Ash-Shafi'i from Malik): **the soul of the believer is a bird feeding in the trees of Paradise until Allah returns it to its body on the Day of Resurrection.** The martyrs' souls are inside green birds — *like stars* among the rest.

**Hamra' Al-Asad (3:172–174).** After Uhud, the idolators started home, then began to regret not finishing the job. Hearing this, the Prophet ﷺ ordered a march — and permitted **only those who had been present at Uhud** to come. They went out still bleeding from the previous day's wounds. 'A'ishah told 'Urwah: *your fathers, Az-Zubayr and Abu Bakr, were among them* — seventy men volunteered. The idolators sent word that they would meet next year, and turned away.

Those who tried to frighten them — *the people have gathered against you, so fear them* — **only increased them in faith**, and they said: ***Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal-Wakil*** — Allah is sufficient for us, and the best Disposer of affairs. Ibn 'Abbas noted that **Ibrahim said these words when he was thrown into the fire**, and Muhammad ﷺ said them here.

The result: *they returned with grace and bounty from Allah; no harm touched them.* Ibn 'Abbas glossed it concretely and almost cheerfully — **the "grace" was that they were kept safe; the "bounty" was that it was the Hajj season, a caravan passed, the Prophet ﷺ traded and made a profit, and he divided it among his Companions.**

And the closing diagnosis of fear itself (3:175): ***It is only Shaytan who makes you fear his allies*** — he inflates them and makes them seem formidable. *So do not fear them; fear Me, if you are believers.*

## 10. Respite, Miserliness, and the Weight of This Life (3:176–189)

**Do not grieve over those who rush into disbelief (3:176)** — the Prophet ﷺ grieved for people's sake, and was told their defiance harms only themselves.

**Respite is not a favor (3:178).** *Do not think that Our giving them time is good for them; We give them time only that they may increase in sin.* Ibn Kathir stacks the parallels: *do they think that in giving them wealth and children We are hastening good to them?* (23:55–56); *We will lead them on gradually from where they do not perceive* (68:44); *Allah's plan is to punish them with these things in this world* (9:85).

**Why calamity comes (3:179).** *Allah will not leave the believers as you are until He distinguishes the wicked from the good.* Ibn Kathir applies it directly to Uhud: the calamity is the instrument by which **the friend of Allah becomes visible and the enemy exposed** — the patience, endurance and obedience of the believers made manifest, and the hypocrites' desertion and treachery brought into the open. Mujahid: He separated them on the day of Uhud. Qatadah: He separated them by Jihad and Hijrah.

**Miserliness (3:180).** What the miser hoards will be *collared around his neck on the Day of Resurrection.* The hadith Al-Bukhari records makes the image exact: whoever is given wealth and does not pay its Zakah, on that Day his wealth will be turned into **a bald-headed poisonous serpent with two black spots above its eyes**, which will coil around his neck, bite his cheeks and say: **"I am your wealth. I am your treasure."** Since the inheritance of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah anyway — *you are only trustees over what He has given you* (57:7) — **spend it now, while it can still benefit you.**

**"Allah is poor and we are rich" (3:181).** The occasion, from Ibn 'Abbas: when *who will lend to Allah a goodly loan?* (2:245) was revealed, they mocked — *has your Lord become poor, that He asks His servants for a loan?* The answer joins their sneer to their older crime: *We shall record what they said, **and their killing of the Prophets without right.*** Ibn Kathir's point: this is how they speak of Allah, and this is how they treated His Messengers — one behavior, one judgment.

**The demand for a sign (3:183)** — *Allah took our promise not to believe any Messenger until he brings an offering consumed by fire.* Allah's reply is a historical refutation: **Messengers before me did bring you exactly that, and you killed them.** So why then did you kill them, if you are truthful?

**Every soul shall taste death (3:185).** Allah alone is the Ever-Living who never dies; jinn, mankind, angels — even the bearers of the Throne — all die, and the Irresistible One remains, Last as He was First. Ibn Kathir reads this as **a comfort to all creation**, since none is exempted. And the measure of success is set at one thing only: *whoever is moved away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise has succeeded.* **A space in Paradise the size of a whip is better than this world and everything in it.** The world is *the enjoyment of deception* — compared to the Hereafter, no more than what clings to a finger dipped in the sea.

**You will certainly be tested (3:186)** — in your wealth, in yourselves, and by **much that will grieve you** from the People of the Book and the idolators. Ibn Kathir's rule: **the believer is tested in proportion to his faith; the stronger the faith, the larger the test.**

The occasion he chooses is a masterclass in restraint. Before Badr, the Prophet ﷺ rode a donkey to visit Sa'd bin 'Ubadah and passed a mixed gathering of Muslims, idolators and Jews where 'Abdullah bin Ubayy sat. Dust from the donkey blew over them, and Ibn Ubayy covered his nose and said, *"Do not cover us in dust."* The Prophet ﷺ greeted them, called them to Allah and recited Qur'an. Ibn Ubayy said: *no speech is better than that — if it is true; but do not trouble us in our gatherings; go back to your place and tell your stories to whoever comes to you.* 'Abdullah bin Rawahah objected, tempers rose, and they were close to fighting before the Prophet ﷺ calmed them. When he told Sa'd what had been said, Sa'd asked him to pardon the man, explaining that Ibn Ubayy had been on the verge of being crowned king of the city when the truth arrived and took it from him: **"he choked on it."**

And the Prophet ﷺ pardoned him. Ibn Kathir's summary of the whole period: **the Messenger and his Companions used to forgive the idolators and the People of the Book and bear their harm, as Allah commanded** (2:109), until the command came. So: *if you are patient and have Taqwa, that is of the matters requiring determination.*

**The covenant to make it clear (3:187).** Allah took a covenant from those given the Scripture **to make the truth known to people and not conceal it** — and they threw it behind their backs for a miserable price. Ibn Kathir turns this on the scholars of his own community immediately: **this is a warning to scholars not to imitate them.** They must spread the beneficial knowledge they have, and are warned against hiding any of it — for whoever is asked about knowledge he has and conceals it will be bridled with fire on the Day of Resurrection.

**Rejoicing in what one has not done (3:188).** Ibn 'Abbas explains the occasion: the Prophet ﷺ asked them something, they concealed what they knew and gave him a false answer, then went off pleased with themselves for having satisfied him and kept their secret. Marwan sent to Ibn 'Abbas protesting that if this verse applied generally, everyone would be punished; Ibn 'Abbas answered that it was revealed about the People of the Scripture. But Abu Sa'id Al-Khudri's account extends it: **hypocrites who stayed behind from battle, then excused themselves with oaths and wanted to be praised for what they had not done.** The hadiths: whoever makes a false claim to gain by it, Allah gives him only decrease; and whoever claims what he has not been given **is like one wearing two garments of falsehood.**

## 11. The Ten Verses (3:190–195)

**"Verily, in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and in the alternation of night and day, there are signs for people of understanding."**

Ibn Kathir opens the sky and the earth out into an inventory: the height and expanse of the heavens; the density and spread of the earth; the turning planets, the seas, the mountains, the deserts, the trees, plants, fruits, animals and metals, and the varieties of color, scent, taste and substance. Then the alternation: each of night and day taking from the other's length, longer, shorter, equal, then repeating — **all of it by the decision of the Almighty, the Most Wise.**

*Ulul-Albab* are those with sound minds who actually think about what things are — as opposed to those who *pass by signs in the heavens and the earth and turn away from them* (12:105).

**They remember Allah standing, sitting and lying on their sides.** Ibn Kathir connects it to the hadith on prayer for the ill — *pray standing; if you cannot, sitting; if you cannot, on your side* — and notes that these people remember Allah **in every condition, in the heart and on the tongue.**

**And they reflect on the creation** — and their reflection produces not a theory but a prayer:

- *Our Lord, You did not create this without purpose* — not in play or jest, but in truth, so that evil is repaid in kind and good rewarded with better.
- *Glory to You* — a denial that He would ever act purposelessly.
- *Save us from the punishment of the Fire.*
- *Our Lord, whomever You admit to the Fire, You have disgraced him* — humiliated before all creation on the Day of Gathering.
- *We heard a caller calling to faith, and we believed* — the caller is the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.
- *Forgive us our sins, efface our evil deeds, and take our souls with the righteous.*
- *Grant us what You promised through Your Messengers, and do not disgrace us on the Day of Resurrection — You never break Your promise.*

Note the movement: **from looking at the sky, to fearing the Fire, to asking to die in good company.**

**The Prophet's ﷺ practice with these verses.** Ibn 'Abbas, who slept at his aunt Maymunah's house, describes the night: the Prophet ﷺ slept, rose in the last third of the night, **looked at the sky, and recited these verses**; then made wudu', used the siwak, and prayed eleven rak'ahs; then, at Bilal's adhan, prayed two and went out to lead Fajr.

And 'A'ishah's account, which Ibn Kathir places last for a reason. Asked for the most astonishing thing she saw from him, she wept and said *everything about him was astonishing* — then described the night he asked her leave to worship his Lord, made wudu' with little water, **stood and wept until his beard was wet, prostrated and wept until the ground was wet**, then lay on his side weeping. When Bilal came for Fajr and asked why he wept when Allah had forgiven him what preceded and what followed, he said: **"What should stop me from weeping, when this night these verses were revealed to me?"** — and recited them, and added:

> **Woe to whoever recites it and does not reflect on it.**

**The answer (3:195).** *So their Lord answered them.* Umm Salamah had said: *"O Messenger of Allah, Allah does not mention women in connection with Hijrah"* — and this came down: **"I will never allow the work of any of you to be lost, male or female; you are of one another."**

Ibn Kathir explains *"you are of one another"* as: **you are equal in relation to My reward.** Then the four named sacrifices — those who emigrated (leaving land, loved ones, brothers, friends and neighbors), who were driven from their homes, who were harmed in His cause (*their only crime, in the eyes of people, was believing in Allah alone* — 60:1, 85:8), and who fought and were killed. Their evil deeds will be effaced and they will be admitted to Gardens beneath which rivers flow. And Ibn Kathir preserves the one exception in the hadith: a man asked whether being killed patiently, advancing and not retreating, would erase his sins; the Prophet ﷺ said yes — then, when asked again, **"Yes — except the debt; Jibril has just told me so."**

## 12. The Closing Command (3:196–200)

**Do not be deceived by their affluence (3:196–198).** *Let not the free movement of the disbelievers through the land deceive you* — it is a brief enjoyment, and their abode is Hell. Set against it: for those with Taqwa, gardens, permanence, **a welcome from Allah** — *and what is with Allah is better for the righteous.* Abu Ad-Darda' drew the sharpest possible conclusion from these two verses read together: **death is better for every believer, and death is better for every disbeliever** — the first because of what is with Allah, the second because respite only increases their sin.

**The believers among the People of the Book (3:199).** The surah, having argued with them for two hundred verses, ends by naming those among them who believe in Allah, in what was revealed to Muhammad ﷺ, and in what was revealed to them, **humbling themselves before Allah, and not selling His verses for a small price** — that is, **not concealing what they know** of the description of the Prophet ﷺ and his Ummah. Ibn Kathir: these are the best of the People of the Book, whether Jews or Christians.

He notes the numbers honestly: fewer than ten Jewish rabbis embraced Islam, 'Abdullah bin Salam among them; **many more Christians did** (compare 5:82–85). And he gives the scene of **Ja'far bin Abi Talib reciting Surah Maryam to An-Najashi** before his priests and patriarchs, until their beards were wet with weeping — and the Prophet's ﷺ announcement when the Negus died: **"A brother of yours in Ethiopia has passed away; come and pray over him"** — and he prayed the funeral prayer for him in absence. Al-Hasan: these are the ones who believed in Muhammad ﷺ before he was sent and recognized Islam, and Allah gave them a double reward — and the hadith of the three who receive a double reward includes **a man of the People of the Book who believed in his own Prophet and then in me.**

**The last verse (3:200):** ***O you who believe — be patient, and outdo others in patience (Sabiru), and Rabitu, and have Taqwa of Allah, so that you may be successful.***

Ibn Kathir unpacks all four:

1. **Sabr** — Al-Hasan Al-Basri: the believers are commanded to be patient in the religion Allah chose for them, never abandoning it **in ease or hardship, comfort or calamity, until they die as Muslims.**
2. **Musabarah** — to out-endure the enemy; to be more patient than those who oppose the truth.
3. **Ribat** — and here he gives both meanings, without choosing between them:
   - **The inner Ribat:** persevering in worship, and specifically **waiting for the next prayer after the prayer.** The hadith: shall I tell you what erases sins and raises ranks? — *perfecting wudu' in difficult conditions, walking many steps to the Masjid, and waiting for the prayer after the prayer* — **"that is the Ribat, that is the Ribat, that is the Ribat."**
   - **The outer Ribat:** manning the frontier posts. *A day of Ribat in Allah's cause is better than this world and all it contains.* *Ribat for a day and a night is better than fasting a month and standing its nights; and whoever dies in Ribat, his deeds continue to be recorded for him, his provision continues, and he is safe from the trial of the grave.* And: **two eyes the Fire will not touch — an eye that wept from fear of Allah, and an eye that kept watch through the night in Allah's cause.**
4. **Taqwa** — *in all your affairs and states.* The Prophet's ﷺ counsel to Mu'adh when he sent him to Yemen: **have Taqwa of Allah wherever you are, follow a bad deed with a good deed and it will wipe it out, and deal with people in a good manner.**

Ibn Kathir ends the surah with a scene from the eighth century. **'Abdullah bin Al-Mubarak**, at the frontier of Tarsus, sent a poem to **Al-Fudayl bin 'Iyad** in the sacred precinct of Makkah, addressed to the worshipper of the two Holy Masjids. Its substance: that the one who wets his cheek with tears should know their necks are wet with blood; that the one who tires his horse for nothing should know their horses tire in battle; that his scent is perfume while theirs is dust and spearpoint — and that the Prophet ﷺ told them the dust of Allah's horses and the smoke of Hell can never meet in one man's nostrils, and that Allah's Book says the martyr is not dead. Al-Fudayl read it in the Masjid, wept, and said: ***"Abu 'Abdur-Rahman has told the truth and given me sincere advice."*** Then he dictated a hadith in return, as payment for the delivery — the one in which a man asks for a deed equal to Jihad and is told that even continuous prayer and unbroken fasting would not reach the rank of the Mujahid, **whose horse earns him reward for as long as it lives.**

And Ibn Kathir closes: *The Tafsir of Surah Al-'Imran ends here — and we ask Allah that we die upon the path of the Qur'an and the Sunnah.*

## 13. In One Breath

Al-'Imran is a surah about **holding your footing** — first intellectually, then physically.

It opens with the two kinds of verses, and the discipline of reading the unclear in the light of the clear, because that is where sects are born. It then walks the history of the chosen households down to Maryam, Zakariyya, Yahya and 'Isa, showing that a man formed in a womb, however miraculous his coming, is a servant — and it settles the argument with a challenge no one dared accept.

Then it turns to a battlefield where the believers themselves failed, and refuses to soften any part of it: **the order was clear, the archers left their posts, the rear was opened, seventy died, the Prophet ﷺ bled.** And into that failure it speaks not condemnation but three sentences that hold the whole surah together — ***"But Allah has pardoned you"***; ***"By the mercy of Allah you were gentle with them"***; and ***"consult them in the matter."***

It ends where reflection should end. Men of understanding look at the sky and the turning of night into day, conclude that none of it is purposeless, and ask to be saved from the Fire and to die in good company. And Allah answers: **no one's work is lost, male or female — you are of one another.**

The last word given is a sequence of four: **endure, out-endure, stand guard, and fear Allah.** Everything before it is the reason why.
