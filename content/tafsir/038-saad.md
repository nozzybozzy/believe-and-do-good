*Revealed in Makkah · 88 ayat · named for the letter it opens with*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Saad has one governing contrast, and once you see it the whole surah organises itself.

At the start stand men who are **in false pride and opposition** — `izzah and shiqaq, arrogance and stubborn resistance. At the end stands Iblis, refusing to bow, saying *I am better than he*. Between those two poles Allah places three servants — Dawud, Suleiman, Ayub — and after each one repeats the same word: **awwab**, the one who keeps returning.

That is the surah. Pride is the disease; returning is the cure; and the story of Iblis at the close is placed there to show the Quraysh what their own posture actually is and where it ends.

The frame is also verbal: the surah opens with an oath **by the Qur'an full of Dhikr (reminding)** and closes with **it is only a Dhikr for all the worlds**. Everything in between is the reminder itself.

---

## 1. The Oath and Its Answer (38:1–3)

> *Saad. By the Qur'an full of reminding.*

On the disconnected letters, Ibn Kathir simply refers back to his discussion at the opening of Al-Baqarah and declines to repeat himself.

On **Dhi adh-Dhikr**, two readings that he says do not conflict:

- **Ad-Dahhak, Qatadah, Ibn Jarir** — full of *reminder*, i.e. it contains your reminder, as in 21:10.
- **Ibn Abbas, Sa`id bin Jubayr, Ibn Uyaynah, Abu Salih, As-Suddi** — full of *honour*, i.e. of high standing.

Both hold, because it is a noble Book that reminds, warns, and leaves no one with an excuse.

Then a small point of classical craftsmanship: **what is the oath's answer?** Ibn Kathir offers 38:14 — *not one of them but denied the Messengers, therefore My torment was justified*. Qatadah, and Ibn Jarir after him, prefer 38:2 — *Nay, those who disbelieve are in false pride and opposition*. Either way the oath lands on the same target: the Qur'an is a reminder, but pride blocks reception.

**38:3 — the door that closes.** How many generations were destroyed; they cried out *when there was no longer time for escape*.

- **Ibn Abbas**: it was no longer the hour for calling, fleeing, or escaping.
- **Muhammad bin Ka`b**: they called out for Tawhid, and reached for repentance, only once their lives were already spent.
- **Qatadah**: they wanted to repent at the sight of the punishment — too late to call out at all.

The lesson is not that repentance is hard. It is that repentance has a **window**, and the window is life.

---

## 2. What Astonished Them (38:4–8)

Three separate objections, each a species of pride:

1. **A human warner** — they wondered that the messenger was one of themselves (compare 10:2).
2. **One God instead of many** — *has he made the gods into One God?* They had inherited idol-worship from their fathers and their hearts were full of love for it, so being asked to empty their hearts of it was more than they could bear.
3. **This man, of all people** — *has the Reminder been sent down to him from among us?* (compare 43:31, and Thamud's identical sneer at Salih in 54:25).

### The occasion of revelation: the room at Abu Talib's

Ibn Jarir's report from Ibn Abbas is the human centre of this passage. Abu Talib is ill; Quraysh chiefs, Abu Jahl among them, come and complain that his nephew is insulting their gods. Abu Talib sends for the Prophet ﷺ. There is exactly one seat's space beside Abu Talib — and **Abu Jahl, afraid that nearness would soften the old man, jumps into it**, so the Prophet ﷺ sits by the door.

Asked what he wants, he answers: *one word* — one word which, if they say it, the Arabs will follow them and the non-Arabs will pay them tribute.

They said: one word? By your father, ten! What is it?

**La ilaha illallah.**

They stood up, brushing down their clothes, and walked out saying the words the Qur'an records: *has he made the gods into One God? This is a curious thing.* And this passage was revealed, down to *Nay, but they have not tasted My torment.*

What the story exposes: they were ready to concede *ten* words. They were not ready to concede **one**, because the one was the only one that cost them anything.

### The leaders' walkout

> *Go on, and remain constant to your gods! Verily, this is a thing designed.*

**Ibn Jarir** reads their meaning precisely: they took Tawhid to be a power grab — a device by which Muhammad ﷺ would gain rank and a following over them. It is the oldest defence against a truth claim: convert it into a motive claim.

**"We have not heard this in the religion of these later days."** Al-`Awfi from Ibn Abbas: they meant Christianity — had this been true, the Christians would have told us. Mujahid and Qatadah gloss *ikhtilaq* as a lie; Ibn Abbas, a fabrication.

---

## 3. Allah's Reply: Do You Own Anything? (38:9–11)

Three questions, escalating, each demolishing the assumption behind *why him and not us*:

- **Do they hold the treasures of your Lord's mercy?** Prophethood is not distributed by public vote. Compare 4:53–55 — they would not give mankind so much as the speck on a date-stone — and 17:100.
- **Do they own the dominion of the heavens and earth?** *Then let them ascend by means* — the *asbab* being, per Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Sa`id bin Jubayr and Qatadah, the ways up to heaven. Ad-Dahhak: then let them climb to the seventh heaven.
- **What they actually are**: a defeated host, like the Confederates before them.

Ibn Kathir marks the fulfilment: this is 54:44–46, *their multitude will be put to flight and they will show their backs* — **which is what happened on the day of Badr**. A Makkan taunt, cashed in on a Madinan battlefield.

---

## 4. The Roll Call, and the Single Common Cause (38:12–17)

Nuh's people · `Ad · Fir`awn *of the stakes* · Thamud · Lut's people · the Dwellers of Al-Aykah — *such were the Confederates*.

They were greater than you in wealth, strength and children, and none of it delayed the command of your Lord by an hour. And the diagnosis is one line: **every one of them denied the Messengers**. That is the whole aetiology.

What awaits the present deniers is **a single Sayhah, with no pause and no repeat** — Malik from Zayd bin Aslam: none can avert it. Ibn Kathir identifies it as the blast on the Trumpet, when Israfil is commanded to sound a long note and no one in the heavens or earth is left untouched by terror, save whom Allah spares.

**Their mockery: "Hasten our Qitt."** *Qitt* is read as one's written record, or one's allotted share. Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Ad-Dahhak and Al-Hasan: they were demanding the punishment be brought forward — the same bravado as 8:32, *if this is the truth, rain stones on us*. Ibn Jarir widens it: hasten for us whatever we have coming, good or bad, here and now. Ibn Kathir calls this the better view.

And the answer given to the Prophet ﷺ is one word — **be patient** — coupled with the promise that the patience ends in victory. That command is also the hinge: it introduces the three prophets who model it.

---

## 5. Dawud — Strength That Bends (38:17–20)

> *Remember Our servant Dawud, endued with Al-Ayd. Verily, he was ever awwab.*

**Al-Ayd** = strength (Ibn Abbas, As-Suddi, Ibn Zayd); strength *in obedience* (Mujahid); strength in worship together with sound understanding of the religion (Qatadah).

The hadith that fills the word out: the most beloved prayer to Allah is Dawud's, and the most beloved fasting is Dawud's — **he would sleep half the night, stand a third, sleep a sixth, and fast every other day.** He never fled a battlefield. And he was *awwab* — he turned back to Allah in all his affairs.

Note the shape of that night: it is not maximal. It is sustainable, and therefore lifelong. Strength here means the capacity to keep returning, not the capacity to burn out spectacularly.

**The mountains and birds** glorified with him at `Ashi (day's end) and Ishraq (sunrise). Birds passing overhead would hear the Zabur and stop mid-air to join him; mountains echoed him back.

### An unexpected discovery of a prayer

Ibn Jarir preserves a lovely aside. Ibn Abbas did not pray Ad-Duha. Abdullah bin Al-Harith took him to Umm Hani, who described how the Prophet ﷺ, on the day of the conquest of Makkah, bathed behind a screen in her house and then prayed eight rak`ahs — standing, bowing, prostrating and sitting all equally brief.

Ibn Abbas left saying he had read the Qur'an cover to cover and never knew of Salat Ad-Duha until that moment — then recited *glorify with him at `Ashi and Ishraq*, adding that he had always wondered what Salat al-Ishraq was, and now he knew.

**The three gifts of 38:20**: a strengthened kingdom (Mujahid: the strongest and most powerful man of his world), **Al-Hikmah**, and **Fasl al-Khitab**.

- *Al-Hikmah* — understanding and intelligence (Mujahid); the Book of Allah and acting on it (Qatadah); prophethood (As-Suddi).
- *Fasl al-Khitab* — **Shurayh al-Qadi and Ash-Sha`bi**: testimony and oaths. **Qatadah**: two witnesses for the plaintiff, or an oath from the defendant. Ibn Kathir notes this remains the basis of the Ummah's judicial procedure until the Day of Resurrection. Mujahid's broader reading — soundness in speech and in judgement — includes all of it, and Ibn Kathir and Ibn Jarir favour it.

## 6. The Two Litigants — and a Lesson in Method (38:21–25)

Two men climb the wall into Dawud's private chamber on a day he had ordered no one to enter. He is alarmed. They present a case: my brother has ninety-nine ewes, I have one, and he demands mine and overpowers me in argument. Dawud rules before hearing the other side; then he *realises he has been tested*, seeks forgiveness, falls prostrate, and turns back.

Here Ibn Kathir does something worth pausing on. The Tafsir literature carries an elaborate backstory to this episode — and he **refuses to relay it**:

> The scholars' accounts here rest mostly on **Isra'iliyyat**. Nothing has come from the Infallible Prophet ﷺ that can be accepted as sound. Ibn Abi Hatim's hadith on it runs through **Yazid Ar-Raqashi** from Anas — and although Yazid was a righteous man, the Imams grade his hadiths weak.

His conclusion: **speak of it briefly and refer knowledge of it to Allah.** The Qur'an is true and what it contains is true; the embroidery around it is not part of the deal.

This is one of the most valuable pages in the surah, and it has nothing to do with sheep. It is a working rule for reading scripture: *where the text is silent, the silence is the content.*

What the passage does teach plainly is in the ruling itself — **many partners wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and they are few** — and in Dawud's response, which is not defensiveness but prostration.

### The Sajdah of Saad

Ibn Kathir settles the fiqh point:

- **Ibn Abbas** (Ahmad, Bukhari, Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i): the prostration in Saad is **not** among the obligatory prostrations — but he saw the Messenger ﷺ perform it.
- Also from Ibn Abbas (An-Nasa'i, reliable chain): *Dawud prostrated as repentance; we prostrate as thanks.*
- **Abu Sa`id al-Khudri**: the Prophet ﷺ recited Saad on the minbar, came down and prostrated, and the people prostrated with him. On a later occasion he saw them readying themselves and said: *this is a Prophet's repentance — but I see you preparing to prostrate* — then came down and prostrated anyway.
- **Mujahid asked Ibn Abbas why he prostrated at all.** The answer was an argument from 6:84–90: Dawud is named among those Allah guided, and your Prophet ﷺ was commanded *follow their guidance*. Dawud prostrated here; so the Messenger ﷺ prostrated here.

And Dawud's own end: *a near access to Us, and a good place of return* — earned, Ibn Kathir says, by his repentance and by the perfect justice of his rule.

## 7. The Verse for Everyone in Authority (38:26)

> *O Dawud! We have placed you as a successor on the earth; so judge between men in truth, and follow not desire — it will mislead you from the path of Allah.*

Ibn Kathir reads this as direct counsel to anyone holding power: rule by the truth revealed, and know that the drift away from it begins as **desire**, not as ideology.

The report he attaches is superb. **Al-Walid bin Abd al-Malik** — a sitting Caliph — asked Ibrahim Abu Zur`ah whether anyone has the right to question the Khalifah. Granted permission to speak, and granted the protection of Allah, Ibrahim replied with a question:

*O Commander of the Faithful — are you dearer to Allah than Dawud? Allah gave him prophethood **and** kingship, and then warned him in His own Book.*

Then he recited 38:26.

On the closing clause — *a severe torment because they forgot the Day of Reckoning* — **Ikrimah** reads: punishment on that Day for what they forgot. **As-Suddi**: punishment for what they neglected to prepare for that Day. Ibn Kathir prefers As-Suddi's as closer to the apparent sense.

## 8. The Wisdom of Creation, and Why There Must Be a Hereafter (38:27–29)

> *We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them without purpose. That is the assumption of those who disbelieve.*

Then the argument that Ibn Kathir develops most fully in this surah — an argument from **justice, addressed to reason**:

> Shall We treat the believers who do righteous deeds like those who spread corruption? Shall We treat the God-fearing like the wicked?

The answer is no, and the consequence is unavoidable. We watch criminals prosper — wealth, children, comfort — and die in that state. We watch wronged believers die of grief. If the All-Wise and All-Just, who does not commit an atom's weight of injustice, is real, **then there must be a realm where the balance is settled**. If it does not happen here, there must be a *there*.

This is why the passage closes on a Book *full of blessings, so that they may ponder its verses, and that people of understanding may remember* — the Qur'an argues, and expects to be reasoned with.

## 9. Suleiman — the Horses, and What Replaced Them (38:30–40)

> *How excellent a servant! He was ever awwab.*

He inherited **prophethood** from Dawud (compare 27:16); Dawud had many other sons.

**The horses at sunset.** The *safinat al-jiyad* were shown to him as king — Mujahid describes horses standing on three legs with the fourth raised, and swift. Ibn Kathir cites more than one of the Salaf: he became so absorbed in them that **the time of `Asr passed**. Not deliberately — through absorption, exactly as happened to the Prophet ﷺ on the day of Khandaq, when `Umar came cursing the Quraysh because he had not been able to pray `Asr until the sun had nearly set, and the Prophet ﷺ answered, *by Allah, I did not pray it either* — then they went to Buthan, made wudu, prayed `Asr after sunset, and prayed Maghrib after it.

*(A charming aside Ibn Kathir includes here: `A'ishah's toys were revealed by a gust of wind, among them a cloth horse with wings. Asked about it, she said — did you not hear that Suleiman had a horse with wings? And the Messenger ﷺ laughed until his molars showed.)*

**"Bring them back to me."** Two readings, and Ibn Kathir declines the more comfortable one:

| View | Held by |
|---|---|
| He struck their necks and hamstrings — *no, by Allah, you will not keep me from worshipping my Lord again* | Al-Hasan al-Basri, Qatadah, As-Suddi |
| He patted their necks and legs out of love | Ibn Abbas (via Ali bin Abi Talhah), favoured by **Ibn Jarir** |

Ibn Jarir's reasoning is that a prophet would not punish an animal or destroy his own wealth over a fault that was not the animal's. Ibn Kathir says this **is subject to review**: such an act may have been permissible under their law, and his anger was for Allah's sake. Decisively, look at what followed — *he gave them up for Allah, and Allah compensated him with something better*: the wind, whose morning journey was a month and whose evening journey was a month. Faster and better than any horse.

He seals it with the Bedouin's hadith: **you give up nothing for the sake of Allah without Allah giving you something better than it.**

**The throne and the body.** *We tried Suleiman and placed on his throne a body, then he returned.* Ibn Kathir gives no story here either — the test happened, and what matters is that he **turned back** and asked forgiveness, then asked for a kingdom belonging to no one after him.

Why that strange request? The apparent sense, supported by hadith: **no one after me should have the right to ask for such a thing.** Two reports:

- **Abu Hurayrah** (Bukhari): an `Ifrit of the Jinn interrupted the Prophet's ﷺ prayer; Allah gave him power over it and he intended to tie it to a pillar of the mosque so they would all see it in the morning — *then I remembered my brother Suleiman's words*, and let it go humiliated.
- **Abu Ad-Darda'** (Muslim): Iblis came at him with a flame of fire; he sought refuge three times, cursed him with the complete curse of Allah three times, reached out to seize him — *and by Allah, were it not for the words of our brother Suleiman, he would have been left chained up as a plaything for the children of Madinah.*

A prophet twice declines a spectacular miracle out of courtesy to a request made centuries earlier. That is what respecting a du`a looks like.

**The kingdom itself**: the wind at his command; the Shayatin as builders (high rooms, basins like reservoirs, fixed cauldrons — work beyond human capacity) and as divers for pearls; the rebellious ones bound in chains. And then: *spend or withhold — no account will be asked of you.*

Ibn Kathir sets against this the choice offered to the Prophet ﷺ in the Sahihayn — servant-and-messenger, or prophet-and-king. He consulted Jibril, who said: **be humble.** He chose the former, because it ranks higher with Allah. Yet Suleiman's option was no small thing either: after listing what he was given here, Allah adds that he too has *a near access to Us and a good place of return* — this world **and** the next.

## 10. Ayub — Eighteen Years, and One Bundle of Grass (38:41–44)

Ibn Kathir's account is restrained and devastating. The affliction took his body, his wealth and his children until nothing in him was sound **but his heart**. He was left on the city dump, shunned by relatives and strangers alike, for close to **eighteen years**. One person remained: his wife, who kept her devotion because of her faith in Allah and His Messenger, who hired herself out as a servant to feed him, and who came straight back to him each time.

Then the call — *Shaytan has afflicted me with distress and torment* (glossed: distress in the body, torment in the loss of wealth and children) — and the cure, which is startlingly physical. **Strike the ground with your foot.** A spring opens; he washes; the outer affliction goes. He strikes again elsewhere; another spring; he drinks; the inner affliction goes.

The long narration in Ibn Jarir adds the detail that hurts most: two friends visited him morning and evening, and one finally said *he must have committed a sin no one ever committed — eighteen years and no mercy.* Ayub's answer was that he did not know what the man meant, but Allah knew that he used to pass two men arguing who invoked Allah's name, and would **go home and offer expiation on their behalf**, lest Allah's name had been mentioned improperly.

That is the character underneath the patience: he was not merely enduring; he was interceding for strangers.

Then the day he took too long, and his wife turned to look — and found him restored, better than he had ever looked. She said, may Allah bless you, have you seen the afflicted prophet of Allah? I have never seen a man more like him. He said: **I am he.** And Allah sent two clouds which rained gold onto his two threshing floors until both were full.

**The bundle of grass.** In his suffering he had sworn to strike his wife a hundred blows over something she had done. Ibn Kathir puts the moral problem exactly: after that service, that mercy, that compassion — how could it be repaid with a beating? So Allah showed him the way out: take a bundle of a hundred stems and strike once. **The oath is fulfilled and the vow is not broken** — and no one is harmed.

Ibn Kathir's comment: this is the way out granted to whoever has Taqwa and turns back to Allah — and he cites 65:2–3, *whoever has Taqwa of Allah, He will make a way out for him.*

Also here, the hadith of the golden locusts (Bukhari): Ayub, bathing, gathered them into his garment, and his Lord called — *have I not made you rich enough to have no need of what you see?* He answered: **yes, O Lord — but I cannot do without Your blessing.**

His family was restored *and the like of them with them* — Al-Hasan and Qatadah: the same family brought back, and others added — **as a mercy from Us**, in return for his patience and his constant turning; **and a reminder** for those who understand that patience ends in a way out.

## 11. The Chosen (38:45–49)

**Ibrahim, Ishaq, Ya`qub** — *Ulil-Aydi wal-Absar*. Ibn Abbas: great strength in worship, and understanding of the religion. Qatadah and As-Suddi say the same.

**Why they were chosen**: *by a quality — the remembrance of the Abode.*

- **Mujahid**: He made them work for the Hereafter, and they had nothing else besides it.
- **Malik bin Dinar**: **Allah removed the love of this world from their hearts** and singled them out for the remembrance of the Abode.
- **Qatadah**: they used to remind people of the Hereafter and strive for it.

Then **Isma`il, Al-Yasa`, Dhul-Kifl** — all among the best; Ibn Kathir refers back to Al-Anbiya' rather than repeat their stories.

## 12. Two Endings (38:49–64)

**For the God-fearing** — `Adn Gardens whose gates **open for them as they arrive**; reclining (said to be cross-legged on couches beneath canopies); fruit prepared as they asked for it; drink brought as they wish it; *Qasirat at-Tarf* — spouses who restrain their glance from all but their husbands — and all of one age (Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Sa`id bin Jubayr, Muhammad bin Ka`b, As-Suddi). And the seal on all of it: **a provision that never finishes** (compare 16:96; 11:108; 84:25; 13:35).

**For the transgressors** — Hell as the place they enter and that closes over them from every side. Their drink: **Hamim** (heated to the utmost) and **Ghassaq** (its exact opposite — cold beyond bearing). *And others of similar kind, all together* — Al-Hasan al-Basri: **different kinds of punishment**; others: matched opposites, scalding and freezing, being raised up and thrown down, Hamim to drink and Zaqqum to eat.

Then the quarrelling, which Ibn Kathir presents as the real texture of that place. Instead of greeting one another, each incoming nation is cursed by the last (7:38). Followers turn on leaders: *no welcome to you — you brought this on us*; then the prayer that no one wants to be the object of: **our Lord, whoever brought this upon us, double his torment.**

And finally the passage that stings hardest:

> *What is the matter with us that we do not see men we used to count among the worst?*

**Mujahid**: this is Abu Jahl — *where is Bilal, where is `Ammar, where is Suhayb…* They had mocked them in the world; now they scan Hell for them and cannot find them, and console themselves that perhaps they are here somewhere unseen. Then they learn where they actually are (7:44–49): calling down from the heights of Paradise, *we have found true what our Lord promised us — have you?*

Ibn Kathir: this account of their mutual dispute is itself declared **the very truth**, beyond doubt.

## 13. Adam and Iblis: the Surah's Own Diagnosis (38:65–85)

The passage begins with the Prophet ﷺ instructed to say what he is — *only a warner* — and what the message is: **a great news, from which you turn away.**

Then the proof of prophethood, given in a single line: *I had no knowledge of the chiefs on high when they were disputing.* Ibn Kathir: **were it not for revelation, how would he know of it at all?** And the dispute in question is precisely the one now recounted — Iblis's refusal, and his argument with his Lord over Adam.

The sequence:

- The announcement **before** the creation: *I am going to create a human from clay*; when he is fashioned and My spirit breathed into him, **fall prostrate**.
- All the angels obeyed. Iblis did not — **and he was never one of them**; he was of the Jinn, and his nature betrayed him at the moment of greatest need.
- The charge: *what prevented you from prostrating to one I created with My two Hands? Were you too proud, or are you of the exalted?*
- The answer, and the first false analogy in history: **I am better than he. You made me from fire and him from clay.**
- The verdict: get out, you are outcast, and My curse is on you until the Day of Recompense. Ibn Kathir notes the name itself is the sentence: **Iblis, from *ablasa min ar-rahmah*** — he despaired of mercy, and there is no hope of it for him.
- The reprieve, granted by the Forbearing One who does not hasten to punish — and the use he made of the safety: *by Your might, I will mislead them all* — **except Your chosen servants** (compare 17:62 and 17:65, *over My servants you have no authority*).
- The close: *the truth is — and the truth I say* — Mujahid reads it as *I am the Truth and the truth I say*; As-Suddi as an oath sworn by Allah. And the oath is that Hell will be filled with him and all who follow him (compare 32:13; 17:63).

Now look back at 38:2. **The disbelievers are in `izzah and shiqaq — pride and opposition.** And here is the archetype: a creature who had every fact right, who never doubted the existence of Allah for a moment, and who was ruined by exactly one thing — an unwillingness to bow to something he considered beneath him. Iblis's problem was never information. It was posture.

Which is why the three prophets in the middle of the surah are each labelled **awwab**. Awwab is the opposite of Iblis. Not sinlessness — Dawud prostrates in repentance, Suleiman turns back after his test, Ayub calls out from the ash-heap. The distinguishing mark is simply that they **come back**.

## 14. The Closing Instruction (38:86–88)

> *Say: I ask you no wage for this, nor am I one of the Mutakallifin.*

No payment from the goods of this world for the message or the counsel. And **Mutakallif** — the one who affects and adds — is glossed: *I add nothing to what Allah has told me. What I am commanded, I do; I neither add nor subtract.*

The report Ibn Kathir attaches (Bukhari and Muslim) turns this into a rule for every scholar and every one of us. **Ibn Mas`ud** said: whoever knows a thing, let him say it; whoever does not know, let him say **"Allah knows best"** — for it is part of knowledge, when you do not know, to say *Allah knows best*. And his proof was this very verse.

Which brings the surah full circle to Ibn Kathir's own restraint at the story of the two litigants. He practised what he here quotes.

**It is only a Dhikr for all the worlds** — for everyone held accountable, men and Jinn (Ibn Abbas); compare 6:19 and 11:17.

**And you shall certainly know its truth after a while.** Qatadah: after death. Ikrimah: on the Day of Resurrection. Ibn Kathir: **no contradiction — whoever dies has already entered the rulings of that Day.**

## If You Remember Nothing Else

1. **Pride is the barrier, not ignorance.** Quraysh would have given ten words; they would not give one. Iblis had perfect theology and one refusal. Saad diagnoses unbelief as a posture of the self, not a gap in the data.
2. **The word the surah repeats is *awwab*.** Dawud, Suleiman, Ayub — each falls, each returns, and the returning is what earns the praise. Sanctity here is not the absence of a fall.
3. **Repentance has a window, and the window is your lifetime.** *They cried out when there was no longer time for escape.*
4. **Give something up for Allah and you are not left poorer.** Suleiman surrendered the horses and received the wind: *you give up nothing for Allah's sake without Allah giving you something better.*
5. **There is always a way out for the one with Taqwa.** A hundred blows became one bundle of grass — the oath kept, the wife unharmed. Where fear of Allah is real, the exit is real (65:2–3).
6. **Power is on trial.** *O Dawud, judge with truth and do not follow desire* — a verse aimed at prophets and caliphs alike, as Ibrahim Abu Zur`ah reminded al-Walid to his face.
7. **The world's injustice is itself an argument for the Hereafter.** The wicked die comfortable, the wronged die grieving; a just God guarantees a reckoning somewhere.
8. **When you don't know, say "Allah knows best."** Ibn Kathir refuses the Isra'iliyyat around Dawud's trial, and Ibn Mas`ud makes the refusal a form of knowledge — grounded in the surah's own final instruction.
