*Revealed in Makkah · 5 ayat · "The Twisted Fibre" — also known as Al-Lahab, "The Flame"*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-Masad is the only surah in the Qur'an devoted to **naming a living opponent and sentencing him**. Not a nation, not a type, not "those who disbelieve" — a man, by his own byname, alongside his wife.

It comes out of a single afternoon. The Prophet ﷺ climbed a hill and called Makkah together to warn them; his own uncle answered by cursing him in front of everyone. The surah is the reply — and Ibn Kathir's structural observation is the key to the whole thing:

> **The first part is a supplication against him, and the second is information about him.**

That is, verse 1 is a curse; verses 2–5 are a **report of what will happen**. And it is a report about a man who was alive, well-connected, and free to falsify it at any moment — and who never did. Abu Lahab lived on for years after hearing that he would enter a Fire of flames, and did not so much as feign belief to embarrass the claim.

There is a bitter symmetry running underneath. He was called **Abu Lahab — "Father of Flame" — because of the brightness of his face**. The surah takes the name he was given for his beauty and hands it back to him as his destination: *a Fire full of lahab*.

## 1. The Day on the Hill (Reason for Revelation)

**Al-Bukhari, from Ibn Abbas:**

The Prophet ﷺ went out to the valley of Al-Batha, ascended the mountain, and cried out the alarm-call the Arabs used for a raid — *Ya sabahah!* The Quraysh gathered to him. He asked them:

> *If I told you all that the enemy was going to attack you in the morning, or in the evening, would you all believe me?*

They said: **Yes.**

He said:

> *Verily, I am a warner to you all before the coming of a severe torment.*

**Abu Lahab said: "Have you gathered us for this! May you perish!"**

And Allah revealed the surah.

In a second narration he stood up **dusting off his hands** — a gesture of contemptuous dismissal — and said, *"Perish you for the rest of this day! Have you gathered us for this?"*

Notice what the exchange establishes before it collapses. They had just **conceded his truthfulness in public**. The rejection that followed was not a verdict on his honesty; it was a refusal of the message by a man who had no argument left but an insult.

---

## 2. Who Abu Lahab Was

- **An uncle of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ** — a son of Abdul-Muttalib.
- Real name: **Abdul-Uzza bin Abdul-Muttalib**. (A name meaning "servant of Al-Uzza," an idol.)
- Surname: **Abu Utaybah**. He was called **Abu Lahab because of the brightness of his face**.
- He used to **often cause harm** to the Prophet ﷺ, and he hated and scorned both him and his religion.

### The man at the back of the crowd

**Imam Ahmad**, from Abu Az-Zinad, records the memory of **Rabi'ah bin Abbad** of Bani Ad-Dil — a man of the Jahiliyyah who later accepted Islam:

> "I saw the Prophet ﷺ in the time of pre-Islamic ignorance in the market of **Dhul-Majaz**, and he was saying: *O people! Say there is no god worthy of worship except Allah, and you will be successful.*"

People gathered around him — and **behind him** was a man with a bright face, squint eyes, and two braids in his hair, calling out:

> **"Verily, he is an apostate and a liar!"**

The man followed him wherever he went. Rabi'ah asked who it was. They told him: **"This is his uncle, Abu Lahab."**

Abu Zinad, suspecting the memory of a child, pressed him: *were you young at the time?* Rabi'ah answered:

> "No. By Allah, that day I was most intelligent, and I was the strongest blower of the flute."

Ahmad was alone in recording this hadith. Its value is the picture it leaves: **the opposition was not distant. It walked two paces behind him, in public, in his own family.**

## 3. "His wealth and his children will not benefit him" (111:2)

The word in the ayah is *kasab* — literally *what he earned*. **Ibn Abbas** and others read it as **his children**, and the same is reported from **Aishah, Mujahid, Ata', Al-Hasan and Ibn Sirin**.

Why that reading matters is clear from what **Ibn Mas'ud** preserves. When the Prophet ﷺ called his people to faith, Abu Lahab said:

> **"Even if what my nephew says is true, I will ransom myself from the painful torment on the Day of Judgement with my wealth and my children."**

So the verse is not a general moral about materialism. It is a **direct answer to a specific boast** — the two assets he named, refused by name. He treated the Hereafter as a market with a settlement price. The verse tells him his currency is not accepted there.

## 4. The Fire (111:3)

> *He will enter a Fire full of flames.*

Ibn Kathir: **it has flames, evil, and severe burning.**

---

## 5. His Wife (111:4)

> *And his wife too, who carries wood.*

She was **Umm Jamil** — **Arwah bint Harb bin Umayyah**, sister of **Abu Sufyan**, and one of the **leading women of the Quraysh**.

Ibn Kathir's judgement on her is precise about the logic of her punishment:

> She was **supportive of her husband in his disbelief, rejection and obstinacy. Therefore, she will be helping to administer his punishment** in the fire of Hell on the Day of Judgement.

Meaning: **she will carry the firewood and throw it upon her husband**, to increase what he is already in — *and she will be ready and prepared to do so*. The partnership does not dissolve at death. It is continued, in the same roles, with the same enthusiasm, on the other side of the reckoning.

---

## 6. The Rope of Masad (111:5)

> *In her neck is a twisted rope of Masad.*

Three readings are preserved, and Ibn Kathir gives all of them:

| Source | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Mujahid & Urwah** | *"From the palm fibre of the Fire."* |
| **Al-Awfi ← Ibn Abbas**, with **Atiyah Al-Jadali, Ad-Dahhak, Ibn Zayd** | **She used to place thorns in the path of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ** — so the thorn-bundle she carried in this life becomes the load she carries in the next. |
| **Mujahid** (a second view) | **"A collar of iron."** Ibn Kathir supports it: don't you see that the Arabs call a **pulley cable** a *masad*? |

**Al-Jawhari** on the word itself: *Al-Masad* is fibre — a rope made of palm fibre or leaves, and also made from the skins or fur of camels. *Masadtul-habla* means to **fasten its twine tightly**.

The image is deliberately humiliating for a noblewoman of Quraysh: a labourer's rope, or a slave's iron collar, around the neck of a woman who ends the surah's own story by declaring herself *the daughter of their leader*.

## 7. Umm Jamil Comes Looking for the Prophet ﷺ

The closing narration, from **Asma' bint Abi Bakr** (via Ibn Abi Hatim ← Al-Humaydi ← Sufyan ← Al-Walid bin Kathir ← Ibn Tadrus):

When *Perish the two hands of Abu Lahab* was revealed, **the one-eyed Umm Jamil came out wailing, a stone in her hand**, chanting a rhyme of her own against him:

> *He criticizes our father, and his religion is our scorn, and his command is to disobey us.*

The Prophet ﷺ was sitting in the Masjid with **Abu Bakr**, who saw her coming and said, *"O Messenger of Allah, she is coming and I fear that she will see you."*

He replied:

> **"Verily, she will not see me."**

Then he recited Qur'an as a protection — as Allah says:

> *And when you recite the Qur'an, We put between you and those who believe not in the Hereafter, an invisible veil.* **(17:45)**

**She advanced until she was standing directly in front of Abu Bakr — and did not see the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.**

She said: *"O Abu Bakr, I have been informed that your friend is making defamatory poetry about me."*

Abu Bakr: *"Nay! By the Lord of this House, he is not defaming you."*

She turned away saying: *"Indeed the Quraysh know that I am the daughter of their leader."*

### The coda

In another version of the hadith, Umm Jamil **stumbled over her waist-gown while making Tawaf** around the Ka'bah, and said, *"Cursed be the reviler."*

**Umm Hakim bint Abdul-Muttalib** — the Prophet's ﷺ aunt — answered her:

> *"I am a chaste woman so I will not speak abusively, and I am refined so I do not know. Both of us are children of the same uncle. And after all, the Quraysh know best."*

A reply that concedes nothing and insults no one, delivered by a woman who could have matched her curse for curse. It is the last word in the surah's tafsir, and it is the counter-portrait to everything the surah condemns.

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**Blood is not a shield, and status is not a currency.** The man cursed here was the Prophet's ﷺ own uncle, of the noblest house in Arabia, wealthy and well-sonned; his wife was a leading lady of Quraysh and the sister of Abu Sufyan. Every advantage the world recognises, they had — and the surah passes over all of it in one line: *his wealth and his children will not benefit him.*

**What condemned them was not disbelief alone, but the choice to make a career of obstruction.** He followed the Prophet ﷺ through markets to heckle him; she laid thorns in his path. So the punishment is delivered in the shape of the crime — the thorn-carrier carries fuel, the Father of Flame receives the flame, and the two of them are kept together, still working as a team.

**And the surah is a wager left standing in public.** Abu Lahab was told, in verses recited across Makkah, that he was going to the Fire. He had years to say a single sentence and make the Qur'an look foolish. He never did.
