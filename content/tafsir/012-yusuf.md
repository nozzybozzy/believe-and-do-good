*Revealed in Makkah · 111 ayat · the only surah that tells one story from beginning to end*

---

## 1. Why This Story, and Why Now

The Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ to narrate to them, and Allah answered with **"We relate to you the best of stories."** Ibn Kathir pauses on the wording of the opening: the most honourable Book, in the most honourable language, to the most honourable Prophet, brought by the most honourable angel, in the most honourable land, beginning in the most honourable month. **The Qur'an is perfect in every respect** — and therefore sufficient without any book beside it.

He places here the incident of Umar bin Al-Khattab bringing a page copied from the Tawrah. The Prophet's ﷺ face changed with anger:

> *"Are you uncertain about it, Ibn Al-Khattab? By the One in Whose Hand is my soul, I have come to you with it white and pure. Do not ask them about anything — they might tell you something true and you reject it, or something false and you believe it. By the One in Whose Hand is my soul, if Musa were living, he would have no choice but to follow me."*

So the story that follows is not entertainment. It is revelation of the unseen, given to a Prophet who was being plotted against by his own people — about a Prophet who was plotted against by his own brothers, and raised above them in the end.

---

## 2. The Dream, and the Wisdom of Silence

Yusuf sees eleven stars, the sun and the moon prostrating to him. **The dreams of Prophets are revelation** (Ibn Abbas). The stars are his eleven brothers; the sun and moon, his parents. The vision came true forty years later — or eighty, by another account — at 12:100.

Ya'qub's first instinct is protective secrecy: *do not relate your vision to your brothers, lest they plot against you.* Ibn Kathir gathers the Sunnah around this counsel:

- A good dream may be told; a disliked one should be left untold, with a turn to the other side, three blows to the left, and refuge sought in Allah — **"it will not harm him."**
- **"The dream is tied to a bird's leg as long as it is not interpreted; if it is interpreted, it comes true."** Hence conceal a coming bounty until it arrives.
- **"Earn help for fulfilling needs by being discreet — for every owner of a blessing is envied."**

Ya'qub reads the dream immediately: Allah will *choose* you, teach you the interpretation of events, and complete His favour on you as He completed it on Ibrahim and Ishaq.

---

## 3. Envy, and the Pit

The brothers' complaint is the whole engine of the story: *Yusuf and his brother are dearer to our father than we, while we are a group.* Note what envy does to language — they call a Prophet's love **"a plain error."**

They debate murder, then exile, then settle on the well. One of them — Rubil (Reuben), or Yahudha, or Sham'un, by different reports — pulls them back from killing. Ibn Kathir observes they *intended repentance before committing the sin* (**"and after that you will be righteous folk"**), and quotes Ibn Ishaq's summary of the crime: it severed the ties of the womb, wronged a father who was old and honoured by Allah, and was merciless to a child who was young, weak and innocent.

Yet the plot could not have succeeded, **because Allah had already decreed that Yusuf would be a Prophet and would govern Egypt.**

In the darkness of the well, revelation comes: *you shall one day inform them of this affair, when they know you not.* Ibn Kathir marks this as the pattern of the whole surah — **Allah's comfort arrives inside the distress, not only after it.**

The brothers return at nightfall, weeping, with a shirt stained with false blood. They slaughtered a sheep for it — **but forgot to tear the shirt.** Ya'qub's reply is the surah's refrain: *your own selves have enticed you; so beautiful patience — and it is Allah Whose help is sought against what you describe.*

---

## 4. Sold Cheap, Bought by the Right Man

The caravan's water-drawer lowers his bucket and finds a boy. Yusuf is sold for **a bakhs price — a decreased, miserable one; twenty dirhams, two per brother.** They regarded him as insignificant, says Ad-Dahhak, *because they had no knowledge of his prophethood and his rank with Allah.*

Allah knew what they did and could have stopped them — but **His decree prevails and His appointed destiny rules.** Ibn Kathir turns this directly to the Prophet ﷺ: Allah likewise knows what Quraysh does to you, gives them respite, and will give you victory as He gave Yusuf victory.

The buyer is the Aziz, minister of Egypt, who tells his wife to honour the boy. Ibn Mas'ud named **three people of the sharpest insight**: the Aziz here; the woman of Madyan who said *"O my father, hire him"*; and Abu Bakr, when he appointed Umar.

---

## 5. The Seduction, and the Sign He Was Shown

She locks the doors and calls him. His answer is instant: **"I seek refuge in Allah! Truly he is my Rabb"** — your husband is my master, who honoured me; I will not betray him. *Verily, the wrongdoers never succeed.*

On *"she desired him, and he would have desired her had he not seen the burhan of his Lord"*: Ibn Kathir cites Al-Baghawi that this concerns a thought that crossed the mind, and attaches the hadith of intentions — an evil act intended but abandoned **for Allah's sake is written as a good deed.** As for what the burhan actually was, opinions differ wildly, and Ibn Jarir's verdict is quoted approvingly: it was a sign from Allah that repelled the thought; **there is no clear proof for any specific account, so leave it vague as Allah left it.**

The race to the door, the shirt torn from the back, the husband at the threshold. Her accusation is instantaneous; Yusuf's defence is plain truth. The witness of her household supplies the logic: a shirt torn from the front convicts him, from the back convicts her. The husband's response is remarkably mild — *turn away from this; and you, ask forgiveness for your sin.*

---

## 6. The Knives, and the Choice of Prison

The women of the city gossip; she answers with a banquet, couches, citron, and a knife for each guest. Yusuf is told to walk out before them. **They cut their hands without feeling it.** *How perfect is Allah! This is no human being; this is none but a noble angel.*

The Prophet ﷺ, meeting Yusuf in the third heaven on the Night of Isra', said: **he was given half of all beauty.**

Then the threat: obey, or prison and disgrace. And Yusuf's choice:

> *"My Lord, prison is dearer to me than that to which they invite me. Unless You turn away their plot from me, I will incline towards them and be one of the ignorant."*

Ibn Kathir draws out what this du'a admits: **if left to myself I have no power over myself.** Immunity is not self-possession; it is Allah's protection asked for by name. And so *his Lord answered him.*

He places here the hadith of the seven Allah shades on the Day when there is no shade but His — including **a man called by a woman of beauty and rank who says: "I fear Allah."** Yusuf was young, beautiful, enslaved, and she was the wife of the Aziz — wealthy, beautiful, powerful. He refused all of it. **This is the highest grade of the case.**

---

## 7. Prison: Da'wah Before Interpretation

Two young men bring him their dreams. Before he interprets a single image, **he calls them to tawhid** — telling them first that this knowledge is Allah's gift, given because he abandoned the religion of those who disbelieve and followed Ibrahim, Ishaq and Ya'qub:

> *"O my two companions of the prison! Are many different lords better, or Allah, the One, the Irresistible? You worship nothing besides Him but names you and your fathers invented... The command is for none but Allah. That is the straight religion, but most men know not."*

Ibn Kathir's comment on Yusuf's path is a general principle: **whoever shuns deviation and follows the way of the Messengers, Allah guides his heart, teaches him what he did not know, and makes him an imam who is followed in goodness.**

Only then does he interpret: one will serve wine to his master; one will be crucified. *Thus is the matter decided.* Then the one request he makes of a human being — *mention me to your king* — and Shaytan makes the man forget. Yusuf remains **a few more years** (bida' = between three and nine; seven, said Wahb bin Munabbih).

---

## 8. The King's Dream, and the Man Who Wouldn't Leave

Seven fat cows devoured by seven lean; seven green ears and seven dry. The court calls it a jumble of false dreams. The freed cupbearer remembers at last.

Yusuf interprets without a word of reproach for being forgotten and **without making his release a condition** — seven fertile years, so store the grain in its ears and eat sparingly; seven hard years that will devour what you stored; then a year of rain and relief.

The king sends for him. **Yusuf refuses to leave the prison** until his name is cleared and the women's plot is investigated. On this, the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"If I had stayed in prison as long as Yusuf did, I would have accepted the offer."*

The women testify: *Allah forbid! We know no evil of him.* And the wife of the Aziz confesses openly — *now the truth is manifest; it was I who sought to seduce him, and he is of the truthful* — adding the line Ibn Kathir treats as the strongest reading of the passage, spoken by her about herself:

> *"I do not free myself of blame. Verily the self is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except when my Lord bestows mercy."*

---

## 9. Authority, and How He Asked For It

*"Set me over the storehouses of the land; I am a knowing guardian."* Ibn Kathir defends this plainly: **praising oneself is permitted when one's ability is unknown and there is need** — he offered **hifz** (honest safekeeping) and **'ilm** (competence in the job), which is exactly what the coming famine required.

*Thus did We establish Yusuf in the land — he could take possession wherever he willed. We bestow Our mercy on whom We will, and We do not waste the reward of the doers of good.* Allah did not let the patience of the well or the patience of the prison go to waste. **And the reward of the Hereafter is better still** — the authority was the smaller of the two gifts.

Yusuf fed a nation on one meal a day, himself included, and gave no family more than a camel's load. Mujahid held that **the king of Egypt accepted Islam at Yusuf's hands.**

---

## 10. The Brothers Return

They come to buy food and do not recognise the minister; **he recognises them at once.** The famine had reached Kana'an, and Ya'qub kept Binyamin back.

Yusuf's strategy unfolds in stages: the demand that they bring their brother; their own money slipped back into their bags; Ya'qub's binding oath in Allah's name; and the instruction to **enter by different gates** — a precaution against the evil eye, *which is true and does harm by Allah's decree* — followed immediately by the disclaimer that no precaution averts Allah's decree: **"the decision rests only with Allah; in Him I put my trust."**

Then the private reunion — *I am your brother, so grieve not* — and the cup placed in Binyamin's bag. The brothers themselves pronounce the sentence (the law of Ibrahim: the thief is given up as a slave), and it binds them.

> *Thus did We plan for Yusuf. He could not have taken his brother under the king's law, except that Allah willed it.*

Ibn Kathir: **this is a plot Allah loves and prefers, because it seeks a real good by wisdom.** And on *"above every possessor of knowledge is one more knowing"* — Al-Hasan: there is no scholar but there is one above him, until it ends at Allah. Ibn Abbas corrected a man who read it otherwise: **Allah is the All-Knowing, and His knowledge is above the knowledge of every knowledgeable person.**

The brothers' old wound shows itself: *if he steals, a brother of his stole before him.* Yusuf swallows the reply and keeps it inside himself.

---

## 11. Ya'qub's Grief — the Model of Sabr

He says exactly what he said the first time: **"your own selves have enticed you; so beautiful patience."** He turns away and says *"Alas, my grief for Yusuf!"*, and **loses his sight from the sorrow he was suppressing** — Qatadah notes he suppressed it and complained to no created being.

His sons tell him he will destroy himself remembering Yusuf. His answer is the definition of permitted grief:

> *"I only complain of my anguish and my sorrow to Allah, and I know from Allah what you do not know."*

Ibn Abbas: what he knew was that **Yusuf's dream was true and Allah would certainly bring it to pass.** Then the command that closes the section, and perhaps the single most quoted line of the surah:

> *"O my sons, go and inquire about Yusuf and his brother, and never despair of Allah's mercy. None despairs of Allah's mercy except the disbelieving people."*

---

## 12. Recognition, and Forgiveness Without Reproach

They come back destitute — *a hard time has hit us; we have brought poor capital; give us full measure and be charitable to us.* And he asks the question that ends the concealment: **"Do you know what you did with Yusuf and his brother, when you were ignorant?"**

Ibn Kathir notes he hid himself by Allah's command and revealed himself by Allah's command — and that **when the hardship reached its hardest, the relief was sent**: *verily with hardship comes ease.*

*"I am Yusuf, and this is my brother. Allah has been gracious to us. Whoever has taqwa and is patient — Allah does not waste the reward of the doers of good."*

They confess: *Allah has preferred you above us, and we were sinners.* His reply is the moral summit of the story:

> *"No reproach on you this day. May Allah forgive you — and He is the Most Merciful of those who show mercy."*

He does not merely drop the charge; **he makes du'a for them.**

---

## 13. The Shirt, the Scent, the Prostration

The shirt goes back to Kana'an — carried, said As-Suddi, by **Yahudha, who had brought the false blood-stained shirt, and wanted to erase that sin with this good act.** Before it arrives, Ya'qub says: *I sense the scent of Yusuf, if you do not think me senile.* Ibn Abbas: **the wind carried it to him from eight days' distance.** His own sons answer him harshly — *you are in your old error* — words, says Qatadah, that should never have been said to a father, let alone to a Prophet of Allah.

The shirt is cast on his face and his sight returns: **"Did I not tell you I know from Allah what you do not know?"**

The sons ask him to seek forgiveness for them; he promises it — and several of the Salaf report that **he delayed the du'a to the last part of the night.**

Then Egypt, the throne, and the prostration of eleven brothers and two parents. Yusuf: *this is the interpretation of my dream; my Lord has made it come true.* He recounts the favours and names the enmity's true author — **Shaytan sowed discord between me and my brothers** — declining to blame them even at the moment of vindication.

On the prostration: it was lawful as a greeting of honour in the earlier laws, **from Adam to 'Isa, and abolished in ours.** When Mu'adh prostrated to the Prophet ﷺ after seeing it done in Sham, he was told: *if I were to order anyone to prostrate to another, I would have ordered a wife to her husband* — and no one is to be prostrated to but Allah.

Every worldly thing is now complete. So he asks for the only thing that matters:

> *"My Lord, You have given me sovereignty and taught me the interpretation of events. Originator of the heavens and the earth, You are my Protector in this world and the next. **Cause me to die as a Muslim, and join me with the righteous.**"*

Ibn Kathir notes it may have been said as he was dying — as the Prophet ﷺ, dying, raised his finger and said thrice: *O Allah, to the Highest Company.*

---

## 14. The Closing Arguments

The surah leaves the narrative and speaks to Makkah directly:

- **This is news of the unseen.** You were not there when they agreed on their plot — *We revealed it to you.*
- **Most people will not believe, however eagerly you desire it** — and you ask them no wage for it.
- **They pass by signs and turn away.** Ibn Kathir opens out into the created world here: stars, planets, neighbouring plots of fertile earth, mountains, seas, deserts, living things and dead, fruits alike in form and different in taste and colour — all of it passed by daily and unregarded.
- **"Most of them believe not in Allah except that they associate partners with Him."** They will say Allah created the heavens — and then worship another beside Him. Ibn Kathir extends this to the *hidden* shirk: Al-Hasan's reading of the hypocrite who prays to be seen; swearing by other than Allah; amulets and charms; belief in omens — *"and Allah dissipates it with tawakkul."*
- **"Say: this is my way. I call to Allah with basirah — I and whoever follows me."** With sure knowledge and evidence, not imitation.
- **The Messengers were always men, from among the people of towns** — not angels, not desert-dwellers; they ate food and walked in markets.
- On *"until, when the Messengers gave up hope and thought that they were denied"* — Ibn Kathir records the debate at length. A'ishah's reading was *kudhdhibu*, "they were **betrayed**" by their people, and she rejected outright the idea that a Messenger could suspect his Lord: **"Allah forbid — the Messengers never suspected their Lord of such a thing."** Ibn Abbas and Ibn Mas'ud read *kudhibu*, referring it to the Messengers despairing of their people's belief, and the people thinking the Messengers had been given no true promise. Either way the verse ends the same: **"then Our help came to them."**

And the closing verse of the surah is the reason the story was told at all:

> *Indeed, in their stories there is a lesson for people of understanding. It is not an invented tale, but a confirmation of what came before it, a detailed explanation of everything, and a guidance and mercy for a people who believe.*

---

## 15. In One Breath

Surah Yusuf is a single narrative arc showing that **Allah's decree moves through the very acts intended to defeat it.** The pit, the sale, the false accusation, the forgotten prisoner — each is a step down that turns out to be a step towards the throne. Around that arc Ibn Kathir gathers a practical ethic: conceal your blessings from envy; speak the truth even when it costs you; ask Allah for protection rather than trusting your own restraint; do da'wah before you do favours; do not make your innocence negotiable; do not waste vindication on revenge; grieve to Allah alone; and **never, ever despair of His mercy — that despair belongs only to the disbelievers.**

It ends where a life should end: with the man who has everything asking only to die a Muslim and be gathered with the righteous.
