*Revealed in Madinah · 73 ayat · "The Confederates" — named for the coalition of tribes that besieged Al-Madinah in 5 AH*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Al-Ahzab is the surah in which a community under siege is remade from the inside. Its outer event is the Battle of the Trench: ten thousand men encircling a city of a few thousand, a treaty betrayed from within, and a deliverance that came without a battle. But Ibn Kathir's commentary shows that the siege is only half the work. The other half is a series of demolitions of inherited custom — adoption, the naming of children, Zihar, the informality of a Prophet's household — each replaced with something the Ummah could carry permanently.

The surah moves between three registers that keep interrupting each other: **the battlefield** (who fled, who stayed, who spoke), **the household** (the wives, the Hijab, the marriage of Zaynab), and **the covenant** (from the pledge taken from the Prophets in 33:7 to the Amanah that the mountains refused in 33:72). By the end, the trench and the trust are the same subject: what a person does with a responsibility that frightens him.

---

## 1. One Man, One Heart (33:4–5)

Before Allah argues a point, He gives a physical fact: **no man has two hearts inside him.** Ibn Kathir treats this as the anchor for three abolitions delivered in a single breath — a wife is not made a mother by the words of Zihar, and an adopted son is not made a son by being called one. *"That is but your saying with your mouths"*: the adoption is words, and a child cannot have two fathers just as a man cannot have two hearts.

There is a second, humbler occasion of revelation preserved from Ibn Abbas: the Prophet ﷺ once trembled while standing in prayer, and the hypocrites praying behind him said he must have two hearts, one with you and one with them. The verse answered them and then went far past them.

**Call them by their fathers.** Abdullah bin Umar's testimony is that Zayd bin Harithah was called *Zayd bin Muhammad* until this was revealed. The command restores lineage where it is known; where it is not, the person is a brother in faith or a freed servant. And it forgives the honest mistake — *there is no sin on you concerning that in which you made a mistake* — which Ibn Kathir pairs with the hadith that a judge who exerts himself and errs still has one reward.

Two graceful footnotes: calling someone "my son" in affection is untouched by this (the Prophet ﷺ said *"O my sons"* to the boys of Banu Abd Al-Muttalib at the Jamarat), and the dispute over Hamzah's orphaned daughter — where Ali, Zayd and Jafar each claimed her — ended with the ruling that **the maternal aunt has the status of the mother**, and with a sentence for each man: *you belong to me and I belong to you* (Ali), *you resemble me in looks and character* (Jafar), *you are our brother and our freed servant* (Zayd).

---

## 2. Closer Than Themselves (33:6)

*The Prophet is closer to the believers than themselves, and his wives are their mothers.*

Ibn Kathir reads this as a statement about authority and about love. His judgement outranks their own preference for themselves — and the love is not optional. Umar told him, *"You are dearer to me than everything except myself."* He said, *"No, O Umar, not until I am dearer to you than yourself."* Umar said it again, correctly. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Now, O Umar."*

The same ayah quietly ends the inheritance that had been established between Muhajir and Ansar by the bond of brotherhood: blood relatives now inherit, but the duty of kindness, support and good counsel between the brothers remains.

---

## 3. The Covenant Before the Siege (33:7–8)

Allah recalls the pledge taken from all the Prophets, naming five — Muhammad first, as a token of honour, then Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa and 'Isa. Placed here, just before the account of the Trench, it frames what follows: a besieged community is standing inside a very old agreement, and the question the siege asks is only whether *they* will keep their end of it.

---

## 4. The Trench (33:9–27)

**How it started.** Leaders of Banu Nadir, expelled to Khaybar, went to Makkah and incited Quraysh, then to Ghatafan. The coalition numbered close to **ten thousand**. On Salman Al-Farisi's advice the Muslims dug a ditch around Al-Madinah from the east; the Prophet ﷺ carried earth and dug alongside them. The Muslims were perhaps three thousand, backs to the mountain of Sal', women and children in the strongholds. Then **Banu Qurayzah broke their treaty** — Huyay bin Akhtab wore down their chief Ka'b bin Asad, who told him, *"By Allah, this is the opportunity for humiliation… you are a bad omen"*, and agreed anyway.

**What it did to people.** The Qur'an's own description is unsparing: *the eyes grew wild and the hearts reached to the throats.* Ibn Ishaq records that Mu'attib bin Qushayr said out loud that Muhammad had promised them the treasures of Chosroes and Caesar while none of them could safely leave to relieve himself. Al-Hasan's summary of the same moment is the sharper one: the hypocrites thought the Muslims would be wiped out, while **the believers were certain that what was promised was true.** Two men, one situation, opposite readings.

The hypocrites' words are catalogued precisely — *"there is no position for you, go back"*; *"our homes lie open"* (and they lay not open, *they but wished to flee*); they *smite you with sharp tongues* when the danger passes, claiming they were the bravest all along. Qatadah's line: cowards in battle, first in line for the booty.

**Hudhayfah's night.** In Muslim's report, a man tells Hudhayfah he wishes he had been there to fight. Hudhayfah asks whether he really would have. On a bitterly cold night the Prophet ﷺ asked three times for a volunteer to bring news of the enemy, promising that man his company on the Day of Resurrection — and no one moved. Called by name, Hudhayfah went, walked into the camp, saw Abu Sufyan warming his back at a fire, drew an arrow — and put it back, because he had been told *do not alarm them*.

**The deliverance.** An easterly wind, intensely cold, tore out their fires and tents, and angels *that you saw not* threw terror into their hearts; the tribes scattered. *Allah sufficed for the believers in the fighting* — no battle was needed. The Prophet's ﷺ words afterward: *He was true to His promise, He helped His servant, He gave might to His soldiers, and He defeated the Confederates alone.* And: **"Now we will attack them and they will not attack us"** — the turning of the war's whole direction.

**The two verses the surah rests on.** In the middle of the panic comes 33:21: *Indeed in the Messenger of Allah you have a good example to follow.* Ibn Kathir calls this an important principle — a general command to follow him in word and deed, revealed here as an instruction in patience under pressure. And 33:23, *among the believers are men who have been true to their covenant*, which Anas bin Malik's family read as being about his uncle **Anas bin An-Nadr**, who had missed Badr and at Uhud told Sa'd bin Mu'adh, *"I can smell the fragrance of Paradise near Uhud"*, and was found with eighty-odd wounds, recognisable only by his fingertips.

**Banu Qurayzah.** With the siege lifted and weapons set down, Jibril came: *the angels have not put down their weapons.* The Prophet ﷺ marched at once, saying *no one should pray 'Asr except at Banu Qurayzah* — and when the time came on the road, some prayed and some waited, and **neither group was rebuked**, a precedent Ibn Kathir lets stand without comment. After twenty-five days they chose Sa'd bin Mu'adh as judge, expecting leniency from a former ally. Sa'd — dying of an arrow wound, having prayed not to die until he was satisfied concerning them — ruled that the fighters be killed and the women and children taken captive. The Prophet ﷺ said: *You have judged according to the ruling of Allah from above the seven heavens.*

## 5. The Choice (33:28–34)

The wives had asked for more of the world's provision than the household had. Rather than a rebuke, Allah gave them **the choice**: the life of this world and a handsome release, or Allah, His Messenger, and the Hereafter.

The Prophet ﷺ began with A'ishah, and told her not to hurry — *consult your parents first.* She said: *"Concerning what do I need to consult my parents? I choose Allah and His Messenger and the Home of the Hereafter"*, and asked him not to tell the others what she had chosen. He answered: *"Allah did not send me to be harsh, but to teach in a gentle and easy manner"* — he would tell any who asked. **Every one of them chose as she had.**

What follows is the logic of that choice made into law. Their reward for sin would be doubled and their reward for obedience doubled, *because their status is so high*; they are not like other women; they are not to soften their speech to men *lest he in whose heart is a disease should be moved with desire*; they are to keep to their houses and not display themselves as in the Jahiliyyah. Then Allah's own reason for all of it: *Allah wishes only to remove Ar-Rijs from you, O members of the family, and to purify you with a thorough purification.*

On **Ahl al-Bayt**, Ibn Kathir is careful. The wives are unambiguously included — the context is addressed to them, and Ikrimah swore publicly on it. But he also records A'ishah's account of the cloak, in which the Prophet ﷺ gathered Al-Hasan, Al-Husayn, Fatimah and Ali under a striped cloak and recited this verse, and Zayd bin Arqam's report from Ghadir Khumm, where the Prophet ﷺ said *"remember Allah with regard to the members of my family"* three times. His conclusion: **they were the reason for revelation, and others are included by generalisation.**

---

## 6. The Ten Pairs (33:35)

Umm Salamah asked the Prophet ﷺ plainly: *"Why is it that we are not mentioned in the Qur'an as men are?"* She was combing her hair when she heard him announcing the answer from the Minbar.

The verse names ten qualities, each in the masculine and the feminine, and Ibn Kathir takes them one at a time — Islam and then **Iman** (which is more specific: the Bedouin who say "we believe" are told faith has not yet entered their hearts); **Qunut**, obedience in stillness; **truthfulness**, which leads to righteousness and to Paradise, while lying leads to immorality and to Hell; **patience**, hardest at the first stroke of a disaster; **Khushu'**, serenity born of knowing you are watched — *worship Allah as if you see Him*; **charity**, best when the left hand does not know what the right gives; **fasting**, "the Zakah of the body"; **chastity**; and **remembering Allah much**. A man who wakes his wife at night and prays two rak'ahs with her is written down that night among *the men and the women who remember Allah much*.

---

## 7. No Option Once It Is Decided (33:36)

The reason for revelation is one of the most affecting stories in the tafsir. **Julaybib** was a man of no standing whatsoever. The Prophet ﷺ proposed marriage on his behalf to an Ansari girl; her mother refused outright. The girl, overhearing, asked: *"Are you refusing to follow the command of the Messenger of Allah? Follow his command, for I will not come to any harm."*

Later, after a battle, the Prophet ﷺ asked whether anyone was missing. They named others. He said, *but I see that Julaybib is missing.* They found him beside seven of the enemy he had killed before he was killed. The Prophet ﷺ stood over him and said, three times: **"He killed seven before he was himself killed. He belongs to me and I belong to him."** He carried him in his arms and placed him in the grave. He prayed for the widow: *"O Allah, pour blessings upon her and do not make her life hard."* Thabit's postscript: there was **no widow among the Ansar more sought after in marriage** than she was.

---

## 8. Zayd, Zaynab, and the Seal (33:37–40)

Zayd's marriage to Zaynab bint Jahsh, the Prophet's cousin, was in trouble. He came complaining; the Prophet ﷺ told him, *"Keep your wife to yourself, and have Taqwa of Allah"* — while, as the verse says, concealing what Allah would make manifest, and fearing the people when Allah had more right to be feared. A'ishah's remark on this is famous: **if the Prophet ﷺ were ever to have concealed anything of revelation, he would have concealed this verse.**

When the 'Iddah ended, he sent Zayd himself to propose on his behalf. Zayd said he could not even look at her as he spoke. Zaynab would not answer until she had prayed — and while she prayed, the Qur'an came down giving her in marriage. She later told the other wives: *"Your families arranged your marriages; Allah arranged mine from above the seven heavens."*

Ibn Kathir is explicit about why this happened publicly: *so that there may be no difficulty to the believers in respect of the wives of their adopted sons.* The abolition announced in 33:4 had to be enacted, not merely stated, and it was enacted at the Prophet's ﷺ own expense — *there is no blame on the Prophet in that which Allah has made legal for him.*

Then the verse that closes the whole subject: **Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but he is the Messenger of Allah and the last of the Prophets.** No son of his lived to adulthood. And the finality is stated in Mutawatir hadith through many Companions, most memorably as the **brick**: a house built beautifully and complete but for the space of one brick, and people walking around it saying *if only that brick were in place.* "Among the Prophets, I am like that brick." Ibn Kathir adds that prophethood has ended but *Al-Mubashshirat* — the true dream of a Muslim — remain, and that every claimant after him, from Musaylimah to the Dajjal, is exposed by the falsehood of his own conduct.

## 9. Remember Him, and He Remembers You (33:41–48)

*Remember Allah with much remembrance.* Ibn Abbas notes that Allah set limits for every obligation and accepted the excuses of those with valid excuses — **except Dhikr**, for which He set no limit and accepts no excuse from anyone not physically prevented. Standing, sitting, lying down; by land and sea; in wealth and poverty; in sickness and health. A Bedouin asked for one thing he could hold on to and was told: *"Keep your tongue moist with the remembrance of Allah."*

Then the extraordinary reciprocity of 33:43: *He it is Who sends Salah on you, and His angels too, that He may bring you out from darkness into light.* Allah's Salah is His praise of the servant before the angels; the angels' Salah is their supplication for him. Ibn Kathir illustrates the mercy behind it with the mother among the prisoners of war who snatched up her child and nursed him, and the Prophet's ﷺ question: would she throw her child into the fire? **"By Allah, Allah is more merciful towards His servants than this woman is to her child."**

The Prophet's ﷺ own titles follow — witness, bearer of glad tidings, warner, one who invites to Allah by His leave, and **a lamp spreading light**. Abdullah bin 'Amr's account of his description in the Tawrah is quoted at length: *not harsh or severe, or noisy in the marketplaces; he does not repay evil with evil, but overlooks and forgives*; through him blind eyes, deaf ears and sealed hearts will be opened.

## 10. The Hijab, and the Etiquette of a House (33:53–59)

This is the ayah of Hijab, and Ibn Kathir opens with Umar's report that his view coincided with his Lord's in several matters — one being his suggestion that the Mothers of the Believers be screened.

The occasion was mundane and human. At the wedding feast for Zaynab, three men stayed on talking long after the food was finished. The Prophet ﷺ was too shy to ask them to leave; he got up, walked the round of his wives' apartments greeting each one, came back, and they were still there. Then the verse: *enter when invited, and when you have taken your meal, disperse without sitting for a talk. Verily, such behaviour annoys the Prophet, and he is shy of asking you to go; but Allah is not shy of telling you the truth.* Ibn Kathir also draws from it a prohibition on hovering while food is cooked — the Arabs called it *Tatfil* — noting that Al-Khatib Al-Baghdadi wrote an entire book against the practice.

From the same passage: requests are to be made from behind a screen, *purer for your hearts and for their hearts*; the Prophet ﷺ is not to be annoyed; and his wives may never be married after him. 33:55 then lists the relatives before whom no Hijab is required, and Ibn Kathir preserves Ash-Sha'bi's reason for the absence of uncles from the list — they may describe her to their sons.

33:59 extends the command outward: *tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to draw their Jalabib over their bodies… that will be better, that they should be known so as not to be annoyed.* The Jilbab, he explains, is the outer wrapper worn over the Khimar; Ibn Abbas and Ubaydah As-Salmani describe covering the face and head with only one eye left showing. The stated purpose is **recognition and protection**, not concealment for its own sake.

## 11. Sending Salah Upon Him (33:56)

*Allah sends His Salah on the Prophet, and also His angels. O you who believe! Send your Salah on him, and greet him with Taslim.*

The Companions asked how, since they already knew the Salam — and were taught the words still said in every prayer, *as You sent Your Salah upon the family of Ibrahim.* Ibn Kathir then gathers the encouragements: whoever sends one Salah receives ten from Allah; a supplication hangs suspended between heaven and earth until Salah is sent upon the Prophet ﷺ, so put it at the beginning, the middle and the end; *the miser is the one in whose presence I am mentioned and he does not send Salah upon me.* Ubayy bin Ka'b asked how much of his own prayer to devote to it and kept raising his offer — a quarter, a half, two thirds — hearing each time, *whatever you want, but more is better for you* — until he offered all of it, and was told: **"Then this would be sufficient to relieve your distress and earn you forgiveness of your sins."**

Friday is singled out: *send plenty of Salah upon me on this day, for your Salah will be presented to me* — and when asked how, after his body has returned to the earth, he answered that Allah has forbidden the earth to consume the bodies of the Prophets.

---

## 12. Do Not Be Like Those Who Annoyed Musa (33:69–71)

Al-Bukhari's report: Musa was so modest he would never let his skin be seen, and the Children of Israel decided he must be hiding a disease. The rock that ran off with his garment ended the slander by exposing him to a crowd — who found him *the best of what Allah had created*.

Ibn Kathir attaches to it the moment Ibn Mas'ud reported a man saying a distribution of booty had not been made for Allah's sake. The Prophet's ﷺ face reddened, and he said: **"May Allah have mercy on Musa. He was annoyed with worse than this, yet he remained patient."**

Then the small, complete instruction of 33:70–71: *have Taqwa of Allah and speak the truth* — straight speech, with no crookedness in it — and the promise attached, that He will set your deeds right and forgive what is past.

---

## 13. The Trust the Mountains Refused (33:72–73)

*Truly, We did offer the Amanah to the heavens and the earth, and the mountains, but they declined to bear it and were afraid of it. But man bore it. Verily, he was unjust and ignorant.*

Ibn Abbas's account: Allah offered it to Adam, who asked what it involved. *If you do good you will be rewarded, and if you do evil you will be punished.* He took it. The mountains had refused **not out of sin but out of reverence** — fear that they could not discharge it. Man agreed, weak, ignorant and unjust as he is.

What the Amanah is, the Salaf answered variously and Ibn Kathir declares the answers compatible: the obligatory duties (Ibn Abbas), obedience, religion and its prescribed limits (Qatadah), prayer and fasting and purification (Zayd bin Aslam), a woman's guarding of her own chastity (Ubayy bin Ka'b). All of them mean the same thing — **responsibility, accepted with reward and punishment attached.**

Hudhayfah's hadith on how it leaves is the surah's most disquieting image: Amanah was rooted in men's hearts, and it will be taken away — a man sleeps and it is lifted from his heart, leaving a mark like the blister a hot coal makes, swollen but empty. Then people trade and hardly anyone is trustworthy, until it is said of a whole tribe that it contains one honest man, and it is said of a man *how strong, how clever, how fine* — and there is not a mustard seed of faith in him.

The surah ends by naming what the Amanah sorts out: the hypocrites and idolaters, men and women, are punished; the believers, men and women, are pardoned. *And Allah is Ever Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**Al-Ahzab is about what holds when everything is pulling.** Ten thousand men at the ditch, a treaty broken behind you, the cold, the fear — and the only difference between the man who said *we were promised the treasures of Chosroes* and the man who said *this is what Allah and His Messenger promised us* was what each already carried inside.

The surah then shows that the same test runs through ordinary life, at a lower volume: whether you will give a child back his father's name; whether you will marry your daughter to Julaybib because the Messenger ﷺ asked; whether you will leave a house when the meal is over; whether you choose the Hereafter when a handsome settlement is on the table. Every one of these is the Amanah in miniature — a responsibility the mountains found too heavy, which a human being picked up.

And the surah's answer to how anyone survives carrying it is given twice, in the middle of the panic and again in the middle of the peace: **you have in the Messenger of Allah a good example**, and **keep your tongue moist with the remembrance of Allah.**
