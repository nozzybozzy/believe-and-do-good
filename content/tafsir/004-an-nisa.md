*Revealed in Madinah · 176 ayat · "The Women"*

---

## 0. Its Standing

Ibn Mas'ud said there were **five ayat in this surah he would prefer to the world and everything in it**, and he named them:

| Ayah | What it says |
|---|---|
| **4:40** | Allah does not wrong anyone by an atom's weight |
| **4:31** | Avoid the major sins and the minor ones are remitted |
| **4:48** | He does not forgive shirk; everything else is within His will |
| **4:64** | Had they come to you and sought forgiveness, they would have found Him Forgiving |
| **4:110** | Whoever does evil, then seeks Allah's forgiveness, will find Him Forgiving, Merciful |

Notice what they have in common: **four of the five are about mercy.** In a surah otherwise dense with law — inheritance shares, prohibited degrees, blood money, rules of war — the verses the Companions treasured most are the ones that leave the door open.

Ibn Abbas, who was still a boy when the Qur'an was being revealed, used to invite questions on it specifically: ask me about An-Nisa'.

And once the Prophet ﷺ asked Ibn Mas'ud to recite to him. Ibn Mas'ud objected — it was revealed to *you*. The Prophet ﷺ said he liked to hear it from others. Ibn Mas'ud recited An-Nisa' until he reached **4:41** — *how will it be when We bring a witness from every nation, and bring you as a witness against these?* — and was told to stop. He looked up and found the Prophet's ﷺ eyes full of tears.

## 1. The Opening: One Soul, One Womb (4:1)

The surah begins by addressing not the believers but **all mankind**, and grounds every law that follows in a single fact: you all come from one person.

- **Taqwa of your Lord** — worship Him alone, without partners.
- **Who created you from a single person** — Adam; and from him his mate, Hawwa'. Ibn Kathir cites the hadith of the rib: it is curved at the top; force it straight and it breaks; leave it and it stays curved. *Live with the curve.*
- **And spread from them many men and women** — scattered across the earth in different forms, colours and tongues, all returning to Him.
- **And revere the wombs** (*al-arham*). The Salaf read this two ways: (a) do not swear by Allah and by kinship and then betray it, and (b) do not sever the ties of kinship — honour them.
- **Allah is ever Watching over you** — paired with the hadith: worship Allah as though you see Him; you do not, but He sees you.

Ibn Kathir draws the thread himself: **Allah mentions the single father and single mother so that people feel compassion for one another and are kind to the weak among them.** That is not a decorative point. It is the premise of the entire surah — orphans, wives, the poor, captives, the persecuted in Makkah. Everything downstream is an application of *one origin, therefore mercy*.

He then records that after reciting this very verse, the Prophet ﷺ — seeing a delegation from Mudar arrive in rags — gave a sermon urging charity: give from your dinar, your dirham, your measure of wheat, your measure of dates.

## 2. Orphans, and the Real Context of Polygamy (4:2–10)

### The property of orphans (4:2)
Hand it over in full at maturity. Two specific frauds are named, because they were actually happening: **swapping your weak sheep for the orphan's fat one** ("a sheep for a sheep"), and **substituting a counterfeit dirham for a good one** ("a dirham for a dirham"). And do not mix their wealth into yours so as to consume both. Ibn Abbas: this is a *great* sin — grave, not technical.

### 4:3 — what this verse is actually about
The famous verse on marrying two, three or four is almost universally quoted out of its context. Ibn Kathir restores it, on the authority of **'A'ishah**, who was asked directly by her nephew 'Urwah:

> A guardian holds a female orphan whose wealth is mixed with his, and whose beauty and money tempt him. He marries her himself — but pays her less than a suitable dowry, less than another suitor would have given. **The verse forbids that.** If you fear you cannot be just to her, marry from the other women, who are many.

So the passage is not primarily a licence but a **restraint on guardians exploiting wards**. Al-Bukhari records the specific case that prompted it: a man married the orphan in his care without desiring her, kept her wealth mixed with his, and withheld her share.

### The limit of four
"Two or three or four" sets a ceiling, not an open list — Ibn Abbas and the majority. The decisive proof is practice: **Ghilan bin Salamah ath-Thaqafi became Muslim with ten wives, and was told to choose four and release the rest.** Ibn Kathir reasons: if a man already married to more than four had to divorce down to four, then marrying more than four in the first place is prohibited a fortiori.

*(He also records the sequel: years later Ghilan divorced his remaining wives and split his wealth among his sons; 'Umar suspected he had heard a whisper of his own imminent death, and ordered him to take back both wives and property.)*

### Then only one
If you fear you will not be just — **one**. And Ibn Kathir immediately cross-references **4:129**: *you will never be able to do perfect justice between wives, however much you desire it.* The word *ta'ulu* was glossed by a long list of the Salaf — Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, al-Hasan, Qatadah, as-Suddi and others — as **to deviate from justice**.

### The dowry as *nihlah* (4:4)
'A'ishah, Qatadah, Muqatil and Ibn Jurayj: *nihlah* means **obligatory** (Ibn Jurayj adds: specified). Ibn Zayd: no one after the Prophet ﷺ may marry without a genuine dowry, nor with false promises about one. Yet it is to be given **with an open hand, like a gift** — and if she freely returns part of it, he may take it lawfully.

### The unwise, and testing orphans (4:5–6)
- Wealth is "a means of support" — so it is not handed to those who cannot manage it. Ibn Kathir lists the grounds: **youth, insanity, weak intellect or religion, and bankruptcy.** (The Salaf differed on who exactly "the unwise" are: children and women, or orphans, or women.)
- **Test the orphans** — test their judgment, until the age of marriage. Puberty is the marker: a wet dream, per the majority; or age fifteen, from Ibn 'Umar being refused at Uhud aged fourteen and accepted at the Trench aged fifteen — "this," said 'Umar bin 'Abdul-'Aziz, "is the line between child and adult."
- Release the property when you find **sound judgment in religion and in money** — and do so **before witnesses**.
- **The guardian's wage:** if he is rich he takes nothing; if poor, he may take what is reasonable for the work he does. The Prophet ﷺ told a poor guardian he could eat from the orphan's wealth without extravagance, without waste, and without mixing it with his own.
- And the warning to Abu Dharr, who was told frankly that he was weak, and loved, and should therefore never take charge of two people nor of an orphan's property.

### Shares, kindness at the division, and fire (4:7–10)
- **4:7** abolishes the pre-Islamic rule outright: the idolators gave inheritance to adult men only. Now **men have a share and women have a share**, small or large, fixed by law.
- **4:8** — when poor relatives who do not inherit, orphans, and the needy are *present* at the division, give them something and speak kindly. Ibn Abbas insisted this verse was **not abrogated** and still applies. Some held it obligatory, others recommended, others that it was superseded. Ibn Abbas's reasoning is worth keeping: these people watch a fortune being parcelled out to others while they stand there with nothing — so Allah, out of compassion, gave them a share in it.
- **4:9** — let executors fear for other people's children as they would fear for their own weak offspring left behind. Ibn Abbas applies it to anyone present when a dying man dictates a will that will harm his rightful heirs: **speak up, and steer him to fairness.**
  - Hence the case of **Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas**, who offered two-thirds of his estate to charity, then half, then a third. *One-third — and even a third is a lot.* Better to leave your heirs wealthy than begging.
- **4:10** — those who consume orphans' wealth unjustly are swallowing fire that will blaze in their stomachs on the Day of Resurrection. This introduces **the seven destructive sins**: shirk, sorcery, unlawful killing, riba, consuming an orphan's wealth, fleeing the battlefield, and slandering chaste believing women.

## 3. The Law of Inheritance (4:11–14)

Ibn 'Uyaynah called the science of *fara'id* **half of knowledge, because it touches every single person.**

### Why it was revealed
The widow of **Sa'd bin ar-Rabi'**, killed at Uhud, came with his two daughters: their uncle had taken everything, and without money they would never marry. The Prophet ﷺ said Allah would decide. The verse came down, and the uncle was ordered to give **two-thirds to the daughters, one-eighth to the mother, and keep the remainder.**

### The core shares

| Heir | Share |
|---|---|
| Son | Twice a daughter's portion |
| One daughter | One half |
| Two or more daughters | Two thirds (shared) |
| Each parent, if there are children | One sixth |
| Mother, if no children and parents are sole heirs | One third (father takes the rest) |
| Mother, if the deceased left siblings | One sixth |
| Husband | One half; one quarter if she left a child |
| Wife (or wives, sharing) | One quarter; one eighth if he left a child |
| Maternal sibling (Kalalah) | One sixth each; two or more share one third |

**Order of settlement:** debts first, then the bequest, then the fixed shares. Ibn Kathir notes the Salaf and later scholars agree on this, "and it is apparent to whoever reads the verse carefully."

### Points Ibn Kathir dwells on

- **Why the male's share is double.** Not as a valuation of persons but of obligations: men must spend on dependants, meet the costs of trade, work and liabilities. And he reads the same verse as evidence of something else entirely — that **Allah is more merciful to children than their own parents are**, since He is the one commanding parents to be fair to them. Hence the scene of the captive woman who found her lost child and nursed him, and the Prophet's ﷺ question: would she throw him into the fire? *Allah is more merciful to His servants than she is to her child.*
- **Why siblings reduce the mother's share but not the father's** — Qatadah's answer, which Ibn Kathir calls sound: it is the father who bears the cost of marrying off the deceased's siblings, not the mother.
- ***You know not which of them is nearer to you in benefit.*** Before this, inheritance ran to children, and parents got only what a will gave them. Allah abolished that and fixed shares for both, because benefit may come from either direction and no one can calculate it.
- **Kalalah.** From *iklil*, the crown that encircles the head — heirs who come round the side rather than up or down. **Abu Bakr** defined it as one who leaves neither ascendants nor descendants, prefacing his answer with the disclaimer that if he was right it was from Allah and if wrong it was from himself and Shaytan. When 'Umar became khalifah, he said he was reluctant to contradict an opinion of Abu Bakr's. The definition became near-consensus.
- **Maternal half-siblings are peculiar in four ways:** they inherit through the mother; male and female take *equally*; they inherit only in Kalalah; and they never exceed one third however many they are.

### The limits (4:13–14)
These shares are **Allah's limits** — obey and enter Gardens, transgress by trick or manipulation and enter the Fire in humiliation. Then the hadith Abu Hurayrah attached to these verses, which lands like a blow:

> A man may act as the righteous do for seventy years, then commit an injustice when he writes his will — and his last deed sends him to the Fire. Another may act as the wicked do for seventy years, then be just in his will — and his last deed brings him to Paradise.

And: **there is no bequest for a rightful heir**, because Allah has already given each his due.

## 4. The Old Punishment, and the Door of Repentance (4:15–18)

**4:15–16** describe the *original* ruling — a woman proved guilty by four witnesses was confined at home "until death takes them, **or Allah ordains for them a way**." Ibn Abbas: that way came in Surah An-Nur, and it abrogated confinement. Agreed upon.

'Ubadah bin as-Samit described the moment: revelation used to press visibly on the Prophet ﷺ; when it lifted, he said Allah had now made the way — and gave the penalties for the unmarried and the married. *Take it from me.*

And once punished or repentant — **stop.** Do not keep reproaching them. "He who truly repents is like one with no sin at all." Even of the slave-girl who is flogged: do not revile her afterwards, for the lashes have erased what she did.

**4:17–18 — the terms of repentance.** Ibn Kathir gathers a striking consensus here. The Companions held that **every sin is committed in ignorance** — Mujahid: whoever disobeys Allah, even willfully, is ignorant while doing it. So "those who do evil in ignorance" is not a narrow category; it is all of us.

*And repent soon afterwards* — how soon?
- Ibn Abbas: until just before he sees the angel of death.
- al-Hasan al-Basri: before the last breath leaves the throat.
- ad-Dahhak: everything before death is "soon."
- **'Ikrimah: this entire life is "soon."**

The hadith settles it: **Allah accepts a servant's repentance as long as the soul has not reached the throat** — as long as the veil has not dropped, and the veil drops when the soul departs in a state of shirk.

What is *not* accepted: repentance at the sight of the punishment (as Pharaoh discovered), repentance after the sun rises from the west, and the deathbed of a disbeliever.

## 5. How to Treat a Wife (4:19–21)

Three pre-Islamic practices are abolished in three lines.

**Inheriting women.** Al-Bukhari, from Ibn Abbas: when a man died, his male relatives took possession of his widow — one might marry her, marry her off, or block her from marriage altogether; **they held more right to her than her own family.** The verse ends it: *you are not permitted to inherit women against their will.*

**Squeezing her for the dowry.** Do not treat her harshly so that she surrenders what you gave her or forfeits a right under duress. The exception — "unless they commit open *fahishah*" — was read by most of the Salaf as adultery; Ibn Abbas, 'Ikrimah and ad-Dahhak read it as defiance and disobedience; **Ibn Jarir chose the general sense** covering all of it, and Ibn Kathir approves.

**And live with them honourably.** Ibn Kathir unpacks this practically: kind words, kind treatment, and *making yourself presentable for her* — "as much as you can, just as you like the same from her." He cross-references 2:228 (they have rights like those over them) and the hadith that the best of you is the best to his family.

Then he simply describes the Prophet ﷺ at home: cheerful, playful, generous, laughing with his wives; racing 'A'ishah — she won, and years later, heavier, she lost, and he said *this one is for that one*; sleeping in a single garment; talking with the wife whose night it was after 'Isha before sleeping.

**"If you dislike them…"** — your patience with a wife you dislike carries reward here and hereafter. Ibn Abbas: he may come to feel compassion for her, and Allah may give him a child through her carrying enormous good. And the hadith: no believing man should hate his believing wife — **if one trait displeases him, another will please him.**

**The dowry is not recoverable (4:20–21).** Even a *qintar* — a fortune — stays with her when you replace her. Ibn Kathir uses this to establish that large dowries are lawful, and tells the story on himself, so to speak, of **'Umar**: he stood on the minbar forbidding dowries above four hundred dirhams; a Qurashi woman rose and asked whether he had not heard *"and you have given one of them a qintar."* 'Umar said, "O Allah, forgive me" — went back up the minbar and withdrew his own ruling. *Let everyone pay what he likes.* The chain, Ibn Kathir notes, is strong.

And the closing phrase: *they have taken from you a firm and strong covenant.* Marriage is a covenant taken in Allah's name — which is why, in the *li'an* case, the husband who demanded his dowry back was told plainly that the money was gone either way.

## 6. Who May Never Be Married (4:22–24)

**The father's wife (4:22).** Prohibited the moment the contract is made, dead or divorced — by consensus. Ibn Abbas: in Jahiliyyah they prohibited what Allah prohibits *except* marrying the stepmother and combining two sisters; these two verses closed both gaps. The Qur'an calls it *fahishah*, *maqt* (loathsome — Ata': Allah hates it), and an evil way. Ibn Kathir records that Abu Burdah was sent by the Prophet ﷺ to a man who married his stepmother, **to execute him and seize his property.**

Ibn Kathir adds a subtle note here: it is natural for a man to resent whoever married his wife before him — which is part of why **the Mothers of the Believers could not remarry** after the Prophet ﷺ, whose right over the believers exceeds a father's.

**The fourteen degrees (4:23)** — Ibn Abbas: *seven by blood, seven by marriage.*

| By blood | By marriage or suckling |
|---|---|
| Mothers | Foster mothers who suckled you |
| Daughters | Foster (milk) sisters |
| Sisters | Wives' mothers |
| Paternal aunts | Stepdaughters (of a consummated marriage) |
| Maternal aunts | Sons' wives (of your own loins) |
| Brothers' daughters | Two sisters at once |
| Sisters' daughters | *(and, from 4:24, women already married)* |

**Suckling.** *It prohibits what birth prohibits* — it establishes the same barriers as blood. But not any amount: 'A'ishah reported that ten sucklings were revealed, then abrogated to **five**, and the wording was still being recited when the Prophet ﷺ died. And it must occur **within the first two years** (per 2:233).

**Mother-in-law vs. stepdaughter — the distinction to hold onto:**
- The **wife's mother** becomes prohibited the moment the marriage contract is made, consummated or not.
- The **wife's daughter** becomes prohibited only once the marriage to her mother is **consummated**. Divorce the mother before consummation and the daughter is lawful. This is exactly what the verse's clause means.
- The phrase "under your guardianship" is descriptive, not a condition — the majority hold the stepdaughter prohibited whether raised in his house or not. Umm Habibah proposed her own sister to the Prophet ﷺ, then Abu Salamah's daughter; he answered that even had Umm Salamah not been his wife, the girl would be unlawful — Thuwaybah had suckled both him and Abu Salamah. **"Do not offer me your daughters or your sisters."**

**Sons' wives — "who spring from your own loins"** excludes adopted sons, and Ibn Kathir connects it directly to 33:37 and the marriage to Zaynab, which the Makkans attacked.

**Two sisters at once** — consensus. Whoever accepts Islam married to two must keep one and divorce the other (Fayruz ad-Daylami was so instructed).

**Married women (4:24)**, except captives of war, after establishing they are not pregnant — the ruling revealed after Awtas.

**Mut'ah.** Mujahid held the phrase *"those of them whom you have enjoyed"* referred to temporary marriage. Ibn Kathir then records its **prohibition**: 'Ali reported the Prophet ﷺ forbade both mut'ah and domesticated donkey meat at Khaybar; and at the conquest of Makkah he announced that what had been permitted was now **forbidden until the Day of Resurrection** — release any women held in it, and take nothing back from what you gave them.

## 7. Marrying Slaves, and Why the Law Is Light (4:25–28)

For a man who lacks the means to marry a free believing woman: he may marry a believing slave woman, **with her owner's permission**, and must pay her **her full dowry with a good heart** — not belittled because she is owned. She must be chaste — not a fornicator, and not one who takes secret lovers. Ibn Kathir adds the reminder embedded in the verse: *you are one from another* — Allah knows the reality; you see only surfaces.

Her punishment for *fahishah* is **half** that of a free unmarried woman — from which the scholars deduce that the penalty in view is **lashes, not stoning**, since stoning cannot be halved.

The permission is for those who fear for their chastity — **but restraint is better**, because the children will belong to her master.

**4:26–28** then state the purpose behind the entire legal section:

- to **make things clear** to you;
- to **guide you to the ways of those before you** — their righteous paths;
- to **turn to you in forgiveness**;
- and — while others who follow their desires want you to swerve far off course —
- to **lighten your burden**, *for man was created weak*.

Ibn Kathir: this is exactly why concessions like marrying a slave woman exist — the commands are made easy because the creature is frail. Tawus glossed "weak" as *weak where women are concerned*; Waki' put it more bluntly: **a man's mind leaves him when women are involved.**

## 8. Wealth, Life, and the Major Sins (4:29–31)

**Do not consume one another's property unjustly** — Ibn Kathir names riba and gambling and, pointedly, **schemes that look lawful but exist to deliver interest**. The lawful alternative is trade **by mutual consent** — and consent, he explains from the hadith, includes the right of either party to withdraw *until they part*.

There was an early over-reaction worth noting: some Muslims heard "do not consume your property unjustly" and concluded they could not eat at one another's houses at all — until 24:61 came down.

**And do not kill yourselves.** Read two ways at once — do not destroy yourselves through sin, and do not literally take life. Then the hadiths: whoever kills himself with iron, poison, or by throwing himself from a height, does that thing to himself in Hell, endlessly; whoever kills himself with an instrument is punished with it on the Day of Resurrection.

And immediately after, the mercy clause of the same verse — *surely Allah is Most Merciful to you* — illustrated with **'Amr bin al-'As**, who had a wet dream on a bitterly cold night on campaign, feared bathing would kill him, recited this very phrase to himself, performed tayammum and led the dawn prayer. When he explained, the Prophet ﷺ **smiled and said nothing.**

**4:31 — the great economy of forgiveness.** Avoid the major sins and the minor ones are wiped away. Hence the Friday hadith: whoever bathes, cleans himself, comes to the prayer and keeps silent until the imam finishes is forgiven what lies between this Friday and the next — **as long as the major sins are avoided.**

Ibn Kathir then assembles the hadiths on what the major sins are:
- **The seven destructive sins** (as above).
- Ibn Mas'ud's three, in order: **making a rival for Allah while He alone created you; killing your child for fear he will share your food; committing adultery with your neighbour's wife.**
- Shirk, undutifulness to parents, killing — and then, sitting up from reclining and repeating it until his Companions wished he would stop: **false testimony.**
- And an unexpected one: **a man cursing his own parents** — by insulting another man's father, so that the man curses his in return.

## 9. Contentment, and the Crisis in a Marriage (4:32–35)

**4:32 — do not covet.** Umm Salamah asked why men go to battle and inherit double while women do neither; the verse came down. Its answer is not a lecture but a redirection: *men have what they earn, women have what they earn* — **and ask Allah of His bounty.** Ibn Kathir's paraphrase of the divine reply: do not wish for what was given to another, for that decree will come to pass and wishing changes nothing; **ask Me instead, and I will give, for I am Most Generous.**

**4:33 — the pledge of brotherhood.** Those bound by oath were given a share; this was practised in early Islam and later abrogated — existing pledges honoured, no new ones made.

### 4:34 — the maintainers, and the disordered marriage

Ibn Kathir reads *qawwamun* as: **the man is responsible for the woman — her maintainer, caretaker and the one who corrects her if she deviates.** He grounds it in the two clauses of the verse itself: the preference Allah has given, and **what men spend** — dowry, provision, expenses — which is why the responsibility falls to them (cf. 2:228). On the first clause he takes the position that certain functions were reserved to men, citing the hadith on a people who appoint a woman to lead them, and extending it to judgeships and comparable offices.

**The righteous wife** is *qanitat* — obedient to her husband (Ibn Abbas) — and guards, in his absence, her honour and his property (as-Suddi). The hadith of Abu Hurayrah: the best woman pleases him when he looks, obeys when he commands, and protects herself and his property when he is away. And the wide door: **if she prays her five, fasts her month, guards her chastity and obeys her husband, she is told to enter Paradise by whichever gate she wishes.**

**The three steps for *nushuz*** — ill-conduct, which Ibn Kathir describes as her placing herself above him, disobeying, ignoring or disliking him:

1. **Admonish her** — remind her of Allah.
2. **Abandon her bed** — Ibn Abbas: turn your back to her, no intimacy; others add: do not speak to her.
3. **Strike** — and here Ibn Kathir stacks the restrictions rather than the permission: only *if* the first two have failed; **without severity** (Ibn Abbas: not violent; al-Hasan al-Basri: not severe); and within the Farewell Hajj framework — *fear Allah regarding women*, provide their food and clothing reasonably, and discipline only lightly. The Prophet ﷺ also told Mu'awiyah bin Haydah: feed her when you eat, clothe her when you clothe yourself, **never strike the face, never curse her, and do not abandon her except within the house.**

**And the moment she complies, it stops.** *Seek not a means against them* — no beating, no shunning, no harassment of any kind. The verse closes *Allah is Most High, Most Great*, which Ibn Kathir reads as a warning aimed squarely at husbands: **if you transgress against your wives without cause, He is their Protector, and He will take retribution from you.**

### 4:35 — arbitration
If the breach persists, the judge sends **one trusted person from his family and one from hers** to examine the case and decide whether they should stay together or part. Ibn Abbas: if the husband is at fault, they restrain him and he pays; if the wife is, she stays and he pays nothing. The scholars differed over whether the arbitrators' decision to *separate* binds without the spouses' authorisation (the majority say it does), but agreed a decision to keep them together binds, and that if the two arbitrators disagree, dissolution does not go through.

And the verse's own tilt: ***if they both desire reconciliation, Allah will bring about harmony between them.*** Ibn Kathir: **Allah gives preference to staying together.**

## 10. The Nine Rights (4:36–39)

One verse, one command, then a widening circle:

> **Worship Allah, associate nothing with Him — and be good to:** parents · relatives · orphans · the poor · the neighbour who is a relative · the neighbour who is a stranger · the companion at your side · the wayfarer · and those your right hands possess.

- **Parents** are joined to *tawhid* here as in 31:14 and 17:23, because they are the means by which you came to exist at all. And the exchange with Mu'adh: Allah's right on His servants is that they worship Him alone; their right on Him, if they do, is that He not punish them.
- **Relatives:** charity to the poor is charity; charity to kin is **charity and the keeping of ties**, both at once.
- **Neighbours:** *Jibril kept urging me about the neighbour until I thought he would give him a share of the inheritance.* Adultery with a neighbour's wife is worse than with ten women; theft from a neighbour worse than from ten houses. And when 'A'ishah asked which of two neighbours should receive her gift: **the one whose door is nearer.**
- **Those your right hands possess:** an order to be gentle with them *because they are weak and held by others*. What you feed yourself, your children, your wife and your servant is all sadaqah. It is sin enough for a man to withhold food from those he is responsible for. If your servant brings your meal, sit him down with you, or at least give him a mouthful — **he is the one who prepared it.** And the Prophet's ﷺ dying words, repeated until his tongue fell still: *the prayer, the prayer — and those your right hands possess.*

**Then the two failures of wealth (4:37–39).** The **miser** who hoards and urges others to hoard, hiding the favour Allah gave him — Ibn Kathir notes the verse calls such people *kafirun*, and explains the pun exactly: *kufr* means to cover, and the miser covers the blessing rather than letting it show. The counterpart is the hadith that when Allah gives a favour, He likes its effect to be visible. And the **show-off**, who spends to be seen — the wealthy man on the Day of Resurrection who lists everything he spent, and is told: *you lie; you did it to be called generous — and you were.* You were paid in this world, in the currency you chose.

The closing question is left hanging as a question: *and what harm would it have done them to believe in Allah and the Last Day and spend from what He gave them?*

## 11. Not an Atom's Weight (4:40–42)

**4:40** — Allah wrongs no one by the weight of a speck; a good deed He **doubles**, and gives from Himself a great reward. Abu Sa'id attached to this verse the intercession hadith: *go back and take out of the Fire everyone in whose heart you find a mustard seed of faith* — and in another wording, the least, least, least speck of it — **and they brought out great numbers.**

Sa'id bin Jubayr took the doubling clause to imply that a disbeliever's punishment may be *lessened* though never ended, citing al-'Abbas asking whether the Prophet's ﷺ uncle Abu Talib benefited at all from having protected him: *yes — he is in a shallow place in the Fire; were it not for me he would be in its lowest depths.* **Ibn Kathir restricts this to Abu Talib alone, not to disbelievers generally.**

**4:41** — the witness from every nation, and the Prophet ﷺ as witness over these. (The verse that stopped Ibn Mas'ud's recitation.)

**4:42** — they will wish the earth would swallow them, *and they will not be able to conceal from Allah a single statement.* A man told Ibn Abbas that things in the Qur'an confused him — this verse against 6:23, where the idolators swear they never associated anything with Allah. Ibn Abbas's answer: when they see that Allah forgives every sin but shirk, **they will lie**, hoping to be forgiven — and then their mouths will be sealed and their hands and feet will testify. The concealment is attempted; it fails.

---

## 12. Approaching the Prayer (4:43)

Three prohibitions and one concession.

**Not while intoxicated** — "until you know what you are saying," which Ibn Kathir calls the most precise possible definition of drunkenness. This verse belongs to the **staged** prohibition of wine: 2:219 came first, then this, after which they simply stopped drinking near prayer times; 'Umar kept praying for a plain ruling until 5:90–91 came and he said, *we abstain, we abstain.* Two occasions of revelation are recorded: a dinner where the guests grew drunk, boasted, and one struck Sa'd's nose with a camel bone; and a gathering at 'Abdur-Rahman bin 'Awf's where the man leading the prayer garbled Surah al-Kafirun.

*(Ibn Kathir attaches the related hadith: if you feel sleepy while praying, go and sleep — you may intend to seek forgiveness and end up cursing yourself.)*

**Nor while junub, except passing through** — the ruling on the masjid, reported from a long chain of the Salaf. Yazid bin Abi Habib's occasion: Ansar whose doors opened directly into the masjid had no other route to water. (Ibn Kathir takes the opportunity to note the sound narration that all doors into the masjid were closed **except Abu Bakr's**, and that the version naming 'Ali is an error.) A menstruating woman may likewise pass through — *your menses is not in your hand.*

**Tayammum.** Illness that water would worsen; travel of any length; after relieving oneself; after *lamastum* — which Ibn Abbas and most of the Salaf read as **sexual intercourse**. Then clean earth, wiping **face and hands** with one strike, as the Prophet ﷺ demonstrated to 'Ammar. *Clean earth is a purifier for the Muslim even if he finds no water for ten years* — and when he finds water, let him use it.

The occasion is 'A'ishah's lost necklace: the army halted where there was no water, Abu Bakr rebuked her sharply while the Prophet ﷺ slept with his head on her thigh, and dawn came with no water — and the verses of tayammum came down. Usayd bin Hudayr's comment to her: **"This is not the first blessing your family has brought us."** Then the camel was moved, and the necklace was found beneath it.

Ibn Kathir closes the passage on the note the verse itself ends on — *Allah is Ever Pardoning, Oft-Forgiving* — **the concession itself is the pardon.**

---

## 13. The People of the Book (4:44–57)

A long, hard passage. Ibn Kathir's treatment is polemical in places; the substance is as follows.

**Distorting the text (4:44–46).** Words displaced from their places; *we hear and we disobey*; the pun on *ra'ina* — sounding like "listen to us" in Arabic while carrying an insult, delivered "with a twist of their tongues, and as mockery of the religion." The verse offers the alternative they refused: *we hear and we obey.*

**4:47 — and a conversion.** Ibn Jarir records that **Ka'b al-Ahbar** was invited to Islam by 'Umar and declined, arguing from his own scripture. Later, passing through Hims, he heard a man reciting this verse — *believe, before We efface faces...* — and said, *"I believe, O Lord. I have accepted Islam, O Lord,"* fearing the threat would reach him. He returned to Yemen and came back with his whole family as Muslims.

**4:48 — the great dividing line.** Shirk is not forgiven for one who meets Allah upon it; **everything else falls within His will.** Ibn Kathir loads this with the hadiths of hope:

> *O My servant, as long as you call upon Me and hope in Me, I forgive you whatever you have done. If you come to Me with the earth's fill of sins, but without associating anything with Me, I will meet you with its fill of forgiveness.*

And Abu Dharr's exchange, repeated four times — *even if he stole and committed adultery?* — *even so, and even if Abu Dharr's nose is in the dust.* He walked away muttering it. Ibn 'Umar added that the Companions used to withhold prayers for the forgiveness of those who committed major sins **until they heard this verse**, and the Prophet's ﷺ words that his intercession is reserved for exactly those people.

**Claiming purity (4:49–52).** Against those who called themselves Allah's children and beloved, or claimed Paradise as an ethnic entitlement, or that the Fire would touch them only a few days: **Allah sanctifies whom He wills**, and no one is wronged by so much as a *fatil* — the thread in the groove of a date-stone. And the incident with Huyayy bin Akhtab and Ka'b bin al-Ashraf, who were asked by the Makkans to judge between themselves and Muhammad ﷺ, listed their own virtues, and pronounced the idolators **better guided** — which is what earned the curse of 4:52.

*(Ibn Kathir's glossary of the three "worthless things" the Qur'an uses to measure injustice: **fatil**, the thread in the date-stone's slit; **naqir**, the speck on its back; **qitmir**, the membrane around it.)*

**Envy (4:53–55).** If they had a share of dominion they would not give people a *naqir*. They envy Muhammad ﷺ the prophethood — yet **the family of Ibrahim** was given the Book, wisdom and a great kingdom, and even then some believed and some rejected. If they disputed with their own prophets, what will they do with one who is not of them?

**4:56–57 — the two ends.** Skins roasted through are **replaced**, so that the punishment continues to register (al-Hasan: seventy thousand times a day). Against it: Gardens beneath which rivers flow, **purified mates** — free of menstruation, filth, bad manners and defect — and shade "wide and ever deepening," which Ibn Kathir illustrates with the tree in Paradise a rider could travel beneath for a hundred years without crossing it.

---

## 14. Trusts, Judgment, and Obedience (4:58–59)

Two verses that Ibn Kathir treats as the constitutional core of the surah.

**4:58 — return the trusts, and judge with justice.** The occasion: the key of the Ka'bah. On the day Makkah fell, the Prophet ﷺ took it from **'Uthman bin Talhah**, entered, and on coming out was reciting this verse — then called 'Uthman and **gave the key back.** 'Umar said he had never heard him recite it before that moment. But Ibn Abbas insists the application is general: **"for the righteous and the wicked"** — it binds everyone.

Ibn Kathir enumerates what "trusts" covers: the rights of Allah on His servants (prayer, zakah, fasting, expiations, vows) and the rights of people on one another — **including what is entrusted with no record and no witness.** Whatever is not discharged here will be extracted on the Day of Resurrection, where even the hornless sheep takes retribution from the horned one.

On justice in judgment: *Allah is with the judge as long as he does not commit injustice; when he does, Allah leaves him to himself.* And the saying: **one day of justice equals forty years of worship.**

**4:59 — obey Allah, obey the Messenger, and those in authority among you.** Ibn Kathir defines each: *obey Allah* — follow His Book; *obey the Messenger* — follow his Sunnah; *those in authority* — **in what is obedience to Allah, not in disobedience.**

The story that fixes the limit: a commander, angered by his troops, lit a fire and ordered them into it. They almost obeyed. A young man said: **we only fled to the Messenger of Allah from the Fire — wait until we ask him.** The Prophet ﷺ told them that had they entered, they would never have come out. ***Obedience is only in what is right.***

Alongside it: hear and obey in what you like and dislike — **unless commanded to sin**; hear and obey even if an Ethiopian slave is made your chief, so long as he rules by Allah's Book; do not dispute authority with its people — **except upon clear disbelief for which you have proof from Allah.**

**And when you differ, refer it back** — Mujahid and the Salaf: to the Book of Allah and the Sunnah of His Messenger. Ibn Kathir draws the sharp inference the verse itself invites: the clause *if you believe in Allah and the Last Day* indicates that **whoever does not refer his disputes there is not a believer in Allah and the Last Day.**

## 15. No Faith Until (4:60–70)

**4:60–63.** Those who claim faith yet seek judgment from **Taghut** — whatever false authority they prefer. Occasions differ (an Ansari and a Jew disputing over whether to go to Muhammad ﷺ or to Ka'b bin al-Ashraf; hypocrites preferring the rulings of Jahiliyyah), but Ibn Kathir says the verse is **general**. And when disaster strikes they come swearing they meant only goodwill and reconciliation.

The instruction to the Prophet ﷺ regarding them is threefold and worth noticing for its restraint: **turn aside from them** (do not punish them for what is in their hearts) · **admonish them** · **and speak to them a word that reaches into their souls** — privately, effectively, in words that might actually benefit them.

**4:64 — the door.** *Had they, when they wronged themselves, come to you and sought Allah's forgiveness, and the Messenger sought forgiveness for them, they would have found Allah Accepting of repentance, Merciful.* One of Ibn Mas'ud's five.

**4:65 — the oath.** Allah swears by Himself that **they have no faith until they make you the judge in what arises between them, find no resistance in themselves to your decision, and submit fully.**

Two occasions:
- **Az-Zubayr's irrigation dispute.** The Prophet ﷺ first gave a lenient ruling — water your land, then let it flow on to your neighbour. The Ansari said: *is it because he is your cousin?* The Prophet's ﷺ face changed, and he gave az-Zubayr his **full legal right**: hold the water until it reaches the walls, then release it. Az-Zubayr believed this verse came down about it. (Ibn Kathir's observation is the sharp one: the man's objection cost him the generous ruling he had already been given.)
- A man who lost a case before the Prophet ﷺ, refused the verdict, and pushed the matter to 'Umar — who went into his house, came out with his sword, and struck him dead.

**4:66–68.** Had they been commanded to kill themselves or leave their homes, few would have done it — and had they done what they were told, it would have been better for them, would have **strengthened their conviction**, and Allah would have given a great reward and guided them to a straight path. Ibn Kathir: Allah knows not only what happened, but what *would* have happened.

**4:69 — the company.** Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with **the Prophets, the Siddiqin, the martyrs, and the righteous** — and how excellent a company. Ibn Kathir gives the reason for revelation: an Ansari came to the Prophet ﷺ visibly sad, and explained that he came morning and evening to look at his face, and that tomorrow the Prophet ﷺ would be raised among the prophets and he would not be able to see him. The Prophet ﷺ said nothing — until Jibril brought this verse, and he sent the man the good news.

Then the two hadiths that make this verse a lifeline:
- **"One is with those whom he loves."** Anas: the Muslims were never happier about anything than this. *"I love the Messenger of Allah, Abu Bakr and 'Umar, and I hope to be raised with them, though I have not done what they did."*
- 'A'ishah heard the Prophet ﷺ in his final illness, voice failing, reciting **"…with those whom Allah has favoured — the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous"** — and knew he was being given the choice. His last words, three times: **O Allah, in the Most High Company.**

And the verse's own footnote: *such is the bounty from Allah* — **it is from Him, by His mercy, not earned by their deeds.**

*(Also here: Rabi'ah al-Aslami, who served the Prophet ﷺ at night and was told to ask for anything, asked for his companionship in Paradise, refused an alternative, and was told — **then help me for your own sake, with many prostrations.**)*

## 16. Fighting, Fear, and the Hypocrites' Ledger (4:71–91)

**Take precautions (4:71)** — weapons, supplies, numbers; go out in parties or all together.

**The one who lags behind (4:72–74).** If you are struck, he congratulates himself on not being there — unaware of the reward he missed. If bounty comes, he wishes he had been with you, *as though no affection ever existed between you* — meaning, Ibn Kathir says, **as though he were never of your religion at all.** His aim is the share of the spoils. 'Abdullah bin Ubayy is named as the type: he stayed back, and lured others to stay back with him.

**Against this: the trade (4:74).** Fight those who sell the Hereafter for this world; **and whoever fights in Allah's cause, whether killed or victorious, will be given a great reward** — the guarantee being that Allah brings the mujahid either into Paradise or home with reward and spoils.

**The reason to fight (4:75).** Not conquest — **rescue**:

> *And what is with you that you do not fight for the cause of Allah, and for the oppressed among men, women and children who cry: Our Lord, take us out of this town whose people are oppressors, and appoint for us from Yourself a protector, and appoint for us from Yourself a helper.*

The town is **Makkah**. Ibn Abbas, who was there as a child, said simply: *I and my mother were among the oppressed.*

**Those who wanted the order delayed (4:77).** In Makkah the Muslims were told to hold their hands, pray and give charity — and they chafed at it, wanting to strike back. Ibn Kathir explains why they were restrained: they were few against many, and the city was the most sacred on earth. When fighting was finally ordained in Madinah, **some of the very people who had wanted it recoiled** and asked for a respite — because it means blood, orphans and widows. 'Abdur-Rahman bin 'Awf had said: *we were mighty as pagans and became weak as believers.* The answer he was given then: *I was commanded to pardon; do not fight them.*

**4:78 — death is not avoidable.** Wherever you are, it reaches you, even in towering fortresses. Ibn Kathir's illustration is perfect: **Khalid bin al-Walid**, dying in bed, said he had fought in battle after battle until there was no part of his body without a wound from a blade or an arrow — *and here I am, dying in my bed. May the eyes of cowards never taste sleep.*

**4:78–79 — good and evil.** They attribute good to Allah and misfortune to the Prophet ﷺ (as Pharaoh's people did with Musa). *Say: all is from Allah* — everything runs by His decree. Then the second half, addressed to the individual: **whatever good reaches you is from Allah; whatever evil befalls you is from yourself** — from your own errors and sins (cf. 42:30).

**4:80–81.** Obeying the Messenger *is* obeying Allah. The hypocrites say "obedience," then plot through the night — **and Allah records their night-plotting.** The instruction again: turn away from them, do not expose them, do not fear them, and **rely on Allah.**

**4:82 — the Qur'an does not contradict itself.** *Had it been from other than Allah they would have found much discrepancy in it.* Ibn Kathir contrasts those firmly grounded in knowledge, who read the unclear in light of the clear and gain guidance, with those diseased of heart, who do the reverse and gain misguidance. And the scene: Companions arguing loudly over a verse near the Prophet's ﷺ door; he came out red-faced, threw dust at them, and told them that **the nations before them were destroyed by disputing with their prophets and setting parts of the Book against other parts.** Act on what you know; refer what you don't to those who know.

**4:83 — rumour and verification.** Those who broadcast news of security or fear before it is checked. *Had they referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority, the ones able to investigate would have known it.* The hadiths: **it is enough of a lie for a man to repeat everything he hears**; whoever relates a report knowing it false is one of the two liars.

The occasion is 'Umar's own. Hearing that the Prophet ﷺ had divorced his wives, he found the whole city talking about it — went straight to the source, asked outright, was told no, then stood at the door of the masjid and announced it at the top of his voice. **"So I investigated that matter properly."**

**4:84 — fight; you are accountable only for yourself, and urge on the believers.** Ibn Kathir cites al-Bara's ruling that a man who charges a hundred enemies alone is *not* throwing himself to destruction — 2:195 is about withholding wealth, not about courage. And the Prophet ﷺ at Badr, dressing the lines: **rise to a Paradise as wide as the heavens and the earth.** Alongside: the hundred grades reserved for the mujahidin, each pair as far apart as heaven and earth — *so when you ask, ask for al-Firdaws.*

**4:85 — intercession.** Whoever intercedes for good has a share of it; whoever intercedes for evil bears a share of that. *Intercede, and you will be rewarded* — and Allah decides what He wills through His Prophet's ﷺ words. *Muqit* was glossed as Watcher, Witness, and Able.

**4:86 — the greeting.** Return it **better**, or at least equal. Better is recommended; equal is obligatory. The three men who came to the Prophet ﷺ — *peace be upon you*, then *and Allah's mercy*, then *and His blessings* — and after each returned greeting he said: **ten. Twenty. Thirty.** Ibn Kathir records the separate rulings governing the People of the Covenant, and closes with the hadith that ties the greeting to faith itself: **you will not enter Paradise until you believe, and you will not believe until you love one another — spread the salam among yourselves.**

**4:88–91 — the disagreement about the hypocrites.** The Companions split into two parties over those who turned back on the way to Uhud — one saying kill them, the other refusing. (Another report: over Makkans who claimed Islam while supporting the idolators.) The Prophet's ﷺ comment on Madinah itself: **she is Tayyibah, and she expels filth as the bellows expels the dross from iron.**

Ibn Kathir then sorts the categories carefully, because the verses do:

| Category | Ruling |
|---|---|
| Those who turn back and make their disbelief public, refusing hijrah | Combatants |
| **Those who join a people with whom you have a treaty** | Treated as the treaty people are treated |
| **Those who come to you unwilling to fight either side** | Left alone — *had Allah willed, He would have set them against you* |
| Those who withdraw, do not fight, and offer peace | **Allah has given you no way against them** |
| Those who seek safety from both sides while reverting to shirk whenever they return to it | Fought, unless they withdraw and offer peace |

Ibn Kathir's example for the third row is telling: this was **the position of Banu Hashim** — men like al-'Abbas, present at Badr with the idolators but there with great reluctance, which is why the Prophet ﷺ ordered that he be **captured, not killed.**

## 17. The Sanctity of a Believer's Blood (4:92–94)

**A believer does not kill a believer — except by mistake.** Ibn Kathir opens with the hadith that a Muslim's blood is sacred except in three cases (a life for a life; the married adulterer; the one who abandons his religion and the community) — and adds the essential rider: **even then it is for the ruler or his deputy to carry out, not for private individuals.**

**For accidental killing, two obligations:**
1. **Kaffarah** — free a *believing* slave. (Hence the Ansari who brought a slave woman and asked the Prophet ﷺ to determine whether she qualified; he questioned her on tawhid, on his messengership, and on the resurrection, and she affirmed each.)
2. **Diyah** to the victim's family — **unless they waive it.**

With variations: if the victim was a believer among a hostile people, no diyah is owed, only the freeing of a slave; if his family are treaty people, both are owed. And whoever cannot manage it fasts **two consecutive months**, "as repentance from Allah."

Ibn Kathir includes **Khalid bin al-Walid at Banu Jadhimah**, who killed people unable to articulate the words for accepting Islam, saying *saba'na* instead. The Prophet ﷺ raised his hands and **declared himself innocent before Allah of what Khalid had done**, and sent 'Ali to pay the blood money and compensate for property — down to the value of a dog's bowl. Ibn Kathir draws the legal point: **the error of the leader or his deputy is paid from the public treasury.**

**4:93 — intentional killing.** Hell, eternity, Allah's wrath, His curse, a great punishment. Ibn Kathir notes this sin is placed alongside shirk in several passages. And: **blood cases are the first disputes judged between people on the Day of Resurrection**; the believer remains unburdened as long as he sheds no forbidden blood.

**Can the deliberate murderer repent?** Ibn Kathir gives both sides properly:
- **Ibn Abbas: no.** He held this verse was the last revealed on the subject and nothing abrogated it — and when the scholars of Kufah disagreed and Sa'id bin Jubayr travelled to ask him, that is what he said.
- **The majority, early and late: yes.** Sincere repentance with righteous deeds falls under 25:68–70 and under *say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of Allah's mercy* (39:53), which covers every sin including kufr, shirk and murder. And the Israelite who killed a hundred and was still taken by the angels of mercy.
- **A reconciling reading**, from Abu Hurayrah and several of the Salaf: this is his **deserved recompense** *if* Allah chooses to punish him — other good deeds may intervene; and even if he enters the Fire, whoever has a speck of faith ultimately leaves it.

**4:94 — "You are not a believer."** A shepherd from Bani Sulaym greeted a party of Companions with the salam; they decided he had said it only to save himself, killed him, and took his sheep. Another version: **al-Miqdad** killed a man who declared the *shahadah*. The Prophet's ﷺ question to him is one of the most quoted lines in the surah:

> **"What will you do with 'there is no god but Allah' tomorrow?"**

And: *he was a believing man concealing his faith among disbelieving people, and he announced it to you, and you killed him — and you yourself used to conceal your faith in Makkah.* Which is precisely what the verse says: ***so were you before, until Allah conferred His favour upon you.***

The commercial motive is named too — *seeking the perishable goods of this life.* Ibn Abbas: **the goods of this world were those sheep.**

## 18. Sitting Still, and Setting Out (4:95–100)

**4:95–96 — not equal.** Those who sit and those who strive with wealth and lives are not the same; Allah has raised the strivers by degrees. Ibn Kathir gives the moment of revelation: the Prophet ﷺ was dictating the verse to Zayd bin Thabit when **Ibn Umm Maktum**, who was blind, said that if he had the ability he would go out. Zayd felt revelation descend with the Prophet's ﷺ thigh on his own, so heavy he feared it would break — and the exception came down: ***except those who are disabled.***

Two conclusions Ibn Kathir draws:
- **Jihad is *fard kifayah*, not *fard 'ayn*** — because Allah promised *good to all of them*, sitters included.
- Those genuinely prevented are not measured against the strivers at all. *There are men who stayed in Madinah who were with you at every march and every valley you crossed — their disability held them back.*

**4:97–99 — the ones who stayed among the idolators.** The angels ask them at death: *in what condition were you?* — meaning, why did you not migrate? *We were oppressed in the land.* — *Was Allah's earth not wide enough?* Ibn Kathir applies this to those **able to emigrate and unable to practise their religion**; and cites the hadith that whoever keeps company and lives with an idolator is like him.

But the exception is drawn generously: **the weak among men, women and children who cannot devise a way and cannot find a route.** Ibn Abbas again: *I and my mother were among those Allah excused.* And the Prophet ﷺ used to pray in the qunut for exactly these people by name — 'Ayyash bin Abi Rabi'ah, Salamah bin Hisham, al-Walid bin al-Walid, **and the weak among the Muslims.**

**4:100 — the one who dies on the road.** Whoever emigrates for Allah will find many refuges and abundance; **and whoever leaves his home as an emigrant to Allah and His Messenger, and death overtakes him, his reward has fallen due upon Allah.**

The hadith of intentions belongs here — deeds are by intentions, and whoever emigrated for a woman or for gain, that is what he emigrated for. And the story Ibn Kathir tells at its fullest: the man who had killed a hundred, was told to leave his land for one where Allah is worshipped, and died on the way. The angels of mercy and of punishment disputed over him; they were told to measure which land he was nearer to; **Allah commanded the good land to draw closer and the evil land to move away** — and he was found nearer by a hand's span. In another narration, at the point of death he **dragged his chest toward the place he was going.**

## 19. Prayer, Shortened and Under Fire (4:101–104)

**4:101 — *qasr*.** Four rak'ahs become two when travelling. The clause "if you fear" describes the conditions prevailing when the verse came down, not a condition of the ruling — and this is settled by **'Umar's own question**. Ya'la bin Umayyah asked him: we are safe now, so why do we still shorten? 'Umar said he had wondered the same thing and asked the Prophet ﷺ, who replied:

> **A charity Allah has given you — so accept His charity.**

Ibn 'Umar, asked the same question, answered simply: **this is the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah.** Corroborated by Anas (two rak'ahs from Madinah to Makkah and throughout the ten days there) and Harithah bin Wahb (two rak'ahs at Mina in perfect safety, with vast numbers present).

**4:102 — the fear prayer.** One party prays with the imam bearing arms while the other guards, then they exchange. Ibn Kathir stresses that its **forms vary** with the position of the enemy — four rak'ahs, three, or two; in congregation when possible, and when battle is actually raging, each man prays alone, on foot or mounted, facing the qiblah or not, walking and fighting as he goes. Some scholars (Ibn Abbas, Ahmad, and others) held it reduces to **one rak'ah** in combat; Ishaq bin Rahwayh said that if you cannot manage even that, **one prostration is enough — for prostration is remembrance of Allah.**

The occasion, at 'Usfan: the idolators under Khalid stood between the Muslims and the qiblah and, having watched them absorbed in Zuhr, planned to strike during 'Asr — *a prayer dearer to them than their children.* Jibril brought these verses **between the two prayers.**

The weapons clause is taken seriously — a group held bearing arms obligatory — with the exemption for rain or illness, "but take your precautions": stay close enough to reach them.

**4:103 — after the prayer.** *Remember Allah standing, sitting and on your sides* — dhikr is urged especially after the fear prayer, precisely because its pillars were curtailed. Ibn Kathir's analogy: injustice is forbidden always, yet 9:36 singles out the sacred months. Then, when secure, **pray it properly** — full bowing, full prostration, humility. *Prayer is a timed prescription upon the believers.* Ibn Abbas: it has a fixed time, like the Hajj.

**4:104 — do not slacken in the pursuit.** You suffer and they suffer alike. **The difference is not in the pain; it is in the hope.** *You hope from Allah what they do not hope for.* So you, not they, should be the eager ones.

## 20. Judging, and Not Defending the Guilty (4:105–113)

*We have sent down the Book to you in truth, that you may judge between people by what Allah has shown you — so do not be a pleader for the treacherous.*

The hadith on the limits of even prophetic judgment, from Umm Salamah:

> **I am only a human being, and I judge by what I hear.** One of you may be more persuasive than another, so that I rule in his favour — and if I award him something belonging to his brother, it is a piece of the Fire; let him take it or leave it.

In the case of the two Ansari men disputing an old inheritance with no evidence, both wept and each renounced his claim; the Prophet ﷺ told them to divide it, aim at fairness, draw lots, **and each forgive his brother** whatever the outcome.

Then the warnings around it: do not argue on behalf of those who betray themselves; **they hide from people but cannot hide from Allah**, who is with them as they plot by night. You may plead for them here and win — *but who will plead for them before Allah on the Day of Resurrection?*

**4:110 — the mercy verse.** Ibn Abbas: this announces His forgiveness, His forbearance and His vast mercy — **whoever commits a sin, minor or major, and then seeks forgiveness, finds Him Forgiving and Merciful, even were his sins greater than the heavens, the earth and the mountains.** Abu Bakr transmitted the practice attached to it: no servant commits a sin, then purifies himself, prays two rak'ahs and seeks forgiveness, without being forgiven.

**4:111–112.** Whoever earns sin earns it against himself; no bearer bears another's burden. And whoever commits a fault and **pins it on an innocent person** has taken on slander and manifest sin.

**4:113.** Had it not been for Allah's grace, a party would have led you astray — *but they lead astray only themselves and cannot harm you at all.* And He taught you what you did not know: **great indeed is Allah's favour upon you.**

## 21. Secret Conversation, and Breaking Ranks (4:114–115)

**There is no good in most of their private talk** — except three: **urging charity, urging what is right, or making peace between people.**

Ibn Kathir treats reconciliation as a major virtue in its own right:
- *He who reconciles people, saying good or conveying good, is not a liar.*
- Umm Kulthum bint 'Uqbah added that she never heard him permit people to say untrue things except in **three cases: war, reconciliation between people, and what a husband and wife say to each other.**
- And Abu ad-Darda's hadith: *shall I tell you of something better in degree than fasting, prayer and charity? — Setting things right between people.* And its inverse: **spoiling relations between people is the shaver** — the destroyer.

**4:115 — the verse behind consensus.** Whoever opposes the Messenger *after guidance is clear to him*, **and follows a way other than the believers' way**, is left to what he has chosen and burned in Hell. Ibn Kathir explains the second clause as the companion of the first: contradiction may take the form of defying a text, **or defying what the Ummah has agreed on** — the Ummah being protected from agreeing upon error, a distinction granted them out of honour to their Prophet ﷺ. And the punishment described is itself frightening: *We shall keep him in what he has turned to* — **the wrong path is made attractive to him**, and he is drawn further in.

---

## 22. Shaytan's Fixed Portion (4:116–126)

**4:116** restates 4:48 — shirk unforgiven, all else within His will — and Ibn Kathir notes the deliberate placement: the promise of forgiveness is set **before and after** this section, to keep hope alive.

**4:117–121 — what idolatry actually is.** They call on female deities; ad-Dahhak explains the mechanism — the idolators claimed the angels were Allah's daughters, worshipped them as intermediaries, then carved female images to represent them. But behind the idols is one addressee: **in reality they call upon nothing but Shaytan.**

Then Shaytan's own programme, quoted against him:

- **"I will take an appointed portion of Your servants"** — a fixed, known share.
- **"I will mislead them"** — from the path.
- **"I will arouse false desires in them"** — Ibn Kathir's gloss: making them intend to repent *later*, filling them with hopes, urging delay and procrastination in doing good.
- **"I will order them to slit the ears of cattle"** — the *bahirah*, *sa'ibah*, *wasilah* markings.
- **"And I will order them to change Allah's creation"** — al-Hasan al-Basri: tattooing. Ibn Mas'ud extends it, on the authority of the Prophet's ﷺ curse, to tattooing, plucking facial hair, and filing gaps between the teeth for beauty — *changing what Allah created.* Asked why he would curse, he answered: **why should I not curse whom the Messenger of Allah cursed, when it is in the Book of Allah** — and recited 59:7.

*He promises them and arouses desires — and Shaytan promises nothing but delusion.*

**4:123–124 — it is not by wishes.** The occasion: Muslims and the People of the Book each claiming precedence — our prophet came first, our book came first, versus our prophet is the last and our book supersedes. Allah judged between them by refusing the premise: **not by your wishes, nor theirs. Whoever does evil will be recompensed for it.**

Ibn Kathir's principle: religion is not obtained by claim or hope; the claim must be validated by proof from Allah. And whoever does righteous deeds — **male or female** — and is a believer enters Paradise and is not wronged by so much as a *naqir*.

But *"whoever does evil will be recompensed for it"* landed hard on the Companions. 'A'ishah told the Prophet ﷺ she knew the hardest verse in the Qur'an, and named it. He said: **that is what strikes the believing servant — even the troubles that vex him.** And to the Muslims generally: *be steadfast and seek nearness — everything that afflicts a Muslim, even the thorn that pricks him, is an expiation.*

**4:125 — the two conditions.** Ibn Kathir extracts from *"one who submits his face to Allah and is a doer of good"* the twin test by which any deed is accepted:

> **Sincerity** — that it be for Allah alone. **Correctness** — that it conform to the Sharia.
> Outwardly correct by following the Sunnah; inwardly correct by sincerity.
> Missing the first, he is a show-off; missing the second, he is an ignorant innovator. **Missing either, the deed is void.**

**Ibrahim as *Khalil*.** He reached the highest degree of love, by obedience — *the one who fulfilled* (53:37), tested with commands which he completed (2:124), *an Ummah in himself* (16:120). When Mu'adh recited this verse at Fajr on returning from Yemen, a man said: **"How the eye of Ibrahim's mother must be comforted."** And the Prophet ﷺ, in his last address: if I were to take a *khalil* from the people of the earth it would be Abu Bakr — **but your companion is the Khalil of Allah.**

---

## 23. When a Marriage Is Failing (4:127–130)

**4:127** returns to the female orphans — Allah gives the ruling directly, and refers back to what was "recited to you in the Book," i.e. 4:3. 'A'ishah explains the *two* opposite abuses:
- If she is wealthy and beautiful, her guardian marries her **without a fair dowry** — forbidden.
- If she is neither, he **refuses to let her marry anyone else**, so that no husband will come between him and her property. Ibn Abbas describes the old practice starkly: he would throw his cloak over her, and after that no one would marry her; if she was beautiful he took her and her wealth; if not, he blocked her until she died — **and then inherited from her.**

The verse also covers **the weak among children**, who in Jahiliyyah received no inheritance at all, and ends: *stand firm in justice for orphans.*

**4:128 — desertion.** If a wife fears aversion or neglect from her husband, **there is no blame on the two of them in making a settlement** — she may forgo part of her rights (her nights, her maintenance) and he may accept, **and settlement is better.** *And souls are swayed by greed* — Ibn Kathir reads this as the realistic note: coming to terms, even at some cost, beats parting.

The living example is **Sawdah bint Zam'ah**, who feared divorce and asked the Prophet ﷺ to keep her and give her day to 'A'ishah — and he did. Ibn Kathir observes that he **kept her as a wife** so that his Ummah would have the precedent. Ibn Abbas's principle from it: **whatever the spouses mutually agree to is permissible.** And Ibn Kathir's blunt closing line: *divorce is not beloved to Allah.*

**4:129 — the honest verse.** *You will never be able to deal justly between wives, however much you desire it.* Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, al-Hasan and others: even if you divide the nights exactly, **love, desire and inclination will not divide evenly.** So the Prophet ﷺ used to apportion equally and then say:

> **O Allah, this is my division in what I own — do not blame me for what You own and I do not.**

Meaning his heart. What *is* commanded is the limit: **do not incline so far as to leave the other suspended** — *mu'allaqah*, which the Salaf gloss as "neither divorced nor married." And the warning: whoever has two wives and leans to one comes on the Day of Resurrection with one side hanging.

**4:130** — and if they do separate, **Allah will enrich each of them from His abundance.** Ibn Kathir: He will give him a better wife and her a better husband. Even the failure is not the end.

---

## 24. Standing Firm for Justice (4:131–136)

Four verses on Allah's self-sufficiency frame the demand: to Him belongs everything in the heavens and the earth; He enjoined *taqwa* on those before you and on you; **if you disbelieve, He owns it all anyway** and is Rich, Praiseworthy. If He wills He can remove you and bring others. And whoever wants the reward of this world should know that **the reward of both worlds is with Him** — so ask Him for both.

**4:135 — the great verse of justice:**

> *Stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to Allah, even against yourselves, or your parents, or your kin — whether he be rich or poor, Allah is nearer to both.*

Ibn Kathir's reading: neither deviate right nor left; fear no one's blame; and **give correct testimony even if it harms you**, for Allah makes a way out for whoever obeys Him. Do not compromise for the sake of a father or a relative. Do not favour a rich man for his rank or a poor man out of pity — Allah has more claim on both than you do.

Then the two ways testimony fails, named in the verse itself:
- ***Talwu*** — to twist it: distortion, alteration, deliberate lying (cf. 3:78, those who twist the Book with their tongues).
- ***Tu'ridu*** — to turn away from it: **concealment and withholding** (cf. 2:283, whoever hides it, his heart is sinful).

Against both: **the best witness is the one who gives his testimony before he is asked for it.**

**4:136 — believers, believe.** Not redundancy, Ibn Kathir insists, but the command to **complete and continually maintain** faith — the same logic by which one who is already guided still asks in every prayer to be guided to the straight path: *make me know it, increase me in it, and keep me firm upon it.*

---

## 25. The Anatomy of Hypocrisy (4:137–147)

**4:137.** Those who believe, disbelieve, believe, disbelieve, then increase in disbelief — Ibn Abbas: **they remain on it until they die.** For them, no forgiveness and no guidance to a way.

**4:139.** Taking disbelievers as allies rather than believers — *do they seek honour with them?* **Honour belongs entirely to Allah** (cf. 35:10, 63:8). The rhetorical force is that they are seeking a thing where it is not.

**4:140 — the sitting rule.** When you hear Allah's verses denied and mocked, **do not sit with them until they change the subject** — otherwise *you are like them.* Ibn Kathir: knowing the prohibition and sitting anyway, in approval, makes you a participant in what they earn. The "already revealed" reference is to 6:68, from Makkah. (Muqatil bin Hayyan held 4:140 abrogated part of that earlier verse.)

**4:141 — the watchers.** They wait to see which way it goes. Victory for the Muslims: *were we not with you?* Success for the disbelievers: *did we not have the upper hand over you and protect you from the believers?* Playing both sides for safety, out of weak faith.

The verse ends: ***Allah will never grant the disbelievers a way over the believers.*** Ibn Kathir gives the readings — on the Day of Resurrection ('Ali, Ibn Abbas, Abu Malik); "way" meaning proof (as-Suddi); and the this-worldly sense he thinks also possible: **they may win a battle, as at Uhud, but they will never be able to extinguish the believers**, and the final outcome belongs to them in both worlds (40:51).

**4:142–143 — the portrait.** They try to deceive Allah, and He is deceiving them. Ibn Kathir explains the psychology: **having succeeded in deceiving people, using the law as cover, they imagine the same will work on the Day of Resurrection** — and indeed they will swear to Him as they swore to you (58:18).

Then the prayer, described mercilessly:
- They rise to it **lazily**, because they neither intend it, believe in it, nor understand it.
- They do it **to be seen**, which is why they are missing from the prayers that can be skipped unseen: **'Isha and Fajr, prayed in the dark.** The Prophet ﷺ said those two are heaviest on the hypocrites, that they would come crawling if they knew the reward, and that he had considered burning down the houses of those who stayed away.
- They **remember Allah only a little** — the hadith of the man who sits watching the sun until it sets between the horns of Shaytan, then stands and pecks out four rak'ahs of 'Asr: **this is the prayer of the hypocrite, this is the prayer of the hypocrite, this is the prayer of the hypocrite.**

**Swaying between the two.** Neither with the believers inwardly nor with the disbelievers outwardly — with the believers outwardly and the disbelievers inwardly, lurching between them as the lightning-flash in 2:20 allows. The image the Prophet ﷺ used: **a sheep wandering between two flocks, going to one, then the other, never knowing which to follow.**

**4:145–147 — and yet.** The hypocrites are in **the lowest depth of the Fire**; Ibn Mas'ud described it as coffins of fire, sealed shut around them. No helper will be found for them —

***except those who repent, make amends, hold fast to Allah, and make their religion sincerely for Allah*** — replacing display with sincerity. **Then they are with the believers**, and Allah will give the believers a great reward.

And the verse the whole passage resolves into: ***What would Allah do with punishing you, if you are grateful and believe?*** He is beyond needing anything from you; He punishes only for sin. **He is Appreciative, All-Knowing** — He appreciates whoever appreciates Him.

---

## 26. Speech, Pardon, and All the Messengers (4:148–152)

**4:148 — the wronged may speak.** Allah does not love that evil be spoken aloud **except by one who has been wronged.** Ibn Abbas: he may invoke against the one who wronged him — **but patience is better for him.** Al-Hasan al-Basri would rather he pray: *O Allah, help me against him and take my right from him.* 'Abdul-Karim al-Jazari draws a precise line: **if a man curses you, you may curse back; if he lies about you, you may not lie about him.** And the hadith: in an exchange of insults, the one who began it carries the sin — **unless the wronged one exceeds the limit.**

**4:149 — the better way.** Whether you show a good deed, conceal it, or **pardon an evil** — Allah is Ever Pardoning, All-Powerful. Ibn Kathir notes the pairing of the two names: He pardons *while* able to punish, which is the whole point. Some of the angels who carry the Throne praise Him for His forbearance despite His knowledge; others for His forgiveness despite His power. And: **charity never decreases wealth; Allah only increases the honour of one who pardons; and whoever humbles himself for Allah, Allah raises him.**

**4:150–152 — believing in some and not others.** Ibn Kathir surveys the pattern: the Jews accept the prophets but not 'Isa and Muhammad ﷺ; the Christians accept the prophets but not the last of them; the Samaritans accept none after Yusha'; and so on. His argument is that **rejecting one prophet invalidates belief in all of them** — because if the rejection is driven by envy, bias or whim rather than proof, then the acceptance was never based on proof either. *Those are the disbelievers in truth.* And those who believe in Allah and His messengers **making no distinction between any of them** will be given their rewards.

---

## 27. The Crimes of the Covenant, and the Truth about 'Isa (4:153–159)

**4:153–154 — the record.** They demand a book brought down from heaven — Ibn Kathir: a demand of stubbornness, like the Quraysh demanding a spring gush from the earth (17:90). *They asked Musa for greater than that* — to see Allah openly — and were struck by lightning. Then the calf, after the clear signs. Then the mountain raised over them for the covenant. Then the command to enter the gate in humility saying *hittah* — which they entered crawling on their backsides, saying *hintah* (a grain of wheat) instead. Then the Sabbath.

**4:155–156 — the charges.** Breaking the covenant; rejecting Allah's signs; **killing the prophets unjustly**; saying *our hearts are wrapped* (the same excuse the idolators made in 41:5) — no, Allah **sealed** them for their disbelief; and **the slander against Maryam**, accusing her of fornication.

**4:157–158 — the crucifixion.** *And their saying: we killed the Messiah, 'Isa son of Maryam, the Messenger of Allah* — a claim they made **in mockery**, as the idolators mocked the Prophet ﷺ as a madman (15:6).

Ibn Kathir narrates the sequence as he received it: envy at the miracles ('Isa healed the blind and the leper, raised the dead, and formed a bird from clay which flew by Allah's leave); constant flight from city to city; then a complaint to the Greek governor that a man in Jerusalem was stirring unrest; the order to arrest and crucify him; the house surrounded on a Friday evening with twelve or so companions inside; and **'Isa's question — who will volunteer to be made to resemble me, and be my companion in Paradise?** A young man volunteered, twice refused as too young, then accepted. The resemblance was cast over him, a hole opened in the roof, and **'Isa was raised while asleep** (cf. 3:55).

Those who surrounded the house seized the young man and crucified him. *But they did not kill him, nor crucify him — it only appeared so to them.* And the verse's own commentary on the confusion that followed: **those who differ about it are in doubt, following nothing but conjecture; they certainly did not kill him.**

Ibn Abbas adds that 'Isa's followers then split three ways over what they had seen — two groups asserting divinity in different forms, and one holding to *the servant and messenger of Allah* — and that the first two combined against the third and killed them.

**4:159 — before his death.** *There is none of the People of the Scripture but will believe in him before his death.* Ibn Abbas and Abu Malik: **before the death of 'Isa** — that is, after his descent, when all of them will believe.

Ibn Kathir then gathers the hadiths of the descent. In sum: he descends by the white minaret east of Damascus, in lightly saffron-coloured garments, hands on the wings of two angels, his head appearing to drip water though untouched by moisture — a well-built man, between red and white, with hair to his shoulders. He **breaks the cross, kills the swine, abolishes the jizyah**, prays behind the Muslims' own imam, kills the Dajjal at the gate of Ludd, and wealth becomes so abundant that no one will accept charity. He performs Hajj or 'Umrah from ar-Rawha'. Safety fills the earth — lions with camels, wolves with sheep, children playing with snakes. He remains forty years, dies, **and the Muslims pray the funeral prayer over him.**

The Prophet ﷺ said of him: *the prophets are brothers of one father, their mothers differ, and their religion is one — and I have more claim to 'Isa than anyone, for there was no prophet between him and me.*

Ibn Kathir also relays the long Dajjal hadith of an-Nawwas bin Sam'an (the forty days, one like a year, one like a month, one like a week, and the ruling to **estimate the prayers accordingly**; the command to recite the opening of Surah al-Kahf), the release of Gog and Magog, their destruction by a worm sent into their necks, the rain that scours the earth clean, and the final gentle wind that takes the soul of every believer, leaving only the worst of people, upon whom the Hour begins.

*(A historical aside from Ibn Kathir's own time: in the year 741 a white stone minaret was built in the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, replacing one destroyed by fire — and, he says, there is a strong sense that this is the minaret named in the hadith.)*

---

## 28. Revelation, and the Line of Prophets (4:160–166)

**4:160–161 — why foods were forbidden them.** For their wrongdoing, for barring many from Allah's way, for **taking riba after being forbidden it**, and for devouring people's wealth wrongfully. Ibn Kathir offers two possible senses of the prohibition: that they were left to misread their own Book and forbid things to themselves out of extremism, or that Allah genuinely prohibited in the Tawrah what had been lawful before (cf. 3:93 and 6:146) as a recompense for rebellion.

**4:162 — the exception.** *But those firmly grounded in knowledge among them, and the believers* — who believe in what came to you and before you, establish the prayer, give zakah, and believe in Allah and the Last Day: **to them We shall give a great reward.** Ibn Abbas names names: **'Abdullah bin Salam**, Tha'labah bin Sa'yah, Zayd bin Sa'yah, Asad bin 'Ubayd.

**4:163–165 — the chain.** Revelation came to you as it came to Nuh and the prophets after him — a rebuttal, Ibn Kathir says, to those who claimed Allah sent nothing down to any human after Musa.

Twenty-five prophets are named in the Qur'an: **Adam, Idris, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Ibrahim, Lut, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub, Yusuf, Ayyub, Shu'ayb, Musa, Harun, Yunus, Dawud, Sulayman, Ilyas, al-Yasa', Zakariyya, Yahya, 'Isa** — and their leader, **Muhammad ﷺ** (with Dhul-Kifl added by several commentators). *And messengers We have not told you of.*

**And Allah spoke to Musa directly** — his honour, and the reason he is called *al-Kalim*. Ibn Kathir records the grammatical controversy with some heat: a man recited the verse so as to make *Musa* the speaker and Allah the addressee; Abu Bakr bin 'Ayyash said only a disbeliever would recite it that way, and traced the correct reading through al-A'mash, Yahya bin Withab, Abu 'Abdur-Rahman as-Sulami and 'Ali back to the Prophet ﷺ. The distortion was Mu'tazili, denying that Allah speaks. Another teacher's retort to one of them: **what will you do with 7:143 — *and his Lord spoke to him*?**

**4:165 — why messengers at all.** Bearers of good news and warners, **so that mankind would have no argument against Allah after the messengers.** Hence 20:134: *had We destroyed them before this they would have said, if only You had sent us a messenger.* And the hadith:

> None is more jealous than Allah — so He forbade indecency, open and secret. None loves praise more than Allah — so He praised Himself. **And none loves to offer an excuse more than Allah — so He sent the prophets as bearers of good news and warners.**

**4:166 — the witness.** Even if they deny you: **Allah bears witness to what He sent down to you, having sent it down with His knowledge — and the angels bear witness. And Allah suffices as a Witness.**

## 29. The Final Call (4:167–175)

**4:167–169.** Those who disbelieve and bar others from Allah's way have strayed far; they will not be forgiven nor guided — *except to the road of Hell.*

**4:170.** *O mankind, the Messenger has come to you with the truth from your Lord, so believe — it is better for you.* And if you disbelieve, everything in the heavens and the earth is His anyway. **He is too rich to need your faith; your disbelief cannot touch Him.**

**4:171 — do not exceed the limits.** Ibn Kathir identifies *ghuluww*, going to extremes, as the characteristic failing here — the elevation of 'Isa from prophethood to divinity, and then the further elevation of those said to follow him, whose every word was treated as inspired, true or false (cf. 9:31, taking rabbis and monks as lords).

He immediately turns it on his own community with the Prophet's ﷺ own words:

> **Do not extol me as the Christians extolled 'Isa son of Maryam. I am only a servant — so say: the servant of Allah, and His Messenger.**

And when a man addressed him as *our master, son of our master, our best, son of our best*, he answered: **say what you have to say, and do not let Shaytan get hold of you. I am Muhammad, son of 'Abdullah, Allah's servant and Messenger — and I do not like to be raised above the rank Allah has given me.**

**What 'Isa is:** a Messenger of Allah; **His word which He conveyed to Maryam**; and **a spirit from Him.** Ibn Kathir is precise here:
- "His word" does not mean he *is* the word — Shadh bin Yahya: **'Isa came into being *because of* the word**, "Be." Qatadah: He said *Be*, and he was. This took the place of ordinary conception, since he had no father — hence the parallel with Adam in 3:59.
- **"A spirit from Him"** does not mean a part of Him. *From Him* is the same construction as in 45:13 (all that is in the heavens and earth is from Him) — meaning **from His creation**. Attributing something to Allah in this way — the she-camel of Allah, the House of Allah, the spirit of Allah — **is a form of honouring it**, not of dividing Him.
- Hence the hadith of 'Ubadah: whoever testifies to *la ilaha illa Allah*, that Muhammad ﷺ is His servant and Messenger, **that 'Isa is Allah's servant and Messenger, His word cast to Maryam and a spirit from Him**, and that Paradise and Hell are real — Allah admits him to Paradise, by whichever of its eight gates he wishes.

**"Do not say: three."** Ibn Kathir cross-references 5:73, 5:116 and 5:17, and gives a short history of the councils and the sects they produced — noting, with evident exasperation at their multiplication, the quip that **if ten Christians meet, they will come out with eleven denominations.**

**4:172–173.** Neither the Messiah nor the angels brought near are ever **too proud** to be servants of Allah. Ibn Abbas glosses "proud" as insolent. Those who believed and did good will be given their rewards **and more from His bounty**; those who disdained worship out of arrogance will find no protector besides Allah — as in 40:60, *those too proud to worship Me will enter Hell in humiliation.*

**4:174–175 — the closing invitation.**

> *O mankind, a decisive proof has come to you from your Lord, and We have sent down to you a clear light.*

The proof eliminates every excuse; the light — Ibn Jurayj and others: **the Qur'an**. And those who believe in Allah and **hold fast to Him** — worshipping Him and relying on Him in everything — He will admit to mercy and bounty from Himself, and **guide them to Himself by a straight path**: straight in this life, in belief and action; and straight in the Hereafter, the road that ends in the Gardens.

## 30. The Last Verse Ever Revealed (4:176)

Al-Bara' said: the last surah revealed was Bara'ah (Surah 9), and **the last *ayah* revealed was this one** — *they ask you for a ruling. Say: Allah gives you a ruling concerning Kalalah.*

The surah that opened with the ties of the womb closes on them.

**The ruling:** a man who dies leaving a sister but no child — she takes **half**; and he inherits from her if she has no child. Two sisters take **two-thirds**. Brothers and sisters together: the male takes twice the female's share.

Ibn Kathir notes that "leaving no child" must be understood as **leaving neither descendants nor ascendants**, because a surviving parent would exclude the sister entirely — by consensus. And he reports the disagreement over the daughter-and-sister case: **Ibn Abbas and Ibn az-Zubayr** read "no child" literally and gave the sister nothing if a daughter survived; the majority gave the daughter half and the sister the other half, relying on other evidence. Mu'adh bin Jabal had judged that way in the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime.

And the most instructive exchange in the section — **Abu Musa al-Ash'ari**, asked about a daughter, a granddaughter and a sister, gave the daughter half and the sister half, and said: go ask Ibn Mas'ud, though I expect he will agree with me. Ibn Mas'ud said: **"I would have gone astray then, and not been among the guided."** He gave the judgment of the Prophet ﷺ: the daughter one half, the granddaughter one sixth (completing two-thirds), and the remainder to the sister. When Abu Musa was told, he said: **"Do not ask me anything as long as this scholar is among you."**

Kalalah was the hardest question in the surah, and stayed hard. 'Umar said three things he wished he had asked the Prophet ﷺ about would have been dearer to him than red camels: **the succession, whether to fight those who withhold zakah, and Kalalah.** In another report, he named the grandfather's share, Kalalah, and a certain type of riba. He once gathered the Companions to announce a ruling on Kalalah "that women would discuss in their private rooms" — and a snake appeared in the house and the gathering broke up. *Had Allah willed it to happen, it would have happened.*

And when he pressed the Prophet ﷺ hardest on it, he was jabbed in the chest with a finger and told: **the verse at the end of Surah An-Nisa' should be enough for you.**

*"(Thus) does Allah make clear to you, lest you go astray. And Allah is the Knower of all things."*

## In One Breath

An-Nisa' begins by telling humanity it has **one origin**, and then spends 176 verses working out what that obligates. The weak are named one by one — orphans whose guardians are tempted by their money, women inherited like furniture, wives dreading desertion, slaves, servants, neighbours, wayfarers, the persecuted in Makkah who cry for a rescuer — and around each of them the surah builds a fence: fixed shares that cannot be redrawn, dowries that cannot be clawed back, degrees of kinship that cannot be crossed, blood that cannot be spilled on a guess, testimony that must be given even against your own father.

Running through it is a single measure of authority: **obey Allah, obey the Messenger, and obey those in command — but there is no obedience to any creature in disobedience to the Creator**, and no faith at all in one who will not bring his disputes to the Prophet's ﷺ judgment and accept it without resistance. Against that stand the hypocrites, described more thoroughly here than anywhere: swaying between two flocks, praying lazily and only when watched, waiting to see who wins.

And holding the whole structure up is the promise the Companions valued above the world: **He does not wrong anyone by an atom's weight; He does not forgive shirk, but everything else falls within His will; and repentance is accepted until the soul reaches the throat.** The law is heavy, the surah admits — *and Allah wishes to lighten your burden, for man was created weak.*

It ends by placing whoever obeys in the company he hopes to be raised with: **the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous** — and how excellent a company they are.
