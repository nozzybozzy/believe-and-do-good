*Revealed in Madinah · 129 ayat · "The Repentance" · also called Bara'ah and Al-Fadihah · among the last surahs revealed*

> A note on the source: the PDF's running header reads "Revealed in Makkah." This is an error in that edition. Ibn Kathir's own text is unambiguous — the surah came down after the return from Tabuk in the ninth year of Hijrah, and its closing verses were the last of the Qur'an to be revealed.

---

## 0. Its Three Names, and What Each Reveals

This surah is known by three names, and each one names a different thing it does:

| Name | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Bara'ah** | *Disavowal / Freedom from obligation* — the word it opens with. It dissolves the treaties with the idolaters. |
| **At-Tawbah** | *The Repentance* — because it is also the surah in which Allah turns in mercy to the Prophet, the Muhajirun, the Ansar, and the three men who were left waiting. |
| **Al-Fadihah** | *The Exposer* — Qatadah's name for it, because verse after verse strips the cover off the hypocrites of Al-Madinah. |

Hold those three together and you have the surah: **a severing, an exposing, and a returning.** The same revelation that closes the door on the idolaters and unmasks the hypocrites is the one that opens the door widest for those who told the truth about themselves.

---

## 1. Why There Is No Basmalah

The single most-noticed feature of this surah is what is missing from it.

Ibn Kathir's answer is textual rather than mystical: **the Companions did not write it there.** When the Mushaf was collected under Uthman bin Affan, the Basmalah was not placed at the head of Bara'ah, and the copies followed what they wrote. He does not embroider on this.

He does establish the surah's lateness with care, quoting Al-Bukhari's report from Al-Bara': the last *ayah* revealed concerned the ruling on *Al-Kalalah* (4:176), and the last *surah* revealed was Bara'ah.

---

## 2. The Announcement at Hajj (9:1–3)

The occasion is a scene, and Ibn Kathir tells it as one.

Returning from Tabuk, the Prophet ﷺ considered attending the Hajj that season, then remembered that the idolaters would be there as usual — and that they performed Tawaf naked. He disliked being among them for that, so he sent **Abu Bakr As-Siddiq** to lead the Hajj and teach the people their rites.

Then Bara'ah was revealed. He sent **Ali bin Abi Talib** after Abu Bakr, carrying the opening thirty or forty verses, to make the proclamation. Asked why he did not simply send it on with Abu Bakr, he answered that the delivery would only be accepted from a man of his own family.

Ali caught up with Abu Bakr on the road. Abu Bakr asked whether he had come as commander or follower. *A follower*, Ali said. Abu Bakr led the Hajj; on the Day of Sacrifice, Ali stood at Mina and proclaimed four things:

1. No disbeliever will enter Paradise.
2. No idolater will perform Hajj after this year.
3. No one will perform Tawaf naked.
4. **Whoever holds a treaty with the Messenger of Allah — it stands until its stated term.**

That fourth clause is the one modern readers most often miss, and Ibn Kathir puts it first among the legal points.

---

## 3. The Four Months, and Who They Applied To (9:2, 9:4)

This is the section where careful reading matters most, and Ibn Kathir is precise.

- **Open-ended treaties**, and treaties with less than four months left to run, were **capped at four months** — a grace period for free movement.
- **Treaties with a fixed term running longer than four months were honoured to their end**, however long that was. 9:4 states this as an explicit exception, conditional on two things: that the other party had not broken faith, and had not backed anyone against the Muslims.
- Allah closes the exception with praise of those who keep their word — *Allah loves those who have Taqwa* — read here as **those who keep their promises**.

The timing is concrete. Ali recited the verses on the Day of Arafah, so the four months ran through the remainder of Dhul-Hijjah, then Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal, and ten days of Rabi' ath-Thani.

---

## 4. The Ayah of the Sword (9:5)

Verse 5 is the most quoted and least contextualised verse of the Qur'an. Here is what Ibn Kathir actually attaches to it.

**Its scope in his reading:**
- *When the Sacred Months have passed* refers to the four-month grace period just described — the view of Mujahid, Amr bin Shu'ayb, Ibn Ishaq, Qatadah, As-Suddi, and Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd.
- *Wherever you find them* means the earth generally, **excluding the Sacred Area**, on the authority of 2:191.
- Ad-Dahhak called it the verse that abrogated every existing peace agreement with an idolater. Ibn Abbas said no idolater retained a treaty after Bara'ah, all prior agreements having expired by the tenth of Rabi' al-Akhir.

**And its exit clause, in the same verse:** *if they repent, establish the prayer and give the Zakah, then leave their way free.*

Ibn Kathir stops on this. The verse names the two obligations after the testimony of faith — **prayer, which is Allah's right, and Zakah, which is the right of the poor** — and he notes that this pairing is why the Qur'an so often mentions them together. It was on this verse, among others, that **Abu Bakr based his decision to fight those who withheld Zakah after the Prophet's death.**

---

## 5. And Immediately After the Sword — Safe Conduct (9:6)

Placed directly after the verse of the sword is its counterweight, and Ibn Kathir clearly wants the reader to feel the order.

If an idolater — one of those you were just commanded to fight — asks for protection, **grant it**, so that he may hear the Word of Allah. Recite the Qur'an to him. Then **escort him to safety**, all the way back to his own land. The reason given at the end of the verse: *because they are men who know not.*

Mujahid: this is for the man who comes to hear what was revealed to you. He is safe coming, safe while listening, and safe going home.

Ibn Kathir generalises the ruling: anyone arriving from hostile territory as an envoy, a trader, a negotiator, or to pay the Jizyah, is granted safe passage until he returns. He cites the Hudaybiyyah emissaries — Urwah bin Mas'ud, Mikraz bin Hafs, Suhayl bin Amr — who came to mediate, were astonished at the Muslims' regard for the Prophet ﷺ, went home and reported it, and this was among the reasons many of them later accepted Islam.

And the hard case: when Musaylimah's envoy affirmed to the Prophet's face that Musaylimah was a messenger, the Prophet ﷺ told him he would have struck his neck **were envoys not immune**. (That same man was executed years later under Ibn Mas'ud's governorship in Kufah — not as an envoy, but as a resident still professing it.)

---

## 6. Why the Disavowal Was Justified (9:7–15)

Ibn Kathir gives the reasoning rather than asserting the ruling:

- *So long as they are true to you, stand you true to them* — the Hudaybiyyah treaty was honoured from Dhul-Qa'dah of year 6 **until Quraysh themselves broke it**, backing Banu Bakr against Khuza'ah, the Prophet's allies, with killing inside the Sacred Area. Only then came the march on Makkah in Ramadan of year 8.
- After the conquest, the Quraysh who accepted Islam were freed — some two thousand, known afterwards as the *Tulaqa'*. Those who did not were **given four months of safe movement**. Among them were Safwan bin Umayyah and Ikrimah bin Abi Jahl, both of whom later became, in Ibn Kathir's words, excellent believers.
- *Ill* and *Dhimmah* in 9:8 are read by Ibn Abbas as **kinship** and **covenant** — the two things these opponents would disregard if they gained the upper hand.
- 9:11 repeats the door: repentance, prayer, Zakah — *then they are your brethren in religion.* The condition of entry is the same for everyone.

On 9:12, *fight the leaders of disbelief* — Ibn Kathir draws a ruling many later scholars leaned on: the verse is triggered by **attacking the religion with mockery and disparagement**, not merely by disbelief. He notes that Hudhayfah and Ali both held that "the people of this verse were never fought again," but Ibn Kathir judges the wording general even though the occasion was specific.

---

## 7. The Wisdom of Being Tested (9:16)

*Do you think you will be left alone before Allah has known who strove, and who took no Walijah besides Allah, His Messenger and the believers?*

*Walijah* — an intimate, a confidant on the other side.

Ibn Kathir lines this up with 29:1–3, 3:142, and 3:179 and draws the single conclusion: **the legislation of Jihad is itself a sorting mechanism**, distinguishing sincere intent from false. Not because Allah needs the information — He knows what happened, what will happen, and how what did not happen would have gone — but because the distinction must be made real.

---

## 8. Who Actually Maintains a Masjid (9:17–22)

The Quraysh had a boast: *we water the pilgrims, we maintain the Sacred House.* Al-Abbas made it himself as a prisoner at Badr — *while you were rushing ahead of us into Islam, we were serving the House and freeing the indebted.*

The answer is blunt: **these are not equal.** And Ibn Kathir gives Ibn Abbas's gloss on why — *I do not accept deeds performed in a state of Shirk.* Deeds do not float free of the one who does them.

There is a beautiful counter-image in the same passage. Where the idolaters *witness against themselves of disbelief*, Allah **testifies to the faith** of those who maintain His houses. As-Suddi's aside is memorable: ask a Christian his religion and he tells you; ask a Jew and he tells you; ask an idolater and he tells you too — no one has to prove their disbelief, they announce it.

And the Companions' saying, recorded from Amr bin Maymun: **the masjids are Allah's houses on earth, and He is generous to whoever visits Him in them.**

The dispute that occasioned the verse is recorded too: three men arguing near the Prophet's Minbar on a Friday — one said watering pilgrims was enough for him, one said maintaining the Sacred Masjid, the third said Jihad outranks both. Umar told them not to raise their voices there, and said they would ask after the prayer. The verse came down.

---

## 9. Loyalty Above Blood (9:23–24)

*Take not your fathers and brothers as supporters if they prefer disbelief to belief.*

Then the list — fathers, sons, brothers, spouses, kin, wealth you have gained, trade you fear will decline, dwellings you delight in. Ibn Kathir notes the shrewdness of the list: **it does not name vices.** Every item on it is a legitimate love. The warning is not against having them but against ranking them above Allah, His Messenger, and striving in His cause.

He pairs it with the exchange between Umar and the Prophet ﷺ. Umar said the Prophet was dearer to him than everything **except himself**. He was told: *no one believes until I am dearer to him than his own self.* Umar said: then now, by Allah, you are dearer to me than myself. The reply: **"Now, O Umar."**

And Ibn Umar's warning that the same passage carries: a people who take up *'Iynah* transactions, follow the tails of cattle, grow content with tillage and abandon Jihad will be given a disgrace not lifted until they return to their religion.

## 10. Hunayn: The Day Numbers Did Not Help (9:25–27)

Mujahid held this was the first passage of Bara'ah revealed. It is the surah's argument in narrative form.

Shawwal, year 8, just after the conquest of Makkah. Hawazin under Malik bin Awf, with Thaqif and others, brought their families, herds and full strength. The Muslims marched ten thousand strong from the conquest, plus two thousand *Tulaqa'*. **They were pleased at their numbers** — and this is the only battle in the Qur'an where Muslim self-satisfaction is named as the problem.

The ambush came at dawn in the valley. Arrows, then a mass charge, then flight. Ibn Kathir's picture of the moment is the passage worth keeping:

The Prophet ﷺ stayed on his **mule** — a slow animal, useless for either charge or escape — pushing it *towards* the enemy while Al-Abbas and Abu Sufyan bin Al-Harith held its ropes to hold it back. He called out his own name so that anyone who did not know him would: *I am the Prophet, no lie; I am the son of Abdul-Muttalib.* Between eighty and a hundred remained.

Then Al-Abbas, who had a carrying voice, was told to call: **"O Companions of the tree!"** — the men of the pledge at Ridwan — and **"O Companions of Surah Al-Baqarah!"** The answer came back across the valley: *Here we are.* Men whose camels would not turn dismounted, took their shields and ran back on foot.

He threw a handful of dust at the enemy after asking Allah to fulfil His promise, and they broke.

A man who fought that day **on the idolaters' side** gave the strangest testimony Ibn Kathir records: they had pursued the Muslims to the rider on the white mule when men with white faces met them and told them to go back in disgrace — *and that was the end for us.*

The aftermath is the *tawbah* the surah is named for. Twenty days later, at Ji'ranah, Hawazin came as Muslims. Offered the choice between their captives and their property, they chose their people — **six thousand prisoners released.** The war spoils went partly to the *Tulaqa'* to soften their hearts, a hundred camels each; Malik bin Awf, the enemy commander, received the same and was reappointed chief of his own people. He wrote a poem in praise of the man who had defeated him.

## 11. The Haram Closed, and the Jizyah (9:28–29)

**9:28** — idolaters are not to approach the Sacred Masjid after that year. The Companions raised an economic objection: *our markets will close, our trade will end.* The answer was a promise of provision from elsewhere — and Ibn Kathir, following Ibn Ishaq and others, reads the compensation concretely: **the Jizyah revenue of the next verse replaced the pilgrim trade.**

**9:29** — the order to fight the People of the Scripture until they pay the Jizyah. Ibn Kathir places this precisely: revealed in year 9, after the peninsula was secure, as the preparation for Tabuk against the Romans. Thirty thousand marched; some of Al-Madinah and the hypocrites around it stayed behind, in a year of drought and severe heat. The army camped about twenty days at Tabuk and returned without a battle.

His reasoning on why the People of the Scripture are addressed here: their prior scriptures pointed to the coming Prophet, so a genuine adherence to those scriptures would have carried them forward to him. Following them **after** rejecting him, he argues, shows the following was never really about their divine origin.

On the terms, Ibn Kathir reproduces the classical position of his era in strong language — that the *Dhimmah* are to remain in a subordinate station — and cites the conditions attributed to Umar bin Al-Khattab for the Christians of Ash-Sham: no new churches or monasteries, hospitality to Muslim travellers for three days, no public display of crosses or scripture in Muslim markets, no imitation of Muslim dress or arms, no obstruction of anyone wishing to embrace Islam, in return for protection of persons, property and worship.

*Read this section as what it is: a fourteenth-century Damascene scholar recording the legal settlement of his own tradition, in the register of his time. Ibn Kathir is reporting a body of rulings, not stating a universal ethic, and the summary above preserves his content without endorsing its tone.*

## 12. "They Took Their Rabbis and Monks as Lords" (9:30–33)

The finest hadith in this section explains a verse people trip over.

**Adi bin Hatim** — son of the famously generous Hatim At-Ta'i, and a Christian — came to Al-Madinah wearing a silver cross. The Prophet ﷺ recited this verse to him. Adi objected: *but they did not worship them.*

The answer:

> They made lawful for them what was forbidden and forbade what was lawful, **and they obeyed them. That is how they worshipped them.**

Obedience in legislation *is* the worship. Hudhayfah and Ibn Abbas explained the verse the same way.

The Prophet ﷺ then asked Adi a run of questions — did you flee so that *Allahu Akbar* would not be said? Do you know anything greater than Allah? Did you flee so that *La ilaha illallah* would not be said? Do you know any deity besides Him? Adi accepted Islam, and Ibn Kathir notes that the Prophet's ﷺ face lit up.

On *they want to extinguish Allah's light with their mouths* — Ibn Kathir's image: like a man trying to blow out the sun.

A linguistic gem is buried here. **A *Kafir* is, at root, one who covers.** Night is *kafir* because it covers with darkness; the farmer is *kafir* because he covers seed in the soil — and 57:20 uses the word in exactly that agricultural sense.

## 13. The Three Kinds of Corruptible Leader (9:34–35)

This is one of the surah's most transferable passages, and Ibn Kathir treats it as a diagnosis of any society, not a polemic against another one.

He identifies **three classes on whom people depend**: their **scholars**, their **worshippers**, and their **wealthy**. When these three go bad, the society goes with them. Ibn al-Mubarak's line, quoted here: what ruined religion but kings, and wicked *Ahbar* and *Ruhban*.

The warning is turned explicitly inward. Sufyan bin Uyaynah: **our corrupt scholars resemble the Jewish rabbis; our misguided worshippers resemble the Christian monks.** And the hadith that you will follow the ways of those before you step by step — *Jews and Christians? Who else.*

How they devour wealth wrongly: **selling the religion for standing**, using position among people to take their property, then confusing truth with falsehood before followers who cannot tell the difference.

Then hoarding. *Kanz*, per Malik from Ibn Umar, is **wealth on which Zakah has not been paid** — not wealth as such. Ibn Umar's own note is that the verse preceded the ordaining of Zakah, and once Zakah came it became the purifier of wealth; Umar bin Abdul-Aziz held the verse abrogated by 9:103.

The punishment fits the crime with terrible precision: the very metal heated and pressed to forehead, flank and back. Ibn Kathir generalises the principle — *whoever craves a thing and prefers it to obedience will be punished by it* — and gives Abu Lahab's wife as the illustration: she helped him in this life and will carry firewood to him in the next.

And the hadith of the treasure that becomes a bald venomous serpent following its owner: *Who are you?* — **I am your treasure that you left behind.**

The exchange that seals it: Abu Dharr, in Ash-Sham, recited this verse. **Mu'awiyah said it concerned only the People of the Book. Abu Dharr said it concerned them and us.** Abu Dharr ended his days at Rabadhah.

## 14. Twelve Months, Four Sacred, and the Sin of Rearranging Them (9:36–37)

*The number of months with Allah is twelve, ordained on the day He created the heavens and the earth; of them four are sacred.*

Ibn Kathir anchors this in the Farewell Sermon: time has come round to its original form; the four are **Dhul-Qa'dah, Dhul-Hijjah, Muharram, and Rajab of Mudar** (between Jumada and Sha'ban).

**Why these four?** His answer is functional and rather lovely — they are arranged around travel and safety:

| Month | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Dhul-Qa'dah | Safe travel *to* Hajj |
| Dhul-Hijjah | The Hajj itself |
| Muharram | Safe travel *home* |
| Rajab | Set alone mid-year, so distant Arabians could make Umrah and return safely |

*So wrong not yourselves therein* — Ibn Abbas ties this back to all twelve months, with the four intensified. **Sin is heavier in a sanctified time**, as it is in the sanctified city (22:25). Qatadah's summary is worth memorising: injustice is always wrong, but Allah magnifies what He wills; He chose messengers from among angels and men, His speech above all speech, the masjids above other ground, Ramadan and the sacred months above other months, Friday above other days, Laylat al-Qadr above other nights — **so sanctify what Allah has sanctified.**

On fighting: initiating hostilities in these months is forbidden; **responding is permitted** (2:194, 2:191). Ibn Kathir handles the obvious objection — the siege of At-Ta'if ran into a sacred month — by distinguishing **continuing a war** from **starting one**.

**An-Nasi' (9:37)** is the practice this all condemns: pre-Islamic Arabs, finding three consecutive sacred months too long to sit still, would **swap Muharram for Safar** — keeping the *number* four while abolishing the *thing*. Ibn Ishaq traces the family that made a hereditary office of it, down to Abu Thumamah Junadah bin Awf, who stood each Hajj announcing which month he had decided was sacred that year, prefacing it with *I am never refuted*.

Ibn Kathir's heading for the passage is the sharpest thing in it: **preferring opinion to Allah's law**. The sin is not disregarding the rule outright; it is keeping the arithmetic while gutting the substance.

## 15. Tabuk: The Call to March (9:38–41)

*What is the matter with you that when you are asked to march, you cling heavily to the earth?*

Ibn Kathir sets the scene physically: the fruit was ripe, the shade was good, the heat was ferocious, and the journey was long. The verse names the real calculation — *are you pleased with this life over the Hereafter?* — and then shrinks the thing being chosen:

> The life of this world compared to the Hereafter is like a man dipping his finger in the sea. Let him look at what comes back on it.

Al-A'mash: it is a traveller's ration. And Abdul-Aziz bin Marwan, dying, calling for his shroud, looking at it and asking whether **this is all he ends up with**, then weeping.

**9:41 — *March forth, light or heavy.*** Abu Ad-Duha held this was the first of Bara'ah revealed. Read variously as **rich or poor, strong or weak, young or old, eager or reluctant.**

Abu Talhah read it and said: *Allah has called us out whether we are old or young.* His children offered to go in his place, having watched him fight beside the Prophet ﷺ, then Abu Bakr, then Umar. He refused, went to sea, and died there; they could find no island to bury him on for nine days, and his body did not change.

And the old man of Damascus, eyebrows sunk over his eyes, riding out with the army at Hims. Told he surely had an excuse, he answered that Allah had called them out light or heavy — **and that those Allah loves, He tests.**

As-Suddi records that the sweep of 9:41 was hard on people and considered it eased by 9:91, the verse of legitimate excuses.

## 16. The Two in the Cave (9:40)

Set inside the mobilisation passage is the surah's quietest verse.

*If you do not help him, Allah already did* — when the disbelievers drove him out, **the second of two**, in the cave of Thawr, three days, while the pursuers combed the road.

Abu Bakr, afraid — and Ibn Kathir is careful to say afraid **for the Messenger**, not for himself — said that if any of them looked down at his feet he would see them. The answer:

> **O Abu Bakr, what do you think of two, with Allah as their third?**

Ibn Abbas on the closing clause: *the word of those who disbelieved* is Shirk; *the Word of Allah* is *La ilaha illallah.* And the hadith that settles what fighting counts: a man asked about one who fights for courage, for tribal honour, for display — which is in Allah's cause? **He who fights so that Allah's word is uppermost.**

## 17. The Anatomy of Hypocrisy (9:42–70, 9:73–96)

This is the longest stretch of the surah, and Ibn Kathir reads it as a portrait built from many angles. Gathering the traits:

- **9:42** — had it been near gain and easy travel, they would have come. The distance was long. Distance is the honest test of motive.
- **9:43** — *May Allah forgive you; why did you give them leave?* Ibn Kathir records Awn's observation that this is the gentlest criticism imaginable: **the pardon arrives before the rebuke.** Qatadah notes that permission to excuse people was later granted at 24:62.
- **9:45** — the doubting heart *wavers*: a step forward, a step back, belonging to neither side.
- **9:47** — Allah disliked their going, **because they would have added only disorder** — rumour, discord, and men in the ranks inclined to listen to them. Ibn Ishaq names Abdullah bin Ubayy and Al-Jadd bin Qays among the chiefs who asked leave.
- **9:49** — *Grant me leave and do not put me to trial.* Al-Jadd bin Qays pleaded that he could not trust himself around Roman women. The reply: **they have already fallen into the trial** — the fitnah he actually fell into, staying behind, was worse than the one he claimed to fear.
- **9:50–51** — your good news grieves them; your setback pleases them. The reply given to the Prophet ﷺ is pure *tawakkul*: nothing reaches us but what Allah has decreed; **He is our Mawla.**
- **9:52** — *do you await for us anything but one of the two best things* — martyrdom or victory. Ibn Kathir keeps the gloss simple, because the verse is: the believer's two possible outcomes are **both good**.
- **9:53–54** — spending is refused not for its amount but for what precedes it. **Deeds are accepted when faith comes first.** They come to prayer lazily and give unwillingly; Allah is *Tayyib* and accepts only what is *Tayyib*.
- **9:56–57** — they swear they are of you, out of fear. If they found a fort, a cave, or a hole in the ground, they would bolt for it. **They are with you by necessity, not affection.**
- **9:61** — they mock the Prophet ﷺ as *all ear*, believing anything said to him. The answer reframes the insult as a virtue: **he is an ear of good for you** — he knows who is truthful, and he is a mercy to those who believe.
- **9:64** — they fear a surah exposing them. *Go on mocking; Allah will bring out what you fear.* This is where Qatadah's name for the surah comes from: **Al-Fadihah, the Exposer.**
- **9:65–66** — *we were only talking and joking.* The reply is the surah's most quoted line on the subject: **was it at Allah, His verses, and His Messenger that you were mocking?** Ibn Umar remembered the man afterwards, clinging to the camel's harness in the flying stones, still pleading that it was only idle talk.
- **9:67** — the hypocrites are *one from another*: they command evil, forbid good, and close their hands. **They forgot Allah, so He forgot them** — treated them as forgotten.
- **9:79** — the meanest trait of all. When a rich man gave, they said he was showing off; when a poor man gave one *Sa'* of dates, they said Allah had no need of it. Ibn Kathir gives the scene: Abu Aqil, who worked a night hauling water for two measures of dates, kept one and brought the other; Abdur-Rahman bin Awf gave four thousand dirhams and was accused of display. **There is no amount that satisfies a mocker — which is how you know the mockery was never about the amount.**

Against all this, **9:71–72** gives the believers' portrait in one line, and it is the exact inversion: *the believing men and women are supporters of one another; they enjoin good, forbid evil, establish prayer, give Zakah, and obey Allah and His Messenger.* Ibn Kathir attaches the two great images of the body politic — the believers as a building whose parts brace each other (the Prophet ﷺ interlocking his fingers), and as **one body: when a part falls ill, the whole runs a fever and cannot sleep.**

And the promise: gardens, dwellings, and then the thing that outranks them all — ***Ridwan*, Allah's good pleasure.** Ibn Kathir closes it with the hadith of the people of Paradise being asked whether they are pleased, protesting that they have been given what no creature was given, and being told: **I grant you My pleasure, and I will never be angry with you after this.**

## 18. Where the Zakah Goes (9:60)

Ibn Kathir notes the placement: the hypocrites had just questioned the Prophet's ﷺ fairness in distributing charity, so **Allah divides it Himself and delegates the decision to no one.** The order of the list is meaningful — the poor come first because their need is most pressing.

| Category | Ibn Kathir's clarification |
|---|---|
| **Al-Fuqara'** (poor) | Per Ibn Abbas and Mujahid: the one who does not ask. Per Qatadah: the one unable to work. Not for the wealthy or the able-bodied earner. |
| **Al-Masakin** (needy) | *"The needy is not the one who goes about asking for a mouthful."* It is the one whose condition is unknown, who does not beg. |
| **Collectors** | Paid from it for their work — **except the Prophet's ﷺ relatives**, for whom charity is *the dirt of the people*. |
| **Al-Mu'allafatu Qulubuhum** | Several sub-types: given to incline them to Islam (Safwan bin Umayyah: *he kept giving until he became the most beloved man to me, after being the most hated*); given to steady a new believer's faith; given to draw in a following. |
| **Ar-Riqab** | Slaves purchasing their freedom by contract — and, per Ibn Abbas and Al-Hasan, buying and freeing outright. Al-Bara's hadith distinguishes freeing a person yourself from **helping pay another's price**; both are named separately. |
| **Al-Gharimun** (debtors) | Those who took on debt reconciling disputes; guarantors ruined by a call; those insolvent; those who repented from a sin that left debt. |
| **In Allah's cause** | Fighters who receive no stipend from the treasury. |
| **Ibn As-Sabil** | The stranded traveller — **even if he is wealthy at home** — and one setting out without means. |

Qabisah's hadith limits begging to three cases only: debt taken on for reconciliation, disaster that consumed one's wealth, and poverty attested by three responsible members of one's people. Outside these, it is unlawful gain.

And the five exceptions permitting a wealthy person to receive Zakah: the collector, the buyer of a charity item with his own money, the debtor, the fighter, and **the rich man who receives it as a gift from a poor recipient.**

## 19. The Plot at Al-Aqabah, and Seventy Times (9:74, 9:80, 9:84)

**The plot.** Hudhayfah bin Al-Yaman, holding the Prophet's ﷺ camel by night on the road back from Tabuk, with Ammar leading it, when riders intercepted them at the pass. The Prophet ﷺ shouted and they scattered. Masked, but their mounts were recognised. Asked what they had intended: **to crowd him and throw him from the pass into the valley.**

Told they could be named and executed, he refused — because the Arabs would say that Muhammad used men to fight and killed them when Allah gave him victory through them. He invoked something else on them instead.

This is why Hudhayfah was called **the keeper of the secret**: he alone was given their names.

**Seventy times.** *Whether you ask forgiveness for them or not — even seventy times — Allah will not forgive them.* Ibn Kathir explains **seventy is an Arabic idiom of magnitude, not a countable limit.** The point is that the door is shut, not that seventy-one would work.

**The funeral.** When Abdullah bin Ubayy — the chief hypocrite — died, his son asked for the Prophet's ﷺ shirt as a shroud and for the funeral prayer. He gave both. Umar took hold of his robe and objected. The Prophet ﷺ said he had been given a choice by the wording of the verse, and that he would ask more than seventy times. Umar said plainly: *he is a hypocrite.* The prayer was offered, the Prophet ﷺ walked with the procession and stood at the grave until burial.

**Then 9:84 came down**, forbidding it, and he never prayed over a hypocrite again.

Ibn Kathir lets both things stand: the mercy that reached even for that man, and the revelation that closed it. Umar's own comment, recorded here, is astonishment at his own boldness in speaking that way to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.

There is a small human detail he keeps: the son's name was Al-Hubab. The Prophet ﷺ renamed him **Abdullah bin Abdullah**, because *Al-Hubab* is a name of the devil.

## 20. The Excuses That Were Accepted (9:90–93)

The surah is merciless about false excuses, which is exactly what makes its treatment of true ones so striking.

*No blame on the weak, the ill, or those who find nothing to spend* — provided they are sincere to Allah and His Messenger. Ibn Kathir separates **permanent incapacity** (blindness, lameness) from **temporary** (illness, poverty), and adds the condition: no blame **so long as those who stay behind do not sow discouragement among those who go.**

Then the verse that carries the surah's tenderness — **9:92, the men who came asking for mounts** and were told there were none, and *turned back with their eyes overflowing with tears of grief that they could not find anything to spend.*

Not tears at missing a battle. **Tears at having nothing to give.**

And the hadith placed beside it, from Anas, in the Sahihayn: *there are men in Al-Madinah who were with you in every valley you crossed.* The Companions asked, *while they are still in Al-Madinah?* **Yes — they were held back by a valid excuse.**

By contrast, 9:93 — the complaint stands against **the rich who ask exemption** and are content to stay behind.

There is also an unexpected use of 9:91 as a du'a: Bilal bin Sa'd at a rain prayer, getting the congregation to acknowledge that wrong had been done, then saying — *we hear Your words that there is no way against the doers of good; we admit our errors.* Rain fell.

## 21. The Bedouins, and the Foremost (9:97–100)

*The Bedouins are worst in disbelief and hypocrisy, and likelier not to know the limits Allah has revealed.*

Ibn Kathir immediately blocks the racial reading: **among the Bedouins are disbelievers, hypocrites and believers**, and the verse concerns proximity to knowledge, not blood. The mechanism is given in a hadith: **whoever dwells in the wilds grows hard-hearted; whoever chases game grows heedless; whoever attends rulers falls into fitnah.**

He illustrates the ignorance with a story that stings: a Bedouin, admiring Zayd bin Sawhan's speech but suspicious of his missing hand, admits **he does not know which hand is cut for theft.** Zayd's reply is simply to recite the verse.

Then 9:99, the correction: **and among the Bedouins are those who believe**, who see what they spend as nearness to Allah and a claim on the Messenger's prayer — *indeed it is nearness for them.* The category was never the point.

**9:100 — the foremost.** Allah is pleased with the first of the Muhajirun and Ansar and *those who followed them in excellence*. Identified variously as the people of the pledge at Ridwan (Ash-Sha'bi), or those who prayed toward both Qiblahs (Abu Musa, Ibn Al-Musayyib, Ibn Sirin, Al-Hasan, Qatadah).

Ibn Kathir turns unusually personal here, and it is worth reading as the voice of a man in a sectarian age: he condemns in the strongest terms the cursing of the Companions, singling out **Abu Bakr** for defence, and states the principle of the people of the Sunnah — **to be pleased with those Allah declared Himself pleased with.**

## 22. Those Who Mixed Good With Evil (9:102–105)

Having drawn the hypocrite in full, the surah now names a third category, and it is where most readers actually live.

Not deniers, not doubters — **believers who stayed behind out of laziness and comfort, then admitted it.** *They mixed a righteous deed with another that was evil.*

Ibn Abbas identifies Abu Lubabah and a handful of companions, who **tied themselves to the pillars of the Masjid** and refused release from anyone but the Messenger ﷺ. When this verse came down, he untied them.

Ibn Kathir insists the verse is **general** despite the specific occasion: it covers every sinner who combines good and evil. And he gives the vision from Al-Bukhari — the men whose bodies were half beautiful and half hideous, sent into a river to submerge, coming out whole. *These are the ones who mixed a righteous deed with an evil one; Allah has pardoned them.*

**Then 9:103, the mechanism of repair:** *Take charity from their wealth to purify them, and pray for them; your prayer is a Sakan for them.* Ibn Abbas: a mercy. This is the verse Abu Bakr invoked against those who claimed Zakah was owed to the Prophet ﷺ personally and to no successor.

**And 9:104:** *do they not know that Allah accepts repentance from His servants and accepts charities?* Ibn Mas'ud's phrasing is the one that lodges: **the charity falls into Allah's hand before it falls into the beggar's.** Grown by its Owner like a foal until a single date is the size of Uhud.

**9:105 — *Work; Allah will see your work, and His Messenger, and the believers.*** Aishah's use of it is a warning against being impressed too early, sharpened by the hadith from Anas: do not admire a man's deeds until you see how he ends — a man may work for Paradise for years and then change, and a man may work for the Fire and then change. **When Allah wills good for a servant, He employs him** — directs him to a good deed and takes his soul in it.

---

## 23. Two Masjids (9:107–110)

The story behind these verses is one of the most complete narratives in the surah.

**Abu Amir Ar-Rahib** — "the Monk" — was a Khazraji ascetic, a Christian of standing in Al-Madinah before the Hijrah. The Prophet's ﷺ arrival and the victory at Badr made him an enemy. He fled to Quraysh, fought at Uhud (Ibn Kathir notes he dug the pits, into one of which the Prophet ﷺ fell, injuring his face and breaking a tooth), tried to win his own tribe over mid-battle and was cursed off the field, then went to **Heraclius** seeking Roman force against Al-Madinah.

He wrote to his sympathisers there: **build me a stronghold** — a base for messengers, an outpost for when I come with an army.

They built it as a masjid, next to Quba', and finished it before Tabuk. Then they invited the Prophet ﷺ to pray in it — *for the weak and the ill on rainy nights* — which would have made it his by endorsement. He deferred: **if we return, Allah willing.**

On the road back, one or two days from Al-Madinah, Jibril brought the news. **He sent men ahead to pull it down before he arrived.** Jabir bin Abdullah said he saw smoke rising from it.

Against it stands **Masjid Quba'**, *founded on Taqwa from the first day* — one prayer in it like an Umrah; the Prophet ﷺ used to visit it riding and walking.

The praise of its people is specific: *men who love to purify themselves*. Asked what they did, they said they had Jewish neighbours who used water after relieving themselves, **and they did the same.** The whole Qur'anic commendation rests on a small habit learned from the neighbours.

Ibn Kathir extracts the general ruling: **pray in masjids built for Allah alone, with people who take their purification seriously.**

And the closing warning of 9:110 — the building they built **remains a doubt in their hearts** until their hearts are cut to pieces, i.e. until death, as the Salaf read it. The comparison he draws is to those who worshipped the calf and retained the inclination. **What you build into your life builds itself into you.**

## 24. The Purchase (9:111–112)

*Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their wealth, and theirs is the Garden.*

Ibn Kathir's comment on the economics is the point: **Allah already owns both, and gave both** — He is buying what is His and paying for it anyway. Al-Hasan and Qatadah: He purchased them and **raised their worth by doing so.**

Shimr bin Atiyyah's line: every Muslim has a sale on his neck he must conclude with Allah — he either completes it or dies without it.

*A promise binding on Him in the Tawrah, the Injil and the Qur'an* — Ibn Kathir stresses the triple guarantee, then: **who is truer to his covenant than Allah?**

And then, immediately, **9:112 defines who these purchased ones actually are** — and note that not one item is a battlefield:

> Those who repent · who worship · who praise · who fast · who bow · who prostrate · who enjoin good and forbid evil · **and who keep the limits of Allah.**

Ibn Kathir walks the list as a structure: repentance clears the ground; worship is the whole of statement and action, of which **praise is the best statement**; *As-Sa'ihun* is read as **fasting**; bowing and prostrating are the prayer; and the last two items turn outward — **they benefit Allah's creation and know what to do and what to avoid.** Worship the Real, and advise the creation. That is the whole of it.

---

## 25. The Limits of Intercession (9:113–116)

*It is not for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they are near kin, after it has become clear that they are of the Fire.*

Two scenes carry it, and both are painful.

**Abu Talib**, dying, with Abu Jahl and Abdullah bin Abi Umayyah at his side. The Prophet ﷺ asking for one word he could plead with. The two men asking whether he would leave the religion of Abdul-Muttalib. Abu Talib choosing that religion. The Prophet ﷺ saying he would seek forgiveness for him **as long as he was not forbidden** — and then this verse, and 28:56, coming down.

**His mother's grave.** He asked permission to visit it and was granted it; he asked permission to seek forgiveness for her and **was not**. Those with him said they never saw him weep as he did that day.

And **Ibrahim**, whose prayer for his father was on the strength of a promise he had made, until it became clear his father was an enemy of Allah — *and he dissociated himself.* Ibn Abbas: he kept asking until his father died, and knew then.

*Ibrahim was Awwah and forbearing* — *Awwah* read as one who invokes constantly, who supplicates with humility, who is tender-hearted, who is certain, who praises much.

Ibn Abbas records a practical distinction the Companions drew from this: they stopped seeking forgiveness for **the polytheist dead**, and were not stopped from praying for **the living**, up until their death. The door closes only when it closes.

**9:115 — *Allah does not lead a people astray after guiding them until He makes clear to them what they should avoid.*** Ibn Jarir's reading: no one is held accountable for a prohibition before it is communicated. **Guidance and misguidance both begin after the command arrives.**

## 26. The Three Who Were Left Behind (9:117–119)

This is the summit of the surah and the reason it is called At-Tawbah. Ibn Kathir gives it at full length in Ka'b bin Malik's own voice, and it deserves to be kept whole.

**The hardship first (9:117).** Tabuk in a year of drought and terrible heat, two men splitting one date, taking turns sucking a single date and drinking water and passing it on. Umar's memory of the thirst so severe they thought their necks would part — a man going out for water and fearing he would not return; men slaughtering camels to squeeze the fluid from their intestines. Abu Bakr asked the Prophet ﷺ to pray; he raised his hands, and rain fell in abundance — **and they found afterward that it had not fallen beyond their camp.** That the verse of forgiveness names *the Prophet, the Muhajirun and the Ansar* — after the hearts of a group of them had nearly deviated — is Ibn Kathir's point: even that generation, at that pitch of hardship, needed forgiving.

**Then Ka'b.** He had never been better off, never had two riding camels before. He kept meaning to prepare. The fruit was ripe and the shade was pleasant. He would go out to get ready and come back having done nothing, telling himself he could catch up any time. The army left. He went out the next morning and came back having done nothing. And the next. Until it was too late, and when he walked through Al-Madinah he saw around him **only men suspected of hypocrisy, or men Allah had excused.**

At Tabuk, the Prophet ﷺ asked after him. A man of Banu Salimah said Ka'b was held back by his fine clothes and his own reflection. **Mu'adh bin Jabal said: what an evil thing to say — we know nothing but good of him.** The Prophet ﷺ said nothing.

Hearing of the return, Ka'b began composing excuses, canvassing his family for advice — and then, when he learned the Messenger ﷺ was actually approaching, **every false excuse left his mind and he knew nothing but the truth would save him.**

Over eighty men came with sworn excuses. They were accepted outwardly; their hearts were left to Allah.

Ka'b came, greeted him, and received *the smile of an angry man.* **"Come."** And then the question: what kept you? Did you not buy an animal to carry you?

His answer is the axis of the whole surah:

> Had I been sitting before any man on earth other than you, I could have escaped his anger with an excuse — I have been given the power of speech. **But I knew that if I pleased you today with a lie, Allah would soon make you angry with me; and if I told you the truth and you were angry, I could still hope for Allah's forgiveness.**

*As for this man, he has told the truth. Get up until Allah decides your case.*

**Then fifty nights.** The Muslims were forbidden to speak to the three. His own people pressed him to go back and confess to a lie like the others; he nearly did — until he learned two men had said what he said, **Murarah bin Ar-Rabi' and Hilal bin Umayyah, both men of Badr.** Their example steadied him.

The details Ka'b remembers are the ones anyone would: that the land itself began to feel foreign to him. That he was the youngest and strongest of the three, so he still went out to the prayers and the markets, where no one spoke to him. That he would greet the Prophet ﷺ and watch his lips to see whether they moved in reply. That when he prayed near him, the Prophet ﷺ would look at him — and look away the moment he looked back.

That he climbed the garden wall of **Abu Qatadah**, his cousin and the dearest person to him, and greeted him, and got nothing; and asked him by Allah whether he knew that Ka'b loved Allah and His Messenger, and got silence; and asked a third time, and was told: *Allah and His Messenger know better.* **He went back over the wall in tears.**

That a Nabatean from Ash-Sham came through the market asking for him and handed him a letter from the **king of Ghassan**: we hear your friend has treated you harshly; Allah has not placed you in a house where you should be humiliated; **come to us and we will honour you.** Ka'b read it, said *this too is part of the trial,* and put it in the oven.

At forty nights, the order came to separate from his wife. He asked whether he should divorce her; no — only keep apart. Hilal bin Umayyah's wife asked permission to keep serving her husband, an old man with no servant; permitted, on condition he not approach her. She said he had not stopped weeping since it began. Ka'b's family suggested he ask the same permission; **he refused, saying he did not know what the Messenger ﷺ would say, since he was a young man.**

On the fiftieth morning, after Fajr, on the roof of one of their houses, **in exactly the state the verse describes — the earth narrow for all its width, and his own soul narrow within him** — he heard a man shouting from the top of Mount Sal': *O Ka'b bin Malik, rejoice!*

He fell in prostration.

The runner who reached him first got the clothes off Ka'b's back — **the only ones he owned that day** — and he had to borrow another set to go to the Masjid. When he came in, the people received him in waves, congratulating him. **Talhah bin Ubaydullah rose and crossed the room to take his hand.** Ka'b never forgot it: *none of the Muhajirun stood for me but Talhah.*

The Prophet's ﷺ face was shining — Ka'b says that when he was pleased his face shone like a piece of the moon, and they all knew it. **"Rejoice in the best day you have seen since your mother bore you."**

*Is this forgiveness from you or from Allah?* — **From Allah.**

Ka'b offered to give away all his wealth. He was told to keep some, as better for him; he kept his share of Khaybar. And he made a vow: since Allah saved him by his truthfulness, he would **speak nothing but the truth for the rest of his life** — and said he knew of no Muslim more helped in this than himself.

His own summary, which Ibn Kathir clearly regards as the moral of the surah: apart from being guided to Islam, **no blessing was ever greater to him than that he did not lie to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and so did not perish as the liars perished.** He then recited what was said of them — *they are Rijs, and Hell is their home* (9:95–96) — and set it beside what was said of him.

Ibn Kathir adds the fine distinction Ka'b himself drew: the verse does not describe them as *those who stayed behind* in the sense of the hypocrites. **It describes the deferral of the decision** — the others were dealt with at once on their oaths; these three were left with Allah.

**9:119 — *O you who believe, have Taqwa of Allah, and be with the truthful.*** This is the ruling drawn from the story. Ibn Kathir attaches Ibn Mas'ud's hadith:

> Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness to Paradise, and a man keeps telling the truth until he is written with Allah as *Siddiq.* Lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness to the Fire, and a man keeps lying until he is written with Allah as a great liar.

## 27. Nothing Is Wasted (9:120–121)

The consolation clause, and the most generous accounting in the Qur'an.

The people of Al-Madinah should not have stayed behind, nor preferred themselves to him. And why not? **Because of what they forfeited.**

Not a thirst, not a fatigue, not a hunger in Allah's cause; **not a step taken that angers a disbeliever; not an injury inflicted on an enemy** — but it is written for them as a righteous deed.

Ibn Kathir separates the two verses cleanly: **9:120 covers what is *not* in your control** — thirst, exhaustion, hunger, the enemy's reaction — and credits it anyway, as a consequence of the good deed you did choose. **9:121 covers what is:** every contribution, small or large, and every valley crossed, recorded so Allah may reward them by the best of what they did.

Qatadah's line: **the further a people march from their families in Allah's cause, the nearer they come to Allah.**

And Uthman bin Affan, who at the appeal for the army of hardship pledged a hundred camels with saddles and supplies; was exhorted again and pledged a hundred more; the Prophet ﷺ came down a step of the Minbar and exhorted again, and he pledged a hundred more. And in another report, a thousand dinars poured into the Prophet's ﷺ lap, who turned the coins over with his hand saying: **nothing Uthman does after today will harm him.**

## 28. Not Everyone Marches (9:122)

*It is not for the believers to go forth all together. Why should not a party from every group go forth, to gain understanding in religion, and to warn their people when they return?*

Ibn Kathir notes that some of the Salaf treated this as **abrogating** the general mobilisation of 9:41 and 9:120. He prefers a reading that harmonises rather than abrogates: **it distributes.** A party from each community goes; those who go with the Prophet ﷺ **gain both** — the campaign and the revelation that came down during it — and carry the learning home.

Ibn Abbas's picture is of a rotation: expeditions going out while a group stayed with the Prophet ﷺ, and whichever group was present when revelation came would teach it to the returning one. *Allah revealed some of the Qur'an to your Prophet, and we learned it.*

After the Prophet ﷺ, Ibn Kathir applies it directly: **in every community, some must go for knowledge and some for defence** — both become communal obligations discharged by a part.

It is worth noticing what this verse does at this point in the surah. After a hundred and twenty verses on failing to march, the last word on marching is: **the seeking of understanding is itself a going-forth.**

---

## 29. The Nearest First (9:123)

*Fight those of the disbelievers who are near you, and let them find harshness in you.*

Ibn Kathir reads it as a **sequence, not a scope** — concentric, starting closest. He maps it historically: the Arabian peninsula first, then, once Makkah, Al-Madinah, At-Ta'if, Yemen, Yamamah, Khaybar, Hadramawt were secure, the move to the People of the Scripture; and the Romans first among those because they were nearest, and were of the Book.

On *harshness*, he gives the balancing verses immediately (5:54, 48:29): **humble to the believers, firm toward the enemy.** The complete believer, he says, is gentle with his brother and hard on his adversary — and the two are one disposition, not two.

The passage that follows is Ibn Kathir the historian, and it turns melancholy. He traces the expansion under Abu Bakr — who held the religion together when it nearly came apart, brought back those who left it, and took Zakah from those who refused it — then Umar, then Uthman, when Islam *wore its widest garment*.

**And then he describes his own age.** Once turmoil began and Muslim rulers turned their enmity on each other, the enemy came into the outposts and then to the capitals, and took whole lands, **because the kings were too busy with one another.** He ends with a prayer rather than a boast. Written in the shadow of the Mongols and the Crusades, this is not triumphalism; it is a man explaining a defeat to himself, and locating the cause **inside** rather than outside.

## 30. Faith Goes Up and Down (9:124–127)

Whenever a surah came down, the hypocrites would ask each other **which of you has been increased in faith by this?**

The answer is the verse's own: *as for those who believe, it increases them in faith, and they rejoice.*

Ibn Kathir calls this among **the strongest proofs that faith increases and decreases** — the position of the Salaf and of most later imams, which he says many scholars report as consensus, and which he treated at length in his introduction to Sahih Al-Bukhari.

The counterpart is grim: for the diseased heart, the same surah **adds impurity to impurity.** The Qur'an is a healing (17:82), and for those who refuse it, deafness and blindness (41:44). His analogy: food that disagrees with a man makes him worse the more he is fed it. **The same revelation, meeting two different hearts, moves them in opposite directions.**

**9:126** — they are tried once or twice a year and neither repent nor take heed. Mujahid: tried with drought and hunger.

**9:127** — the physical description of avoidance. A surah comes down, and they glance at one another — *is anyone watching?* — and slip out. Ibn Kathir puts it beside 74:49–51: **like wild donkeys fleeing a lion.** They cannot sit where the truth is being said. And the consequence, in the verse's own words: *Allah has turned their hearts away* — because they turned first (61:5).

## 31. The Last Verses Revealed (9:128–129)

The surah that opened with the severing of ties ends with the tenderest description of the Messenger ﷺ in the Qur'an. Ubayy bin Ka'b said these were **the last verses of the Qur'an to be revealed**; Zayd bin Thabit said he found the closing verse of Bara'ah with Khuzaymah bin Thabit when the Mushaf was gathered.

*There has come to you a Messenger from among yourselves.*

- **From among yourselves** — one of you.
- **Your suffering grieves him** — Ibn Kathir attaches the two hadiths on ease: *I was sent with the easy Hanifiyyah*, and *this religion is easy.*
- **Concerned for you** — for your guidance and your benefit here and hereafter.
- **To the believers, full of pity and mercy** — *Ra'uf, Rahim*, two of Allah's own names given to His Messenger ﷺ.

And the image from Ibn Mas'ud that fixes the whole verse: **I am holding you by your waists to keep you out of the Fire, while you throw yourselves in like moths and flies.**

Then the ending — after a surah of broken treaties, exposed plots, hard marches and fifty silent nights:

> *If they turn away, say: Allah is sufficient for me. There is no god but He. In Him I put my trust, and He is the Lord of the Mighty Throne.*

Seven names of nothing at all, and one of everything. The last word revealed to the Prophet ﷺ was not a command, a ruling, or a threat. **It was what to say when everyone leaves.**

## 32. In One Breath

At-Tawbah is the surah of the closing accounts. It dissolves the last treaties with idolatry and gives four months' grace to do it in — while honouring to the day every treaty that was kept, granting safe passage to any enemy who merely wants to hear the Qur'an, and holding the door of repentance open at every single verse where it shuts another. It exposes the hypocrites of Al-Madinah so thoroughly that Qatadah named it The Exposer, cataloguing them not by their creed but by their conduct: the excuses that scaled with distance, the oaths sworn to be left alone, the mockery of both the man who gave much and the man who gave a handful of dates. Against them it sets the believers who brace each other like a building and ache together like one body, the eight named channels through which wealth returns to the poor, and the accounting in which no thirst, no hunger, no valley crossed and no coin spent is ever lost. And at its centre it puts a man who had no excuse and said so — who watched a whole city stop speaking to him for fifty nights, burned a foreign king's offer of honour in his oven, and was told at the end that this was the best day since his mother bore him. That is the surah's argument in a single life: **the ties that are severed here are severed for the sake of the one tie that holds, and the way back to it is to tell the truth about yourself and wait.**
