*Revealed in Makkah · 52 ayat · named for the prophet whose supplication sits at its heart (14:35–41)*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Ibrahim opens and closes on the same note: **a Book sent down so that mankind might be led out of darkness into light.** Everything between those two bookends is the working out of that one sentence — what the light is, what the darkness is, who accepts, who refuses, and what becomes of each.

The surah has a distinctive shape. It moves from Muhammad ﷺ to Musa to the whole line of earlier messengers, then to two parables (a good tree and a rotten one), then to the grave, then to the Day of Resurrection — and only then, near the end, to Ibrahim himself, standing in a barren valley, praying for the city he has just founded. The prayer is placed *after* the argument, not before it, and that placement is the point: the Ka'bah the Quraysh circle was established by a man who begged to be kept away from idols.

---

## 1. The Book, and What It Is For (14:1–3)

Ibn Kathir passes over the disconnected letters — he refers the reader back to his discussion at the opening of Al-Baqarah rather than repeating it — and goes straight to the purpose clause.

This Book is the most honoured Book, sent to the most honoured Messenger, sent **to all the people of the earth, Arabs and non-Arabs alike**. Its function is extraction: to lead people out of misguidance and crookedness into guidance and the straight way.

Two cross-references anchor the phrase *from darkness into light*:

- Allah is the *Wali* of the believers — He brings them out from darkness into light; the disbelievers' *awliya'* are the Taghut, who bring them out of light into darkness (2:257)
- It is He Who sends down clear signs to His servant that He may bring you out of darkness into light (57:9)

Note the grammar Ibn Kathir preserves: **darkness is plural, light is singular.** Misguidance has many forms; guidance has one.

The disbelievers are then described by three things, in order: they **prefer** this life to the Hereafter, they **hinder** others from Allah's path, and they **seek to make it crooked** — though the path itself is straight and does not bend for those who defy it.

---

## 2. Every Prophet Spoke His People's Language (14:4)

*And We sent not a Messenger except with the language of his people.*

Ibn Kathir reads this as an act of divine kindness: Allah sends messengers from among a people and speaking their tongue, so that the message can actually be understood. Guidance and misguidance follow **after** the explanation has been made — *then* Allah misleads whom He wills and guides whom He wills, in His wisdom, misleading those who deserve it and guiding those who deserve it.

But the verse also marks a boundary that Muhammad ﷺ crosses. Every previous prophet was sent to his own nation. Ibn Kathir cites the hadith of Jabir in the two Sahihs — the five things given to the Prophet ﷺ and to no one before him:

| | |
|---|---|
| 1 | Victory through awe, at a month's distance |
| 2 | The earth made a place of worship and a purifier |
| 3 | War booty made lawful |
| 4 | The right of intercession |
| 5 | **Sent to all mankind, not to one nation** |

---

## 3. The Days of Allah (14:5–8)

Musa is given the same commission in the same words: *bring your people out of darkness into light* — and one addition, *remind them of the days of Allah*.

**"The days of Allah"** — Mujahid, Qatadah and others: His favours and bounties. Deliverance from Fir'awn, the passage through the sea, the shading cloud, the manna and quails.

**"Signs for every patient, thankful person."** The lesson of the exodus is available only to a particular kind of person — one who is patient under affliction and thankful in prosperity. Qatadah's line: *excellent is the servant who, if tested, is patient, and if given prosperity, is thankful.* And the hadith in the Sahih: the believer's whole affair is amazing, because every decree is good for him — affliction met with patience is good, bounty met with gratitude is good.

**"In it was a tremendous trial from your Lord" (14:6)** — two readings, and Ibn Kathir declines to choose:

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| First | The *favour* was so great you can never thank Him for it perfectly |
| Second | The *affliction* Fir'awn inflicted was itself the tremendous trial |

*"Both meanings might be considered here, and Allah knows best."*

**Then the axis verse of the surah (14:7):** *If you give thanks, I will give you more; but if you are thankless, verily My punishment is severe.* Gratitude is not decorative — it is the mechanism by which a blessing is retained and increased. Its opposite works the same way: a servant may be deprived of a provision already written for him because of a sin he commits.

And immediately the correction against any thought that Allah gains from this (14:8): *if you disbelieve, you and all on earth together, Allah is Rich, Worthy of all praise.* Ibn Kathir brings the hadith Qudsi from Muslim — if the first and last of mankind and jinn had the heart of the most pious among them, it would not increase His kingdom in the least; if they had the heart of the most wicked, it would not decrease it; if all of them stood and asked and each was given what he asked, it would not decrease His kingdom **except by what a needle carries when dipped in the ocean**.

---

## 4. The Old Argument (14:9–12)

Nuh, 'Ad, Thamud, *and those after them* — **none knows them but Allah.** Ibn Kathir takes this literally and pointedly: Ibn Mas'ud said of this phrase, *"The genealogists utter lies."* And 'Urwah bin Az-Zubayr: *"We did not find anyone who knows the forefathers of Ma'dd bin Adnan."*

**"They put their hands in their mouths"** — a phrase Ibn Kathir does not resolve into one meaning:

| Interpretation | |
|---|---|
| They gestured at the Messengers' mouths, telling them to stop |
| They put their own hands over their mouths in denial |
| They gave no answer at all |
| They bit their hands in rage |
| **Mujahid, Muhammad bin Ka'b, Qatadah:** they belied the Messengers and refuted the call *with their mouths* |

Ibn Kathir states his own preference: Mujahid's reading is supported by what follows in the verse itself — *"we disbelieve in what you were sent with."*

The exchange that follows is the whole history of prophecy in miniature:

- **Messengers:** Can there be doubt about *Allah* — the Creator of the heavens and earth? Ibn Kathir's observation here is sharp: most nations *did* affirm a Creator. Their error was not atheism but intermediaries — beings they imagined would benefit them or bring them closer.
- **Messengers:** He calls you that He may forgive your sins (in the Hereafter) and give you respite for a term appointed (in this life).
- **Nations:** *You are only human beings like us.* Bring us a clear authority — meaning, a miracle of our choosing.
- **Messengers:** Yes, we are human. But Allah bestows His grace on whom He wills. **A miracle is not ours to produce on demand** — only by Allah's permission, after we beg Him for it.

And then the Messengers' resolution, which is the emotional peak of this passage: *Why should we not put our trust in Allah, when He has guided us in our ways? We shall certainly bear with patience all the hurt you cause us.*

---

## 5. "We Shall Drive You Out" — and the Inheritance (14:13–14)

The disbelievers' final argument is not an argument: **expulsion or apostasy.** Ibn Kathir lines up the parallels — Shu'ayb's people (7:88), Lut's people (27:56), the Quraysh plotting to imprison, kill or expel the Prophet ﷺ (8:30, 17:76).

The answer comes as revelation to the Messengers: *We shall destroy the wrongdoers, and We shall make you dwell in the land after them.* Ibn Kathir reads the Hijrah directly into this — the man Makkah expelled returned to it victorious, and within a short time people entered Allah's religion in crowds.

He supports it with the standing decree: *Our Word has gone forth of old for Our servants, the Messengers, that they verily would be made triumphant* (37:171–173); *Allah has decreed: it is I and My Messengers who shall be victorious* (58:21).

But note the closing clause of 14:14 — *This is for him who fears standing before Me and fears My threat.* The inheritance is not tribal. It is conditional on fear of the standing.

---

## 6. The Tyrant's Drink (14:15–17)

*And they sought victory* — whose plea is this? Ibn Kathir records both:

| Authority | Reading |
|---|---|
| Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Qatadah | The **Messengers** sought Allah's help against their nations |
| Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd bin Aslam | The **nations** invoked Allah's judgment against themselves (as at 8:32, "rain down stones on us") |

*"It is possible that both meanings are desired here"* — the idolaters called for judgment at Badr, and the Messenger ﷺ called for victory. Both were answered on the same day.

Then the fate of *every obstinate, arrogant tyrant*. Ibn Kathir's treatment is deliberately unhurried and physical, because the verse is:

- **Hell is in front of him** — not behind, not later; awaiting him, and he is brought to it morning and afternoon until the Day of the Call
- **Made to drink festering water** — Mujahid and Ikrimah: puss and blood
- **He sips it unwillingly** — he refuses, until the angel strikes him with a rod of iron (22:21)
- **Great difficulty swallowing** — the taste, the colour, the unbearable heat or cold
- **Death comes from every side** — 'Amr bin Maymun: *"every bone, nerve and blood vessel."* Ibn Abbas: every type of torment comes to him carrying death with it — **yet he does not die**
- **And in front of him, a great torment** — even in that state, something worse is still ahead

The Prophet ﷺ said that on the Day of Resurrection Jahannam will be brought forth and will call out to the creatures: *"I was given the responsibility of every rebellious tyrant."*

---

## 7. Ashes in the Wind (14:18)

The first of the surah's two great parables, and it is about *good deeds* — not sins.

The disbelievers arrive on the Day of Judgment expecting to collect. They performed charity, kept ties, showed courage. They will find **ashes on a stormy day**: something that was real, that they can see, and that cannot be gathered.

The defect is foundational, not quantitative. Their acts were *built on groundless basis* — worship directed elsewhere, messengers rejected — so there is nothing for the reward to attach to.

Ibn Kathir stacks the parallel images:

- Deeds turned to **scattered floating dust** (25:23)
- Spending like a **freezing wind** that destroys a harvest (3:117)
- Charity destroyed by reminders of generosity and injury: like **a smooth rock with a little dust on it, struck by heavy rain, left bare** (2:264)

*That is the straying, far away.*

---

## 8. If He Wills, He Can Replace You (14:19–20)

The resurrection argument, made from scale: He who created the heavens — with their planets, stars and heavenly objects — and this earth with its valleys, mountains, deserts, seas and the countless species of tree and plant and animal, is not unable to raise a human body.

And a warning folded into the same verse: *If He wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation — and that is not hard for Allah.* Ibn Kathir gathers four parallels (35:15–17; 47:38; 5:54; 4:133). The point of them all: **you are replaceable, and He is not in need.**

---

## 9. The Dispute in the Fire (14:21)

*They will all appear before Allah* — gathered on a flat plain with no cover to hide behind.

Then the followers turn on their leaders: *We were following you — can you avail us anything now?* And the leaders answer honestly, which is the cruellest part: *Had Allah guided us, we would have guided you.* There is no dodge left. *It makes no difference whether we rage or bear it with patience; there is no place of refuge.*

Ibn Kathir gives his own judgment: **this conversation happens after they have entered the Fire**, not before — matching 40:47–48, where the dispute is explicitly *in* the Fire. He adds the parallel scenes: each nation cursing the one before it (7:38–39), the followers cursing their chiefs (33:67–68), and the blame traded back and forth at 34:31–33 — where the weak say *had it not been for you we would have believed*, the arrogant reply *did we keep you from guidance?*, and the weak answer, **it was your plotting by night and day.**

---

## 10. Shaytan's Speech (14:22)

Delivered *after the matter has been decided* — Ibn Kathir says Iblis stands and addresses his followers in the Fire, and his purpose is explicit: *to add depression to their depression, sorrow to their sorrow, grief to their grief.*

The speech has four moves:

1. **Allah promised you truly, through His Messengers — and I promised you and betrayed you.**
2. **I had no authority over you.** No proof, no evidence — *except that I called you, and you responded.*
3. **So blame me not; blame yourselves.** The Messengers established unequivocal proof against you; you disobeyed them and followed me into what I called you to.
4. **I cannot help you, nor can you help me.**

On the closing phrase — *I deny your former act of associating me as a partner* — two readings:

| Authority | Reading |
|---|---|
| Qatadah | "I deny that you associated *me* with Allah before" |
| Ibn Jarir | "I deny **being** a partner with Allah at all" |

Ibn Kathir: *"This opinion is the most plausible"* — supported by 46:5–6 and 19:82, where the objects of worship deny their worshippers and become their enemies.

Ash-Sha'bi's remark frames the whole scene: on that Day, **two speakers will address the people** — 'Isa, questioned about whether he told men to worship him and his mother (5:116–119), and Shaytan, delivering this. One denies falsely attributed divinity; the other denies falsely accepted obedience.

---

## 11. And Then the Other Ending (14:23)

Placed immediately after Shaytan's speech, deliberately: after the miserable ones and their humiliation, *the final destination of the happy ones.*

Gardens beneath which rivers flow — flowing wherever they wish them to flow. Dwelling forever, never transferred out. And the greeting: **Salam.**

Ibn Kathir collects the greeting across the Qur'an: the keepers of Paradise saying *Salamun 'alaykum* (39:73); angels entering from every gate with the same words (13:23–24); *therein they shall be met with greetings and the word of peace* (25:75); and the one that closes the loop — in Paradise their opening is *Subhanaka Allahumma*, their greeting is *Salam*, and the close of their call is **Al-Hamdu Lillahi Rabbil-'Alamin** (10:10).

---

## 12. The Two Trees (14:24–26)

The surah's most famous passage, and Ibn Kathir treats it with unusual care.

**The goodly word / the goodly tree.** Ibn Abbas, via Ali bin Abi Talhah:

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| The goodly word | *La ilaha illallah* |
| The goodly tree | The believer |
| Root firmly fixed | The testimony, firm in the believer's heart |
| Branches reaching the sky | The believer's works, ascending to heaven |
| Fruit at all times | Good deeds ascending by day and by night, in summer and winter |

Ad-Dahhak, Sa'id bin Jubayr, 'Ikrimah, Mujahid and others agreed.

**Which tree?** Al-Bukhari's report from Ibn Umar: the Prophet ﷺ asked the gathering to name a tree resembling the Muslim, whose leaves do not fall in summer or winter and which gives fruit at all times. Ibn Umar thought of the date palm but stayed silent out of deference to Abu Bakr and Umar. When no one answered, the Prophet ﷺ said: **it is the date palm.** Afterwards Umar told him, *"Had you said it, it would have been more precious to me than such and such."*

(Ibn Abbas adds a second reading: the goodly tree is a tree *in Paradise*.)

**The evil word / the evil tree.** Disbelief, which has no basis or stability. Anas bin Malik identified it as the colocynth — a bitter, unscented plant, called *Ash-Shiryan*. **Uprooted from the surface of the earth, having no stability**: cut off at the root, existing without foundation, exactly like Kufr. And therefore the works of the disbelievers never ascend and are never accepted.

Two trees, and the whole difference is underground.

---

## 13. The Firm Word — the Questioning in the Grave (14:27)

*Allah will keep firm those who believe, with the word that stands firm in this world and in the Hereafter.*

Al-Bukhari records from Al-Bara' bin 'Azib that the Prophet ﷺ applied this verse directly to **the questioning in the grave**. Tawus and Qatadah split it: *in this world* = firmness on La ilaha illallah and on righteous action; *in the Hereafter* = the questioning in the grave.

Ibn Kathir then quotes at length the long hadith of Al-Bara' — the fullest death narrative in the tafsir. Its structure is a mirror, point for point:

| | **The believer** | **The disbeliever** |
|---|---|---|
| Angels descend | Faces white and radiant as the sun | Faces dark |
| They bring | White shroud and fragrance of Paradise | *Musuh* (coarse sackcloth) |
| The summons | "O good and pure soul, depart to Allah's forgiveness and pleasure" | "O impure, evil soul, depart to Allah's anger and wrath" |
| The extraction | Flows out like a drop from the lip of a jug | Scatters through the body; torn out like a thorny branch dragged through wet wool |
| The scent | The finest musk ever found on earth | The most putrid smell of a corpse |
| At each heaven | "Whose is this good soul?" — called by the best name he had | "Whose is this evil soul?" — called by the worst name he had |
| The gates | Opened; escorted up to the seventh heaven | **Refused** — *the gates of heaven will not be opened* (7:40) |
| The record | Written in **'Illiyyin** | Written in **Sijjin**; the soul is thrown down (22:31) |
| The three questions | "Allah is my Lord" / "Islam" / "He is the Messenger of Allah" — *I read the Book of Allah and believed* | "Oh, oh, I do not know" — three times |
| The verdict from heaven | "My servant has said the truth — furnish him from Paradise, open a door for him to it" | "My servant has lied — furnish him from the Fire, open a door for him to it" |
| The grave | Expanded as far as his sight reaches; filled with light | Constricted until his ribs cross each other |
| The visitor | A man of handsome face, clothes and scent: *"I am your good works"* | A man of dreadful face, clothes and smell: *"I am your evil work"* |
| His plea | "O Lord, hasten the Hour!" | "O Lord, do not commence the Hour!" |

Ibn Kathir adds two further reports that enrich the scene:

- **Abu Hurayrah (At-Tirmidhi):** two angels, dark and blue, named **Munkar and Nakir**. The believer is told *"Sleep"*; he asks to return to his family to tell them; he is told, *sleep like a bridegroom who is woken only by the dearest of his family.* The hypocrite's answer is: *"I do not know — I heard people say something and I repeated it."*
- **Abu Hurayrah (Ibn Jarir):** the deeds themselves stand guard at the four sides of the body. Punishment approaches from the head — **the prayer** says, *no entrance from my side*. From the right — **Zakah**. From the left — **the fast**. From the feet — **charity, keeping ties of kinship, acts of kindness to people**.

## 14. Those Who Exchanged the Blessing for Disbelief (14:28–30)

Ibn Abbas: this refers to **the people of Makkah**. Ali bin Abi Talib, asked directly about it, said it refers to **the disbelievers of Quraysh on the day of Badr** — the blessing was the faith that came to them, which they exchanged for disbelief and by which they led their own people to destruction.

Ibn Kathir generalises it without softening it: Allah sent Muhammad ﷺ as a mercy and blessing to all mankind. Those who accepted the blessing and were thankful enter Paradise; those who denied it enter the Fire. **The Prophet ﷺ himself is the blessing in question** — which makes rejection of him not neutrality but exchange.

And the closing dismissal to those who set up rivals: *Say: enjoy — but certainly your destination is the Fire.* Do whatever you are able to do; the end is fixed (compare 31:24 and 10:70).

---

## 15. Prayer and Charity, Before the Day of No Bargaining (14:31)

Two commands, addressed to *My servants who have believed*:

- **Establish the prayer** — Ibn Kathir specifies: on time, perfectly, preserving the bowing, with humility, preserving the prostrations
- **Spend from what We have provided, secretly and openly** — the due Zakah, spending on relatives, kindness to all others

Before *a Day on which there is neither mutual bargaining nor befriending.* No ransom is accepted — not even the earth's fill of gold (2:123; 2:254; 57:15).

Qatadah's gloss on *befriending* is the practical one: in this life, friendships bring real benefit, and a man chooses his friends for reasons. **If the friendship was for Allah's sake it will be maintained; if for other than Allah, it is bound to be cut off.**

---

## 16. Count Them If You Can (14:32–34)

A deliberate catalogue: the heavens as a protective ceiling and the earth as a bed; rain, and after it the variety of vegetation and fruit in different colours, shapes, tastes, scents and uses; ships carrying goods and travellers across the sea; rivers running through the land for drinking and irrigation; the sun and the moon in constant pursuit; the night and the day, each taking from the other's length or giving up some of its own.

*And He gave you of all that you asked for* — Ibn Kathir: He has prepared for you all that you need in all conditions.

*And if you count the blessings of Allah, you will never be able to enumerate them.* The servants cannot count them — **let alone thank Him duly for them**.

Two supplications close the passage:

- The Prophet ﷺ, in Al-Bukhari: *O Allah, all praise is due to You, without our ever being able to thank You sufficiently, nor ever wishing to be cut off from You, nor ever feeling rich apart from reliance on You.*
- Dawud: *"O Lord, how can I ever duly thank You, when my thanking You is itself a favour from You to me?"* — and the answer: **"Now you have thanked Me sufficiently, Dawud."** The admission is the gratitude.

*Verily, man is an extreme wrongdoer, ungrateful.*

---

## 17. Ibrahim in the Barren Valley (14:35–41)

The structural argument Ibn Kathir is making: the Quraysh built their idolatry around a House **established on the worship of Allah alone**, by a man who publicly disowned those who worship anything else.

**"Make this city one of peace and security."** Ibn Kathir notes the wording carefully — *this city*, said after it was established, unlike 2:126 where it is said before. The chronology is confirmed by 14:39: Ibrahim thanks Allah for Isma'il **and** Ishaq, and Isma'il was thirteen years older. So this is the later prayer, spoken after the House was built.

**"Keep me and my sons away from worshipping idols."** Ibn Kathir draws the practical lesson: it is proper for whoever supplicates to ask for his parents and offspring as well as himself.

**"Whoever follows me is of me; and whoever disobeys me — You are Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful."** Ibn Kathir sets this beside 'Isa's almost identical words (5:118) and reads both the same way: a referral of the matter to Allah, not a prediction of the outcome.

This produces one of the most affecting reports in the surah. The Prophet ﷺ recited Ibrahim's verse and 'Isa's verse, raised his hands, and said three times: **"O Allah, save my Ummah"** — and wept. Allah sent Jibril to ask what made him weep. The answer returned: *"We will make you pleased concerning your Ummah, and will not treat them in a way you dislike."*

**"I have settled some of my offspring in an uncultivable valley by Your Sacred House."** Two details Ibn Kathir dwells on:

- *So fill some hearts **among men** with love towards them.* Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Sa'id bin Jubayr: had Ibrahim said *the hearts of mankind*, then Persians, Romans, Jews, Christians and everyone else would have crowded around it. He said **among men** — and so the longing is particular, not universal.
- *And provide them with fruits.* The valley grows nothing. And yet — *a secure sanctuary to which are brought fruits of all kinds* (28:57). Ibn Kathir's note: **there are no fruit-bearing trees in Makkah, yet every kind of fruit is carried to it from everywhere.** That is the acceptance of the Khalil's prayer, visible in a marketplace.

The sequence closes with three requests that ascend in scope: *make me one who establishes the prayer* → *and from my offspring* → *forgive me, my parents, and all the believers on the Day the reckoning is established.* Ibn Kathir notes the timing of the middle petition: Ibrahim said this **before** he declared himself free of his father, once it became clear to him that he was an enemy of Allah.

---

## 18. Do Not Think He Is Unaware (14:42–43)

Addressed to the Prophet ﷺ directly. Delay is not inattention: *do not think that because Allah gave them respite and postponed their punishment, He is unaware or overlooking it.* **He keeps full account, on record against them.**

Then the gathering, rendered almost clinically:

- **Hastening forward** — running to the caller (54:8; 20:108–111; 70:43)
- **Necks outstretched** — heads raised up, per Ibn Abbas and Mujahid
- **Their gaze not returning to them** — eyes fixed and unblinking from horror
- **Their hearts empty** — Qatadah: the place of the heart is empty, **because the hearts have risen to the throats**

---

## 19. "Give Us a Little More Time" (14:44–46)

The last request of the wrongdoers, and its refusal.

*Our Lord, respite us for a little while — we will answer Your call and follow the Messengers.* Ibn Kathir places it in a family of identical, futile requests: *My Lord, send me back* (23:99); *would that we were sent back — then we would not deny the signs of our Lord* (6:27); the criminals hanging their heads (32:12).

The reply is not an argument but a reminder of their own words: **had you not sworn before that you would never leave?** Mujahid: leave this world for the Hereafter. Compare 16:38 — *they swear by Allah their strongest oaths that He will not raise up him who dies.*

And a second reply: *you dwelt in the dwellings of those who wronged themselves, and it was clear to you how We dealt with them.* You lived in the ruins. You knew the story. It changed nothing — *perfect wisdom, but the warners benefit them not* (54:5).

**"Their plot was not such as to remove the mountains."** Three positions, laid side by side:

| Authority | Reading |
|---|---|
| Ibn Abbas (via Al-'Awfi), Al-Hasan Al-Basri | Their plotting was **too feeble** to move mountains |
| Mujahid (per Ibn Jurayj) recited it so as to mean | Their plotting **was** such as to move mountains |
| Ibn Abbas (via Ali bin Abi Talhah), Ad-Dahhak, Qatadah | It refers to their **Shirk** — compare *whereby the heavens are almost torn* (19:90) |

Ali bin Abi Talib attached to this verse the strange story of the king who disputed with Ibrahim about his Lord: he raised two eaglets, starved them, tethered them to a wooden box and lured them upward with meat on a staff, sitting inside with a companion who described what he saw — until he said the earth looked like a fly. Mujahid told a version of the same story about Nebuchadnezzar, in which a voice called out *"O tyrant, where are you headed?"*, and the eagles descended so violently that the mountains nearly shifted from the noise.

Ibn Jarir's summary is the sober one: **their shirk troubled neither the mountains nor any other creature. The harm of what they did came back to haunt only themselves.** Ibn Kathir agrees, pairing it with 17:37 — *you can neither rend the earth nor reach the mountains in height.*

---

## 20. A Different Earth (14:47–51)

*So think not that Allah will fail to keep His promise to His Messengers.* Victory in this life and on the Day the Witnesses come forth. Nothing He wills escapes Him.

**"The Day when the earth will be changed to another earth, and so will the heavens."** In the two Sahihs, from Sahl bin Sa'd: the people will be gathered on **a white, flat earth, like a loaf of wheat bread, with no landmark for anyone.**

'A'ishah said she was the first person ever to ask the Prophet ﷺ about this verse — *where will the people be then?* His answer: **"On the Sirat."**

And a second answer, to a Jewish rabbi who came asking the same question (recorded by Muslim through Thawban): **"In the darkness before the Bridge."** The exchange that follows is remarkable — who crosses first (**the poor emigrants**), what they eat on entering Paradise (the caul of fish liver, then a bull of Paradise that grazed its pathways), what they drink (a fountain named **Salsabil**) — and then the rabbi's real question, about how a child's sex is determined, after which he said, *"You have told the truth, and you are indeed a Prophet,"* and left. The Prophet ﷺ commented: **he asked me about things I had no knowledge of until Allah gave it to me.**

Then the criminals, on that Day:

- **Bound together in fetters** — Ibn Kathir: each chained to his like. *Assemble those who did wrong, together with their companions* (37:22); *when the souls are joined with their bodies* (81:7)
- **Garments of Qatiran** — the tar used to coat camels. Qatadah: one of the fastest substances to catch fire. Ibn Abbas: it means **molten lead** (and the verse admits a reading supporting this — Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Qatadah)
- **Fire covering their faces** — *the Fire will burn their faces, and therein they will grin with displaced lips* (23:104)

Ibn Kathir attaches here the hadith of Abu Malik Al-Ash'ari on the four practices of Jahiliyyah that will remain in the Ummah — boasting of lineage, discrediting family ties, seeking rain through the stars, and **wailing for the dead** — with the warning that the professional wailer who dies unrepentant will be raised wearing a garment of Qatiran.

*That Allah may requite each person according to what he has earned. Truly, Allah is swift at reckoning.* Mujahid glosses *swift at reckoning* simply as **"keeping count."** And His power over all creation is as His power over one: *the creation of you all and the resurrection of you all are only as a single person* (31:28).

---

## 21. The Closing Verse (14:52)

*This is a Message for mankind — that they may be warned by it, and know that He is One God, and that men of understanding may take heed.*

Ibn Kathir ties the last verse back to the first: the Book was sent *to lead mankind out of darkness into light* (14:1), and it closes by stating the same purpose in different words. This Qur'an is for all mankind and jinn — *that I may warn you thereby, and whomsoever it reaches* (6:19).

*And that men of understanding may take heed* — meaning, Ibn Kathir says plainly, **those who have good minds.**

---

## The Thread

Surah Ibrahim is built on a single verb: *to bring out.* Out of darkness into light, out of Egypt, out of the grave, out of the earth on the Day it is changed. And it insists, again and again, that being brought out costs nothing and is owed nothing — Allah is Rich, Worthy of all praise; if all creation had the heart of the most pious man among them it would add nothing to His kingdom.

What the servant contributes is one thing only: **gratitude** — *if you give thanks, I will give you more.* And the surah takes the trouble to show what gratitude actually looks like, from four directions. It looks like a date palm giving fruit in every season. It looks like an old man in a valley with no crops, asking that his children not become idolaters. It looks like Dawud admitting he cannot thank properly, and being told that is the thanks. And in the grave, it looks like the prayer standing at the head, the fast at the left, and kindness to people at the feet, each saying: *no entrance from my side.*

Against that stands the other tree — the bitter colocynth, cut off at the root, with nothing beneath it — and the deeds that looked solid until the wind came.

*This is the end of the Tafsir of Surah Ibrahim, and all praise is due to Allah.*
