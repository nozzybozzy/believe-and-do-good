*Revealed in Makkah · 8 ayat · "The Mutual Increase" — the rivalry to pile up more than the next man*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

At-Takathur is eight verses long and it does something very economical: it describes an entire human life in one clause, and then spends the rest of the surah on what happens afterwards.

The life is this — **you were distracted, and then you died.** *The mutual increase diverts you, until you visit the graves.* There is no account of what the person achieved in between, because on this surah's reckoning nothing in between was the point. The word *takathur* is competitive: not merely wanting, but wanting **more than someone else has**. And the verb *alhakum* is not "you chose" — it is *diverted*, *distracted*, *kept busy*. The surah's picture of a wasted life is not a life of great crimes. It is a life of being fully occupied by the wrong thing until the occupation ran out.

Then the surah turns on the reader with a threat repeated twice, corrects the reader's idea of what they know, and shows them the Fire — first as knowledge, then as sight. And it closes with the question that has troubled readers of this surah for fourteen centuries: **you will be asked about the delights.** Not the sins. The blessings.

The movement is worth naming, because it is the surah's spine:

| Verse | Stage |
|---|---|
| 102:1–2 | Distraction, all the way to the grave |
| 102:3–4 | *Kalla* — a threat, then the same threat again |
| 102:5 | ***'Ilm al-yaqin*** — if you knew with the certainty of **knowledge** |
| 102:6–7 | ***'Ayn al-yaqin*** — you will see it with the certainty of **sight** |
| 102:8 | The questioning — about what you were given, not what you lost |

## 1. The Diversion, and Where It Ends (102:1–2)

> *The mutual increase diverts you, until you visit the graves.*

Ibn Kathir's reading is direct: people are preoccupied by love of the world, its delights and its adornments, and this **distracts them from seeking the Hereafter and desiring it.** The distraction runs on until death arrives — *and you visit the graves, thus becoming its inhabitants.*

The phrase repays attention. To *visit* a grave is the language of a temporary stop, and the surah applies it to the dead themselves. Even the grave is not the destination.

**The report of Ubayy bin Ka'b** (Al-Bukhari, in the Book of Ar-Riqaq — the narrations that soften the heart), from Anas bin Malik:

> "We used to think that this was a part of the Qur'an until the Ayah was revealed which says: *The mutual increase diverts you.*"

He was referring to the famous hadith — **"If the Son of Adam had a valley of gold, he would desire another like it..."** The Companions held that saying so closely that they took it for revelation, until this surah came down and said the same thing in Allah's own words.

---

## 2. "My Wealth, My Wealth" — What You Actually Own

Ibn Kathir stacks four hadiths here, and together they form the most practical passage in the surah. Each one asks the same question: *of everything you call yours, how much is really yours?*

**Imam Ahmad, from 'Abdullah bin Ash-Shikhkhir** — he came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ while he was reciting *The mutual increase diverts you*, and he said:

> *The Son of Adam says, "My wealth, my wealth." But do you get anything of benefit from your wealth except for that which you ate and you finished it, or that which you clothed yourself with and you wore it out, or that which you gave as charity and you have spent it?*

Also recorded by Muslim, At-Tirmidhi and An-Nasa'i.

**Muslim, from Abu Hurayrah** — the same idea, sharpened to a list of three and given a closing line that lands hard. The servant says *my wealth, my wealth*, yet he gets only three things from it:

1. what he **eats** and finishes,
2. what he **wears** until it is worn out,
3. what he **gives in charity** and it is spent.

> *Everything else other than that will go away and leave him for the people.*

The first two are consumed and gone. Only the third survives the transaction. Everything else is not lost — it is simply **inherited**, transferred to somebody else, and was never his.

**Al-Bukhari, from Anas bin Malik** — the same accounting, applied to the funeral procession:

> *Three things follow the deceased person, and two of them return while one remains behind with him. The things which follow him are his family, his wealth and his deeds. His family and his wealth return while his deeds remain.*

Recorded also by Muslim, At-Tirmidhi and An-Nasa'i. It is worth picturing literally: two of the three mourners turn around at the graveside and walk back.

**Imam Ahmad, from Anas**, in the Two Sahihs as well:

> *The Son of Adam becomes old with senility, but yet two things remain with him: greed and hope.*

*Al-hirs wal-amal.* The body fails; the appetite that drove the whole diversion does not. This is why the surah says *until you visit the graves* — nothing short of that stops it.

## 3. A Threat, and Then the Same Threat Again (102:3–4)

> *Nay! You shall come to know! Again nay! You shall come to know!*

**Al-Hasan Al-Basri:** "This is a threat after a threat."

**Ad-Dahhak** reads the repetition as two audiences rather than one intensified warning: the first addressed to the disbelievers, the second to the believers. On that reading, nobody listening is outside the verse.

---

## 4. If You Knew (102:5)

> *Nay! If you knew with a sure knowledge.*

Ibn Kathir supplies the unstated conclusion of the conditional: *if you knew with true knowledge, you would not be diverted by rivalry for wealth away from seeking the abode of the Hereafter until you reach the graves.*

This is the surah's diagnosis. The problem is not that people **disbelieve** in the Hereafter — many affirm it. The problem is that their knowledge of it is not the kind of knowledge that changes behaviour. Real certainty would have shown up in how they spent their time.

---

## 5. Seeing It (102:6–7)

> *Verily, you shall see the blazing Fire! And again, you shall see it with certainty of sight!*

Ibn Kathir names these two verses as **the explanation of the threat** issued in 102:3–4. *You shall come to know* — this is what you will come to know.

And then the image the surah is remembered for:

> It is a Fire which, **if it exhaled a single breath**, every angel near to Allah and every Prophet who was sent would fall down upon their knees — from fear, from awe, and from the sight of its horrors.

Not the sinners. The angels and the prophets — the ones with nothing to fear — brought to their knees by one exhalation.

## 6. "You Shall Be Asked About the Delights" (102:8)

> *Then on that Day you shall be asked about the delights!*

Ibn Kathir's gloss: on that Day you will be questioned **concerning your gratitude** for the favours Allah blessed you with — health, safety, sustenance and the rest. *Did you return His favours by being thankful to Him and worshipping Him?*

**The meal of dates and meat** (Ibn Jarir, and Muslim, from Abu Hurayrah). This is the story that fixes the verse's meaning, and it is startling precisely because nothing extravagant happens in it.

Abu Bakr and 'Umar were sitting when the Prophet ﷺ came upon them and asked what had brought them there. They swore that nothing had driven them from their houses **but hunger**. He ﷺ replied: *By He Who has sent me with the truth, nothing has brought me out other than this.*

The three went to the house of a man of the Ansar. His wife received them; the husband was out fetching water. He came back with his bucket and said: *Welcome — nothing has visited the servants of Allah better than a Prophet who has visited me today.* He hung up the bucket, climbed a palm and came down with a cluster of dates. The Prophet ﷺ asked why he had not picked from it; the man said he wanted them to choose with their own eyes. Then he took a blade for a sheep, and the Prophet ﷺ said: **"Do not slaughter one that gives milk."** So he slaughtered another, and they all ate.

Then the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *You will be asked about this on the Day of Judgement. Hunger caused you to come out of your homes and you did not return until you had eaten this meal. So this is from the delights.*

Three men so hungry they had left their houses; a meal of dates and mutton in a stranger's yard. **That** is a *na'im* one is answerable for. The verse is not aimed at luxury. It is aimed at everything.

**Two favours most people are unjust towards** (Al-Bukhari, and the Sunans of At-Tirmidhi, An-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah, from Ibn 'Abbas):

> *Two favours are treated unjustly by most people: **health** and **free time**.*

Ibn Kathir's explanation is exact: people fail to give these two their due, and **whoever does not maintain a right that is obligatory upon him is unjust.** The word for it is not *ungrateful* but *wronging* — you take something owed to someone else.

**The final hadith** (Imam Ahmad, from Abu Hurayrah — Ahmad alone records it in this form). Allah the Mighty and Majestic says on the Day of Judgement:

> *"O Son of Adam! I made you ride upon the horses and camels, I gave you women to marry, and I made you reside and rule in the earth. So where is the thanks for that?"*

The surah opened with a man counting what he had. It closes with the same inventory read back to him — by its Owner.

## If You Remember Nothing Else

- **The surah describes a whole life in one line:** you were kept busy competing, and then you died. What made it a waste was not wickedness — it was *distraction*.
- ***Takathur* is comparative.** The disease is not wanting things; it is wanting **more than him**. That contest has no finish line short of the grave.
- **You own three things:** what you ate, what you wore out, and what you gave away. Only the third one leaves the grave with you. The rest is a bequest to other people, whatever your name is on it today.
- **Your family and your wealth walk you to the graveside and then turn around.** Your deeds do not.
- **Certainty comes in two grades** — knowledge (102:5) and sight (102:7). Everyone eventually gets the second. The surah's whole appeal is to act on the first.
- **The senile body still wants more.** Greed and hope outlive strength, so the appetite will never exhaust itself on its own — it has to be **out-argued**.
- **You will be questioned about the good things, not just the bad.** A meal of dates eaten in hunger counted. So do health and spare time — the two blessings, the Prophet ﷺ said, that most people **wrong**.
