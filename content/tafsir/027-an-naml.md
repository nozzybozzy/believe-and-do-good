*Revealed in Makkah · 93 ayat · named for the ant that warned her colony, 27:18*

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

An-Naml is the middle surah of the "Ta Sin" group, and it works by **contrast**. It sets four prophets side by side — Musa, Dawud and Suleiman, Salih, Lut — and lets the pattern speak: power given by Allah, met either with gratitude or with arrogance.

Its centrepiece is unusual. Most Makkan surahs of this kind close each story with destruction. An-Naml gives the longest single narrative to a story that ends in **conversion, not annihilation** — the Queen of Saba' walks in a sun-worshipper and walks out a Muslim. That story sits between Fir`awn (destroyed), Thamud (destroyed) and Lut's people (destroyed), which is precisely the point: the door was open the whole time.

The second half turns from stories to **evidence**. Five times Allah lists what only He does — creation, rain, mountains, the barrier between the seas, answering the desperate — and after each one asks the same question: *Is there any god with Allah?* The surah then moves to the Hour, the Beast, the Trumpet, and ends with the Prophet ﷺ commanded to worship, recite, and leave the outcome alone.

---

## 1. The Opening: A Book That Splits Its Readers (27:1–6)

The same Qur'an is *guidance and glad tidings* for those who believe — Ibn Kathir stresses this means those who believe in it, **follow it, and put it into practice** — while for those who deny the Hereafter, Allah says: *We have made their deeds fair seeming to them, so that they wander about blindly.*

That is not arbitrary. It is the recompense for denying the Hereafter in the first place, parallel to 6:110 — *We shall turn their hearts and their eyes away, as they refused to believe therein the first time.* Denial comes first; the blindness is what settles in afterwards.

The believers are defined by three things, in this order: **Salah, Zakah, and certainty about the Hereafter** — resurrection, reckoning, reward, Paradise and Hell, believed with conviction rather than assumed.

---

## 2. Musa at the Fire (27:7–14)

Musa is lost at night with his family and sees a fire on the mountainside. He goes to fetch directions and an ember to keep them warm. Ibn Kathir preserves the beautiful summary of what actually happened: *he came back with great news, and a great light.*

What he found was a fire burning in a green bush — the flames growing brighter while the bush grew greener — its light reaching up to the clouds. **Ibn Abbas said it was not fire at all, but shining light**; in one report, the Light of the Lord of the worlds.

Two things in this passage repay attention:

- **"The Messengers fear not in front of Me."** Musa fled from the staff-turned-serpent, and was reassured — the fear was natural, and did not disqualify him.
- **The exception clause** that follows (*except him who has done wrong and afterwards changed evil for good; then surely I am Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful*) Ibn Kathir reads as **good news for all mankind**, not just prophets: whoever commits evil, abandons it, and turns back, has his repentance accepted.

Fir`awn's people rejected the signs *though they themselves were convinced thereof* — they knew, and denied anyway, out of wrongdoing and pride. Ibn Kathir names the moral bluntly: this is a warning to Quraysh, and Muhammad ﷺ has the stronger proof of the two.

---

## 3. Dawud, Suleiman, and the Ant (27:15–19)

Suleiman *inherited* from Dawud — and Ibn Kathir is careful here: **not wealth, but kingship and prophethood.** Had wealth been meant, Suleiman would not have been singled out among Dawud's many sons; and the Prophet ﷺ said, *We Prophets cannot be inherited from; whatever we leave behind is charity.*

Suleiman's armies of jinn, men and birds march in strict formation, the birds shading him with their wings. Mujahid notes officers were appointed to keep rank — *just as kings do nowadays*.

Then the whole apparatus stops for an ant. She warns her colony to get indoors, *lest Suleiman and his armies crush you while they perceive not* — she assumes his innocence even while fleeing him. Suleiman smiles, and makes the one prayer the surah preserves as a model:

> *My Lord! Grant me the power that I may be grateful for Your favours which You have bestowed on me and on my parents, and that I may do righteous deeds that will please You, and admit me by Your mercy among Your righteous servants.*

Note what the greatest king in history asks for at the peak of his power: **not more, but the ability to be thankful for what he already has** — and to be gathered, at death, with the righteous.

---

## 4. The Hoopoe: A Bird with an Argument (27:20–26)

The hoopoe served as Suleiman's water-finder, able to see water beneath the ground. He goes missing at inspection, and Suleiman threatens severe punishment — the Salaf explain this as plucking his feathers and exposing him — *unless he brings me a clear reason*.

Ibn Kathir preserves a small classroom scene here. The Khariji Nafi` bin Al-Azraq challenged Ibn Abbas: if the hoopoe can see water underground, why can't he see a boy's trap? Ibn Abbas replied that **when the decree strikes a person, his eyes go blind and he loses all caution**. Nafi` never argued with him about the Qur'an again.

The hoopoe returns with news, and delivers a small sermon: a woman rules Saba'; she has a great throne; and *I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah.* Then he adds — of his own accord — a full statement of tawhid: the One who brings out what is hidden in the heavens and earth, who knows what you conceal and reveal, *Allah, there is no god but He, Lord of the Supreme Throne.*

Ibn Kathir draws the legal conclusion: **because the hoopoe was calling to worship of Allah alone, killing him would have been forbidden.** The Prophet ﷺ forbade killing four creatures — ants, bees, hoopoes and sparrow hawks.

---

## 5. The Letter, and the Queen Who Consulted (27:27–37)

Suleiman writes. The hoopoe drops the letter through her window and **steps aside out of good manners** — which is exactly why she calls it *a noble letter*: no king's messenger behaves like that.

The letter is three lines: the Basmalah, *be not exalted against me*, and *come to me submitting*. Ibn Kathir calls it **the utmost in brevity and eloquence, straight to the point.**

Her response is the most admired piece of statecraft in the Qur'an:

- She **consults**: *I decide no case till you are present with me.* Her chiefs offer war and then defer to her.
- She **reads history**: *kings, when they enter a town, they destroy it and make the most honourable of its people the lowest* — and Allah endorses her reading: *And thus they do.*
- She **tests with a gift**. Ibn Abbas preserves her reasoning to her people: *if he accepts the gift, he is a king — fight him. If he refuses it, he is a Prophet — follow him.*

Qatadah's remark is worth keeping: *May Allah have mercy on her — how wise she was as a Muslim, and before that as an idolater.*

Suleiman does not even look at the gold. *Will you help me with wealth? What Allah has given me is better than what He has given you.* Ibn Kathir renders his meaning: **we will accept nothing from you except Islam or the sword.**

---

## 6. The Throne, and the Real Test (27:38–44)

Two offers to fetch her throne before she arrives:

- An **`Ifrit of the jinn** — Mujahid says a giant, Abu Salih says he was like a mountain — offers to bring it before Suleiman rises from his seat, i.e. within half a day. *I am strong and trustworthy for such work.*
- **Asif bin Barkhiya**, Suleiman's scribe — a believing man who knew the Greatest Name of Allah — offers it *within the twinkling of an eye*. He rose, made ablution, and prayed.

Knowledge of Allah's Name outruns the strength of an `Ifrit. That is the point of the pairing.

And when the throne appears, Suleiman does not celebrate. He says: **this is by the grace of my Lord — to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful.** Ibn Kathir underlines the second half — *whoever is ungrateful, certainly my Lord is Rich, Bountiful* — with the hadith Qudsi from Sahih Muslim: if the first and last of mankind and jinn were as pious as the most pious, it would add nothing to Allah's dominion; and if they were as evil as the most evil, it would take nothing from it. **Our worship enriches us, not Him.**

Bilqis is then tested twice:

1. **The disguised throne.** Asked *is your throne like this?*, she gives the perfect answer: *as though it were the very same.* She neither claims it (it was locked behind seven doors and hundreds of miles away) nor denies it (it had been altered). Ibn Kathir: **this is the ultimate in intelligence and strong resolve.**
2. **The glass floor.** Entering the Sarh — a palace floored with smooth glass over flowing water — she mistakes it for a pool and lifts her hem. It is at this moment, seeing what Allah had given him, that she submits: *My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Suleiman, to Allah, the Lord of all that exists.*

She was tested on her judgement and on her assumptions, and passed by admitting error rather than defending herself.

---

## 7. Salih, and Nine Men in a Cave (27:45–53)

Salih's people split into two parties the moment he says *worship Allah*. They demand the punishment be hastened, and he answers with the alternative they never consider: *why do you not seek Allah's forgiveness, that you may receive mercy?*

They call him a bad omen. The Qur'an's standing reply, quoted from three places: **your omen is with yourselves** — all things are from Allah, by His will and decree.

Then the nine ringleaders — the ones behind the killing of the she-camel — swear a mutual oath to assassinate Salih and his household by night, then swear innocence to his relatives: *we witnessed not the destruction of his household, and we are telling the truth.* A lie planned in advance, with an alibi built in.

*So they plotted a plot, and We planned a plan, while they perceived not.* On their way to ambush him at his place of prayer, a rock fell from the mountain above; they ran into the cave for shelter and it sealed the mouth of the cave with them inside. **Their own people never knew what became of them.** Allah punished some of them there and some of them elsewhere, and saved Salih and those who believed.

---

## 8. Lut (27:54–58)

*Do you commit immoral sins while you see?* — Ibn Kathir reads *while you see* as: openly, in front of one another, in your gatherings. The sin was compounded by being public and unembarrassed.

Their only answer was to expel him: *these are men who want to be clean and pure* — said as an accusation. Ibn Kathir's gloss: they were embarrassed by the presence of people who did not approve of them, so they moved to remove them.

His wife was left behind with the destroyed, **not because she did the deeds, but because she approved of them and informed on his guests.** Proximity to a prophet saved nothing; the honour was his, not hers.

---

## 9. "Is There Any God With Allah?" (27:59–64)

The pivot of the surah. Five arguments, each ending with the same question — and Ibn Kathir notes it carries two meanings at once: *is there any god who could do this?* and *after this, who else could be worth worshipping?*

| The evidence | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| Creation of the heavens and earth; rain that grows gardens *full of beauty and delight* | *It is not in your ability to cause the growth of their trees* |
| The earth made a fixed abode; rivers; firm mountains; the barrier between fresh and salt water | Each sea kept as it is meant to be — sweet for drinking and irrigation, salt so the air is not corrupted |
| **He answers the distressed one who calls on Him, and removes the evil** | The one everyone turns to when calamity strikes, including those who deny Him |
| Guidance in the darkness of land and sea; winds sent ahead of His mercy | Stars, landmarks, rain-clouds — mercy arriving with an announcement |
| He originates creation and repeats it; provides from heaven and earth | Then: *Bring forth your proofs, if you are truthful* — and they have none |

Two things Ibn Kathir draws out here:

**The idolaters already conceded the premise.** Asked who created the heavens or sends down the rain, they answered *Allah* (29:63) — then worshipped things they admitted could neither create nor provide.

**On inheriting the earth**, generation after generation: Allah could have created everyone at once, or let no one die until all died together — but then the earth would have been unlivable, crowded and cramped. The staggering of birth and death is itself a mercy, planned as such.

And attached to the verse about the distressed one, a hadith answering *what are you calling for?*: **I am calling people to Allah alone — the One who, if you call on Him when harm befalls you, relieves you; when you are lost in the wilderness, brings you back; when drought strikes, makes your crops grow.** Then, asked for advice: *do not slander anyone, and do not think of any good deed as insignificant — even meeting your brother with a cheerful face, or emptying your vessel into the vessel of one who is asking for water.*

---

## 10. What Only Allah Knows (27:65–75)

*None in the heavens and the earth knows the Unseen except Allah* — an **absolute exception**, admitting no partner in that knowledge. Ibn Kathir pairs it with the hadith in Sahih Muslim: when Jibril asked the Prophet ﷺ when the Hour would come, he answered, **the one being asked knows no more than the one asking.**

To those who mock — *when will this promise come?* — the answer is: *perhaps that which you wish to hasten is close behind you.* Ibn Abbas: some of it has already drawn near.

And to the Prophet ﷺ, twice, a instruction of release: *grieve not over them, nor be straitened by what they plot.* Don't destroy yourself with regret over people who have chosen this. **Their plotting is Allah's problem, not yours.**

---

## 11. The Beast, and the Trumpet (27:82–90)

**The Beast of the Earth** emerges when the Word falls due — at a time when mankind has become corrupt, abandoned Allah's commands and altered the religion. It will *speak to them*.

Ibn Kathir gathers the hadiths: the Hour is preceded by ten signs, including the sun rising from the west, the smoke, the Dajjal, Ya'juj and Ma'juj, the descent of `Isa, three land cave-ins, and a fire from Yemen driving people before it. **The sun's rising from the west and the Beast's emergence come first, one close on the heels of the other.** The recurring instruction attached to all of it: *hasten to do good deeds before these six things appear.*

The Beast carries **the staff of Musa and the ring of Suleiman**, marking believer and disbeliever on the face so plainly that people will address each other by it in the marketplace and at the dinner table. Ibn Az-Zubayr's description of its form is preserved in vivid, composite detail.

Then the **Trumpet**. Israfil blows, and everyone in the heavens and earth is terrified — *except him whom Allah wills*, which Ibn Kathir identifies as **the martyrs, who are alive with their Lord**. The long hadith of Abdullah bin `Amr traces the sequence: the Dajjal, then `Isa, then seven years without enmity between any two people, then a cool wind from Syria that takes every soul with a speck of faith in it, leaving the worst of mankind to be the ones the Hour falls upon.

And the image the surah is remembered for:

> *You will see the mountains and think them solid, but they shall pass away as the passing away of the clouds. The work of Allah, Who perfected all things.*

The most permanent-looking thing you know will drift like weather. Then the accounting: whoever brings a good deed gets **better than its worth** — Qatadah adds, meaning what was done sincerely for Allah alone — and safety from the terror of that Day.

---

## 12. The Closing Command (27:91–93)

The surah ends by narrowing to four instructions for the Prophet ﷺ, and through him for everyone:

1. **Worship the Lord of this City** — Makkah, made sacred by Allah on the day He created the heavens and the earth, sacred until the Day of Resurrection. Ibn Kathir notes the naming is an honour to the city, then immediately widened: *and to Whom belongs everything.*
2. **Be among the Muslims** — those sincere to Allah alone and obedient to His commands.
3. **Recite the Qur'an** — meaning, convey it. *Whoever is guided is guided for himself; whoever goes astray — say: I am only one of the warners.* The outcome is not the messenger's to secure.
4. **Say: all praise be to Allah** — praise, Ibn Kathir says, for the fact that **He punishes no one until the case has been made against them and the warning has reached them.**

*He will show you His Ayat, and you shall recognise them. And your Lord is not unaware of what you do.*

The tafsir closes with two lines Imam Ahmad used to recite:

> *If you are alone one day, do not say "I am alone" — say "Someone is watching me." And do not imagine Allah's attention wanders for an instant, or that anything is hidden from Him.*

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**Suleiman's smile at the ant is the theological centre of the surah.** He commanded jinn, men and birds; he had the throne of a foreign queen delivered in the blink of an eye; and the two moments the Qur'an chooses to record are the two moments he turned his power back into gratitude — *grant me that I may be grateful*, and *this is to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful*.

Set against him: Fir`awn, who knew the signs were true and denied them out of pride; the nine of Thamud, who plotted at night and were buried in the cave they ran to; Lut's people, who exiled the only ones among them who wanted to be clean.

And between the two, **Bilqis** — who was given everything, ruled well, consulted her people, tested the man who summoned her, and when the evidence came in, said simply: *I have wronged myself.* The surah spends more verses on her than on any of the prophets it destroys nations for. She is the answer to *is there any god with Allah?* — a person who heard the question honestly and changed.
