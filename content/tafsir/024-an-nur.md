*Revealed in Madinah · 64 ayat · named for the Ayah of Light at its centre, 24:35*

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

An-Nur is the surah that builds a household. Almost everything in it concerns the space where people are unguarded — the doorway, the bedroom, the courtyard, the rumour that travels between neighbours — and it legislates that space with unusual directness. Punishments, oaths, permission, the gaze, the garment, marriage, meal-times, who may enter and when.

But it does not present itself as a legal code. At the exact centre of all this domestic legislation, Allah places a parable of light in a niche, and then immediately says where that light belongs: **in houses which Allah has ordered to be raised.** The structure is the argument. Purity of the home and purity of the heart are the same project, and the surah moves between them without seam.

It is also the only surah revealed to defend the honour of one woman. Ten consecutive ayat answer a month of gossip in Madinah — and in answering it, Allah legislates for every Muslim who would ever repeat something they had not verified.

---

## 1. A Surah That Announces Itself (24:1)

> *A Surah which We have sent down and which We have enjoined, and in it We have revealed manifest Ayat.*

Ibn Kathir notes that Allah is pointing to the high esteem in which He holds this surah — which does not diminish the others. **Mujahid and Qatadah**: *We have enjoined* means He has spelled out the lawful and unlawful, commands, prohibitions, and prescribed punishments. **Al-Bukhari** notes the reading *Faradnaha* — enjoined upon you, and upon those who come after you.

A surah that opens by insisting on its own binding force is telling you what kind of surah it is.

---

## 2. The Prescribed Punishment for Zina (24:2–3)

One hundred stripes. Ibn Kathir immediately draws the distinction the ayah assumes:

- **Unmarried** (never had lawful intercourse): one hundred stripes, plus banishment for one year — established in the hadith of the two Bedouins in the Two Sahihs, where the Prophet ﷺ overturned a private settlement of a hundred sheep and a slave-girl and judged by the Book of Allah, sending Unays to the woman.
- **Married**, free, adult, of sound mind: stoning. Ibn Kathir cites **Umar's** famous address — that the ayah of stoning was revealed and recited, that the Prophet ﷺ carried it out and the Companions after him, and that Umar feared a time would come when people would deny it because they could not find it in the written text.

**"Let not pity withhold you."** This is not a command to be heartless. Ibn Kathir is careful: natural pity is not forbidden — a Companion said he felt pity even when slaughtering a sheep, and was told he would be rewarded for it. What is forbidden is the pity that makes a judge abandon the punishment altogether. And the threshold matters: *"Settle it among yourselves, for once a matter requiring a prescribed punishment reaches me, I am obliged to carry it out."*

**"Let a party of the believers witness."** Publicly — because the deterrent and the scandal are part of the point.

**24:3** then forbids marrying into that life. A man once sought the Prophet's ﷺ permission to marry a woman known for prostitution who offered to support him financially; the Prophet ﷺ answered him only by reciting this ayah.

## 3. Slander, and the Escape Route: Qadhf and Li'an (24:4–10)

Accuse a chaste woman and fail to produce four witnesses, and **three rulings** fall on you: eighty stripes; your testimony rejected forever; and the label *fasiq* — of bad character in the sight of Allah and of people.

Repentance lifts the second and third, not the first. The flogging happens either way. **Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib** — leader of the Tabi'in — held that a repentant slanderer's testimony is again acceptable; **Ash-Sha'bi and Ad-Dahhak** held it is not, unless he admits he lied.

**Sa'd bin Ubadah**, leader of the Ansar, said aloud what everyone was thinking: *if I found a man with my wife, must I leave him undisturbed while I go and find four witnesses?* His people defended him — he was simply an intensely jealous man.

Days later **Hilal bin Umayyah** came at dawn having seen exactly that. The Prophet ﷺ demanded evidence or the punishment on Hilal's back; Hilal insisted Allah would provide a way out. Revelation came: the **Li'an**. Four oaths from the husband, a fifth invoking Allah's curse if he lies; four from the wife, a fifth invoking Allah's wrath if he is truthful.

Ibn Kathir notes why *wrath* falls to her: a man rarely exposes his own wife without cause, so the woman swearing falsely is the one who knows the truth and deviates from it anyway. She hesitated at the fifth oath, nearly confessed, then said, *"I will not disgrace my people,"* and swore.

They were separated permanently. The child was attributed to no father, and no one was permitted to slander either of them. When the child was born matching the description the Prophet ﷺ had given, he said: *"Were it not for the Book of Allah, I would have dealt with her."* **Ikrimah** adds that the boy grew up to become governor of Egypt, still carrying his mother's name.

And then, over all of it: **"Had it not been for the grace of Allah and His mercy on you!"** — meaning, Ibn Kathir says, that many of your affairs would have been unbearable without this way out.

## 4. Al-Ifk: The Month That Nearly Broke Madinah (24:11–26)

A'ishah's own account, preserved by Az-Zuhri from four separate narrators who each remembered different parts. She was left behind after searching for a broken onyx necklace; her howdah was lifted onto the camel while empty because she was so light. **Safwan bin Al-Mu'attal** found her at dawn, said only *"Truly, to Allah we belong,"* and led his camel until they rejoined the army at midday, in full view.

Ibn Kathir stresses that the openness itself was the refutation: nobody guilty arrives at noon in front of an entire army.

Then a month. A'ishah ill and unaware; the Prophet ﷺ visibly withdrawn, saying only *"How is that lady?"*; Abdullah bin Ubayy fabricating and circulating; the Aws and Khazraj nearly coming to blows in the mosque; Barirah questioned and finding nothing against her but a young girl falling asleep over the dough. Then the moment when she stopped crying entirely and told the Prophet ﷺ she could only quote Ya'qub — *"patience is most fitting"* — and turned her face to the wall. She had hoped for a dream. She thought herself too insignificant for revelation.

Ibn Kathir then draws out the ayat as *discipline*, one by one:

- **"Consider it not a bad thing for you. Nay, it is good for you."** Addressed to the family of Abu Bakr — honour in this world, raised status in the next. Ibn Abbas said to her as she lay dying: your innocence was revealed from heaven.
- **"Why did the believers not think good of their own people?"** Ibn Kathir's model here is **Abu Ayyub Al-Ansari**, whose wife asked whether he had heard what people were saying. *Yes, and it is a lie. Would you do such a thing?* — *No, by Allah.* — *Then A'ishah is better than you.* Measure the accusation against yourself first.
- **"Why did you not say: it is not right for us to speak of this?"** A second discipline: even if Shaytan puts an ugly thought in your mind, do not let it reach your tongue. *Allah has pardoned my Ummah for what occurs to their minds, so long as they do not speak of it or act on it.*
- **"You counted it a little thing, while with Allah it was very great."** The line Ibn Kathir dwells on longest. Even about an ordinary woman it would not be small. And: *a man may say a word that angers Allah without realising how far it will go, and be cast into Hell for it.*
- **"Those who love that immorality should spread among the believers"** — a third discipline, aimed at the appetite for scandal itself. With it, Thawban's hadith: *do not seek out the faults of your Muslim brother; whoever does, Allah will expose his faults, even if he hides in his house.*
- **"Follow not the footsteps of Shaytan."** Qatadah: every sin is one of his footsteps.

Then the turn that Ibn Kathir clearly loves. **Abu Bakr** had sworn never again to spend on **Mistah**, his poor relative who had joined the slander. **24:22** came down: *Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love that Allah should forgive you?* Abu Bakr's answer — *"Of course, by Allah, we love that You should forgive us"* — and he resumed the payments and swore never to stop. Ibn Kathir: this is why he deserved the name As-Siddiq.

**24:23** places slandering chaste believing women among the **seven destructive sins**. Ibn Kathir records the consensus that anyone who slanders A'ishah *after* this ayah is a disbeliever, for obstinacy against the Qur'an — and the same for all the Mothers of the believers. The Day comes when tongues, hands and feet testify; the Prophet ﷺ once smiled until his back teeth showed, describing a man who will insist on being his own only witness — and then his limbs will speak, and he will curse them.

**24:26 — "Good statements are for good people."** Ibn Abbas reads it of words; Abdur-Rahman bin Zayd of people. Either way the conclusion is the same: Allah would not have made her the wife of the best of mankind if she were not good. And *for them is forgiveness and honoured provision* — which Ibn Kathir takes as a promise that she is his wife in Paradise too.

## 5. Permission at the Door (24:27–29)

Ask, then greet. Three times, and if no answer comes, leave. **Abu Musa** did exactly that at Umar's door and was nearly beaten for it until the Ansar produced a witness — and Umar's admission is memorable: *"What kept me from knowing this was being busy in the marketplace."*

The details Ibn Kathir gathers are all about not startling people:

- Do not stand facing the door — stand to one side. In those days doorways had no curtains.
- Do not answer *"Who is that?"* with *"Me."* Jabir did, and the Prophet ﷺ disliked it — it tells the household nothing, which defeats the purpose.
- **Ibn Abbas** lists this among three ayat people have abandoned. Asked whether one must seek permission from orphan sisters living in the same house: *Yes.* Asked for a concession, he refused twice — *do you want to see them naked? do you want to obey Allah?*
- Ibn Mas'ud's wife **Zaynab** said he would clear his throat as he reached the door, so as never to come upon them suddenly.
- *"And if you are asked to go back, go back."* One of the Muhajirun admitted he tried all his life to obey this and could never quite do it cheerfully.

---

## 6. The Gaze and the Garment (24:30–31)

**To the men first.** Lower the gaze, guard the private parts. Jarir asked about the sudden, accidental glance and was told simply to turn away. When the Companions said they could not avoid sitting in the streets, the Prophet ﷺ told them to give the street its rights: *lower the gaze, return the greeting, enjoin good and forbid evil.*

*"That is purer for them"* — Ibn Kathir quotes: whoever restrains his gaze, Allah illuminates his heart. And the sober hadith that every son of Adam has his share of zina — the eye's zina is looking, the tongue's is speaking, the ear's is listening, the hand's is striking, the foot's is walking — and the private parts confirm it or deny it.

**Then to the women.** The occasion, per Muqatil bin Hayyan: women visiting a house in Bani Harithah entering with their anklets, chests and forelocks showing, and Asma' bint Murshidah exclaiming how ugly it was.

- *Except what is apparent* — Ibn Mas'ud, followed by Al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin, An-Nakha'i: the outer garment and what cannot be helped.
- *Draw their Khumur over their Juyub* — the head-covering brought down over neck and chest, in deliberate contrast to Jahiliyyah dress. A'ishah on the women of the early emigrants: when this ayah came down, they tore their waist-sheets and covered themselves with them. *"May Allah have mercy on them."*
- The list of those before whom adornment may be shown is a list of Mahrams — and **Ikrimah** notes the striking omission of paternal and maternal uncles, since they may describe her to their sons.
- No woman should describe another woman to her husband as though he were looking at her.
- *"Let them not stamp their feet."* They used to, so the anklets would be heard. Ibn Kathir generalises the principle: any movement calculated to reveal what is hidden falls under it — and so does perfume worn out of the house, and walking down the middle of the road rather than its sides.
- *"The male in-law is death"* — the warning about the relative who has easy access and is not Mahram.

The passage ends where a passage of law rarely ends: **"And all of you beg Allah to forgive you, O believers, that you may be successful."**

---

## 7. Marry, and Free (24:32–34)

**"Marry those among you who are single."** A command, says Ibn Kathir. *Al-Ayama* covers men and women, never-married and previously married alike.

- *O young men, whoever can afford to marry, let him marry — it restrains the gaze and guards the private parts. Whoever cannot, let him fast.*
- **"If they be poor, Allah will enrich them."** Ibn Mas'ud: *seek richness through marriage.* Three whom Allah has taken it upon Himself to help: the one who marries seeking chastity, the slave seeking to buy his freedom, and the one who fights in His cause. The Prophet ﷺ married a man who owned nothing but his waist-wrap and could not afford an iron ring — his Mahr was to teach her what he knew of the Qur'an.
- **Mukatabah.** If a servant asks for a contract of emancipation and has the means to earn, write it. **Ata'** thought it nothing less than obligatory; Umar whipped Anas for delaying Sirin's contract and recited the ayah at him. And *give them from the wealth of Allah* — most scholars, including Ibn Jarir, take this as their stated share of Zakah.
- **The prohibition of forcing slave-girls into prostitution.** Ibn Kathir names the man: Abdullah bin Ubayy again, and the women — Musaykah, Mu'adhah — who refused and came to the Prophet ﷺ. *"If anyone compels them, then after such compulsion Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful"* — Ibn Abbas: forgiving **to them**; the sin is on the one who forced them.

## 8. The Ayah of Light (24:35)

> *Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The parable of His Light is as a niche and within it a lamp: the lamp is in a glass, the glass as it were a brilliant star, lit from a blessed tree, an olive, neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would almost glow forth though no fire touched it. Light upon Light!*

**Ibn Abbas**: *Light of the heavens and the earth* means the Guide of their inhabitants; and with Mujahid, the One controlling their affairs, their stars, their sun and moon. The Prophet's ﷺ night prayer opened with it: *To You be praise, You are the Light of the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them.*

On *His Light*, Ibn Kathir gives two readings:

1. It refers to **Allah** — the parable of His light is as a niche (Ibn Abbas).
2. It refers to **the believer** — the light in his heart is as a niche. This is the reading the context supports, and Ibn Kathir develops it fully:

| The image | What it stands for |
|---|---|
| **The niche** | the chest, the place where the lamp is set |
| **The lamp** | the light itself — the Qur'an and the faith in his heart (**Ubayy bin Ka'b**) |
| **The glass** | the believer's heart, in its purity and clarity, like a jewel |
| **The oil** | the Qur'an and the Shariah — pure, with no impurity or deviation |
| **The tree, neither east nor west** | in open desert, unshaded by tree, mountain or cave; sun on it from dawn to dusk, so its oil is the purest of all |

**"Light upon Light."** Ibn Abbas: faith and deeds. And the illustration Ibn Kathir preserves — the light of the fire and the light of the oil: combined they shine, and neither gives light without the other. So too the light of the Qur'an and the light of faith.

The passage closes with the hadith of **four hearts**: the clear heart, lamp-lit, of the believer; the covered and tied heart of the disbeliever; the upside-down heart of the hypocrite who knows and then denies; and the armour-clad heart holding both faith and hypocrisy — faith in it like a sprout watered with good water, hypocrisy like a sore fed by blood and pus. Whichever prevails becomes the man.

---

## 9. The Houses Where the Lamp Belongs (24:36–38)

Having described the lit heart, Allah says where it is found: **in houses which Allah has ordered to be raised.** Ibn Abbas: raised, kept clean, and — pointedly — *He forbade idle talk in them.*

Ibn Kathir, who says he wrote a whole book on this subject, gathers:

- *Whoever builds a masjid seeking the Face of Allah, Allah builds him the like of it in Paradise.*
- Umar: build a place for people to worship, and beware of red and yellow decoration that distracts them. *I was not commanded to ornament the masjids* — Ibn Abbas: as the Jews and Christians did. And: the Hour will not come until people compete in building them.
- Not a place for lost camels or for trade. To the man announcing a lost camel: *May you never find it — the masjids were built only for what they were built for.*
- Twenty-five times the prayer at home or in the market; the angels praying for you as long as you remain in your place of prayer; and the glad tidings of **complete light on the Day of Resurrection** for those who walk to the masjids in the dark.

**"Men whom neither trade nor business diverts."** Ibn Umar was in the marketplace when the Iqamah was called; they shut their stores and walked in, and he recited this ayah. Not men without trade — men whose trade does not divert them. *That Allah may reward them according to the best of their deeds, and add even more out of His grace.*

## 10. Two Kinds of Darkness (24:39–40)

Two parables for two kinds of disbeliever:

**The mirage.** The one whose ignorance is *deep and compound* — who calls others to his disbelief and believes his deeds are good. Like a thirsty man crossing a flat plain toward what looks like a sea; when he arrives, *he finds it to be nothing, but he finds Allah with him, who will pay him his due.* Ibn Kathir pairs it with the deeds turned into scattered dust (25:23), and with the hadith of those shown Hell on that Day looking like water, who go to it and fall in.

**The dark sea.** The one whose ignorance is *simple* — the follower who knows nothing of whom he follows or where he is going. Waves over waves, cloud above them, darkness upon darkness; he stretches out his hand and can hardly see it. Ibn Kathir's illustration: *Where are you going? — With them. — Where are they going? — I don't know.* **Ubayy bin Ka'b** counts five darknesses: his speech, his deeds, his coming in, his going out, and his destination.

**"And he for whom Allah has appointed no light — for him there is no light."** Ibn Kathir sets it directly against 24:35's *Allah guides to His Light whom He wills*, and then breaks into prayer: *We ask Allah to put light in our hearts, and light on our right and our left, and to increase us in light.*

---

## 11. The Signs Nobody Argues With (24:41–46)

Having drawn the line between light and darkness, the surah turns outward to what is simply, visibly true.

- **Everything glorifies Him** — angels, people, jinn, animals, inanimate things, *and the birds with wings outspread.* Each has been guided to its own manner of prayer and glorification, and He knows each one.
- **The clouds**: driven gently while still weak, joined after being scattered, heaped in layers, rain emerging from the gaps. **Ubayd bin Umayr** names four winds — the scatterer, the generator, the joiner, the fertiliser. Hail sent down from mountains in the sky, striking whom He wills and averted from whom He wills. Lightning that nearly takes the sight of the eye that follows it.
- **Night and day**, one lengthened at the other's expense until they are equal, then reversed.
- **Every moving creature from water** — some on their bellies, some on two legs, some on four. One substance, every form.

## 12. "We Hear and We Obey" (24:47–54)

The hypocrite's tell, precisely diagnosed: he accepts arbitration when the ruling will favour him and refuses it when it will not. Ibn Kathir: *his initial acceptance was not because he believed it was the truth, but because it happened to match his desire.* Allah leaves only three possibilities — disease in the heart, doubt about the religion, or fear that Allah and His Messenger will be unjust — and all three are disbelief.

Against it: **"The only saying of the believers... is that they say: we hear and we obey."**

**Ubadah bin As-Samit**, present at Al-Aqabah and at Badr, gave this counsel on his deathbed: listen and obey in ease and in hardship, when you feel energetic and when you do not, and when you feel selfish; train your tongue to speak the truth; do not oppose those in authority — *unless they openly command you to disobey Allah, in which case follow the Book of Allah.*

## 13. The Promise of Succession (24:55–57)

> *Allah has promised those among you who believe and do righteous deeds that He will certainly grant them succession in the land... and will surely give them, in exchange after their fear, security — if they worship Me and associate nothing with Me.*

Ibn Kathir writes this section as a historian, tracing the promise being kept: Makkah, Khaybar, Bahrain, the Peninsula, Yemen; then Abu Bakr holding the Ummah together and sending armies to Persia, Syria and Egypt; then Umar, of whom he says that after the Prophets the world never saw his like in justice; then Uthman, and the reach east to China and west to the Atlantic. To Adiyy bin Hatim the Prophet ﷺ had promised that a woman would travel alone from Al-Hirah to make Tawaf fearing nobody, and that Kisra's treasures would be opened — and Adiyy lived to see both, and said the third would come too.

But **Abu Al-'Aliyah** records the other half honestly: they were safe through Abu Bakr, Umar and Uthman, *"until what happened happened"* — and then fear returned and they set guards. *They changed, so their situation changed.*

Ibn Kathir's own summary is unsparing: the Companions' victories were in proportion to their commitment, and when later people fell short in the commandments, their strength and victory fell short accordingly. The condition in the ayah — *if they worship Me and associate nothing with Me* — is the whole mechanism. Paired with it is Mu'adh's hadith on the donkey: the right of Allah over His servants is that they worship Him alone; the right of the servants over Allah, if they do, is that He will not punish them.

## 14. Three Times of Privacy, and the Shared Table (24:58–61)

Where 24:27 governed strangers, this governs the people already inside.

**Three times** when servants and pre-pubescent children must ask permission: before Fajr, during the afternoon rest when clothes are set aside, and after Isha. Outside those hours there is no blame on either side — they are the ones who move about the house serving you. When the children reach puberty, they ask at all times.

Ibn Kathir notes flatly that the ayah is clear, unabrogated, and almost universally neglected — Ibn Abbas denounced people for it, saying he still told his own servant-woman to ask permission. Asked whether it was abrogated, a scholar replied: *It has not been.* — *But people do not do it.* — *May Allah help them.*

**Elderly women** past the hope of marriage may set aside the outer garment, provided they make no display — *but to refrain is better for them.*

**24:61** is a quiet dismantling of social awkwardness. The blind, the lame and the sick had been excluded from shared meals — or excluded themselves — because they could not see the food, could not sit comfortably, or ate less than others, and everyone feared being unfair. Relatives' houses, and the houses whose keys you hold, and a friend's house, are all opened, so long as you know it does not upset them. And to the tribe of Banu Kinanah, who thought eating alone shameful enough that a man would go hungry beside his loaded camel waiting for company — and to the Muslims who had concluded from 4:29 that no one could eat at anyone's house at all — the ayah says simply: *No sin on you whether you eat together or apart.*

Ibn Kathir adds the balancing hadith anyway: *"We eat and are not satisfied." — "Perhaps you eat separately. Eat together and mention the Name of Allah, and it will be blessed for you."*

And then: **"When you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting from Allah, blessed and good."** Greet your own family. Mujahid: even entering an empty house, say *peace be upon us and upon the righteous servants of Allah* — and it is narrated that the angels return the greeting.

## 15. How to Address Him, and How to Leave (24:62–64)

Believers do not slip away from a shared matter — a Friday, an Eid, a consultation — without asking his permission. *Those who ask your permission, those are the ones who truly believe.*

**"Make not the calling of the Messenger among you as your calling one another."** Ibn Abbas: they used to say *O Muhammad,* or *O Abu Al-Qasim*; they were taught to say *O Prophet of Allah, O Messenger of Allah.* A second reading, also from Ibn Abbas: do not imagine his supplication against you is like anyone else's — his is answered.

Meanwhile the hypocrites were ducking behind the Companions to sneak out of the Friday khutbah, and **"Allah knows those of you who slip away under shelter."**

Then the warning the whole surah has been building toward:

> *And let those beware who oppose the Messenger's commandment, lest some Fitnah befall them or a painful torment be inflicted on them.*

Ibn Kathir reads *commandment* as his way, method and Sunnah — every word and deed measured against his, whoever the speaker, *for whoever does a deed not in accordance with this matter of ours will have it rejected.* And *Fitnah* here is internal: lest disbelief, hypocrisy or innovation enter the heart.

He closes with the parable of the fire: a man kindles a flame, and moths and insects begin hurling themselves into it, and he beats them back but they overwhelm him and fall in anyway. *This is the parable of me and you. I am seizing you by your waistbands, keeping you from the Fire, and you are plunging into it.*

The surah's last word is knowledge: **He knows your condition** — nothing hidden, not the weight of a speck — *and the Day when they will be brought back to Him, then He will inform them of what they did.*

## The Takeaway

An-Nur legislates almost exclusively for the unwatched hours: the doorway, the afternoon rest, the walk home, the conversation between two neighbours about a third. These are precisely the moments no court reaches. So the surah does two things at once — it sets hard, public, enforceable limits on the worst violations, and it plants at its centre a lamp in a niche, because the only sufficient enforcement is a heart that is already lit.

The pivot is the tree neither of the east nor of the west: unshaded, exposed to the sun from dawn to dusk, and for exactly that reason yielding the purest oil. Nothing shelters it, and nothing needs to.

Two lines carry the rest. **"You counted it a little thing, while with Allah it was very great"** — for every word repeated without verification. And **"Do you not love that Allah should forgive you?"** — the question that unlocked Abu Bakr's oath, and the surah's answer to what you should do about the person who wronged you.
