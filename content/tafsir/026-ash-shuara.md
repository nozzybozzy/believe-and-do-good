*Revealed in Makkah · 227 ayat · "The Poets" — named for four verses near the very end*

> **A note on the filename:** the PDF is labelled "Shura," but Surah 26 is **Ash-Shu'ara'**, *The Poets*. Ash-Shura is Surah 42. This summary covers what's actually in the file — Surah 26.

---

## 0. What This Surah Is Doing

Ash-Shu'ara' opens on a Prophet ﷺ grieving himself to the point of self-destruction because his people will not believe. The entire surah is Allah's answer to that grief, and the answer is not comfort — it is **perspective**.

Seven prophets are paraded past in sequence: Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb. Each faced the same denial. Each said, in nearly identical words, *"Will you not have Taqwa? I am a trustworthy Messenger to you. Have Taqwa of Allah, and obey me. No reward do I ask of you for it."* Each was rejected. Each was vindicated.

And after each story, the same two-line refrain falls like a gavel — **eight times in the surah**:

> *Verily, in this is indeed a sign, yet most of them are not believers.*
> *And verily your Lord, He is truly the All-Mighty, the Most Merciful.*

That repetition is the surah's architecture. The message to Muhammad ﷺ: *this is not happening to you; this is what always happens.* And the message to the deniers of Quraysh: *there is a pattern, and you are standing inside it.*

## 1. The Grief That Had to Be Restrained (26:1–9)

> *It may be that you are going to destroy yourself, that they do not become believers.*

Mujahid, Ikrimah, Qatadah, Ad-Dahhak, Al-Hasan and others gloss **Bakhi'** plainly: *kill yourself.* Ibn Kathir reads it as consolation — Allah easing the weight of a burden the Prophet ﷺ was carrying too far, the same way He does at 18:6.

Then comes one of the most important theological statements in the Qur'an about why the world is the way it is:

> *If We will, We could send down to them from the heaven a sign, to which they would bend their necks in humility.*

Ibn Kathir: **`if We so willed, We could send down a sign that would force them to believe, but We will not do that, because We do not want anyone to believe except by choice.`** Compulsion is available and deliberately withheld. Cross-referenced with 10:99 and 11:118 — Allah could have made mankind one Ummah; He did not.

**The Names at the end of the refrain are read as a pair with a purpose:**
- **Al-Aziz** (All-Mighty) — Abu Al-Aliyah, Qatadah, Ar-Rabi' and Ibn Ishaq: mighty in punishing those who defy Him.
- **Ar-Rahim** (Most Merciful) — Sa'id bin Jubayr: merciful to those who turn back. And Ibn Kathir's own gloss: *He does not hasten to punish the sinner; He gives him time to repent.*

Every one of the seven destruction narratives ends on **mercy**, not wrath. The delay you are living in is the mercy.

## 2. Musa and Fir'awn — The Longest Account (26:10–68)

**Musa's four objections, and how they were answered.** He raised them honestly: *I fear they will deny me · my breast straitens · my tongue does not express well · they have a charge of crime against me and I fear they will kill me.* The answer was not a rebuttal but a presence: ***"Go you both with Our signs. Verily, We shall be with you, listening."*** Ibn Kathir: *`I will be with you by My protection, care, support and help.`*

**The debt argument.** Fir'awn's opening move is emotional blackmail — *did we not raise you in our own house?* Musa's reply is the surah's sharpest single line of reasoning:

> *And this is the past favor with which you reproach me — that you have enslaved the Children of Israel.*

Ibn Kathir: *`Is there any comparison between your favors to one man among them and the evil you have done to all of them?`* A private kindness cannot be invoiced against a public crime.

**"And what is the Lord of Al-'Alamin?"** Ibn Kathir is emphatic here, and takes a swipe at the philosophers: this was **not** a question about Allah's nature or substance. Fir'awn denied the Creator existed at all — he had told his people *I know not that you have a god other than me* (43:54) — so he was in no position to ask about His essence. Those who read it as a metaphysical inquiry are mistaken.

Musa answers with creation itself (heavens, earth, everything between), then with lineage (*your Lord and the Lord of your ancient fathers* — i.e. the ones who came before Fir'awn existed), then with the argument that ends the debate: **Lord of the east and the west.** Ibn Kathir spells out the implicit challenge — *if Fir'awn is your god, let him reverse the sunrise* — and links it directly to Ibrahim's demolition of Nimrud at 2:258.

> **When Fir'awn was defeated in debate, he resorted to force.** Ibn Kathir marks this transition explicitly: proof had been established clearly and rationally, so he switched to threats of imprisonment. It is the recurring signature of falsehood throughout the surah.

**The sorcerers — the surah's great reversal.** They came for money (*will there surely be a reward?*), were promised access to power (*you shall be of those brought near*), and threw their ropes swearing *"By the might of Fir'awn!"* — which Ibn Kathir dismisses with a line that lands in any century: **`this is what the ignorant masses say when they do something: this is by the virtue of So-and-so!`**

Then the staff swallowed everything, and the men who had come as mercenaries **fell prostrate**. Why them and not the crowd? Because *they knew something their people did not* — as professionals, they alone could tell that what Musa did was not their craft.

Fir'awn threatened amputation and crucifixion. Their answer:

> ***"No harm! Surely, to our Lord we are to return."***

Ibn Kathir renders it: *`no problem, that will not harm us and we do not care.`* Faith that was hours old outlasted a lifetime of it in everyone around them. **`So he killed them all.`** — five words, and the tafsir moves on.

**The sea.** Ibn Abbas: it divided into **twelve paths**, one per tribe. As-Suddi adds that there were windows in the walls of water so they could see one another. Musa's line as the army closed in — *"Nay, verily with me is my Lord. He will guide me"* — is Ibn Kathir's model of certainty under pressure: *`Allah is the One Who commanded me to bring you here, and He does not go back on His promise.`*

A quiet detail: on the way out, Musa asked after the grave of **Yusuf**, and an old woman of the Children of Israel showed him where it was, so his remains left Egypt with them — some say Musa carried them himself, fulfilling the will Yusuf had left generations earlier.

## 3. Ibrahim — The Question That Dismantles Inherited Religion (26:69–104)

Ibrahim's method is interrogation, not assertion. *What do you worship? Do they hear you when you call? Do they benefit you or cause harm?*

Their answer is the only one they have: ***"But we found our fathers doing so."*** Ibn Kathir's verdict is devastating in its simplicity — **`they knew that their idols could not do anything, but they had seen their fathers doing this, so they made haste to follow in their footsteps.`** They were not deceived. They were incurious.

**The seven-fold portrait of the Lord worth worshipping** (26:78–82) — Ibrahim defines God by what He actually does:

> *Who created me, and He guides me · Who feeds me and gives me drink · **And when I am ill, it is He Who cures me** · Who will cause me to die, then bring me to life · And Who, I hope, will forgive me my faults on the Day of Recompense.*

Ibn Kathir stops on the illness verse for a point of **adab** with enormous implications: Ibrahim **attributes the sickness to himself**, though Allah decrees it, *out of respect towards Allah*. This is the same grammar as Al-Fatihah — guidance and favour said outright, the wrath left in the passive voice, the straying attributed to the people. Even the Jinn speak this way (72:10). **Good is named to Him; hardship is phrased around Him.**

**The prayer, and its one revoked clause.** Ibrahim asks for *Hukm* (Ibn Abbas: knowledge), to be joined with the righteous, for *an honorable mention in later generations*, for Paradise — and for his father's forgiveness. Ibn Kathir notes this last one was **later withdrawn** (9:114, 60:4): Ibrahim stopped once it became clear his father died an enemy of Allah. The hadith in Bukhari of Ibrahim meeting Azar on the Day of Resurrection, and being answered *"I have forbidden Paradise to the disbelievers,"* is the hardest passage in the surah — and the clearest statement that lineage saves no one.

**Qalb Salim — the clean heart** (26:89). The one currency that clears on that Day, when *neither wealth nor sons will avail*:
- **Ibn Kathir**: free from any impurity or Shirk.
- **Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib**: the sound heart.
- **Ibn Sirin**: the heart that knows Allah is true, that the Hour is coming, and that Allah will resurrect those in the graves.
- **Abu Uthman An-Nisaburi**: the heart free from innovation and content with the Sunnah.

**The argument in the Fire** (26:96–102). The followers turn on the leaders, then on themselves: *By Allah, we were truly in a manifest error, when we held you as equals with the Lord of all that exists.* Ibn Kathir's gloss: *`we obeyed your commands as we should have obeyed the commands of the Lord of all that exists.`* **Shirk here is not a statue — it is obedience given to a man that belonged to Allah.**

Then: *"Now we have no intercessors, nor a close friend."* And *"if we only had a chance to return..."* — to which Ibn Kathir gives the flat answer: *Allah knows that if they were to come back, they would only go back to doing forbidden things, and He knows that they are liars.*

## 4. The Four Short Nations — Variations on One Theme (26:105–191)

Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb. Each opens identically — *they denied the Messengers* (plural: Ibn Kathir notes that **denying one messenger is denying them all**) — and each carries a distinct diagnosis of the sin underneath.

| Prophet | Nation | The sin diagnosed | The end |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nuh** | His people | Snobbery — *"Shall we believe when the inferior follow you?"* | The laden ship; the rest drowned |
| **Hud** | 'Ad | Vanity building and brutality | The Sarsar wind — seven nights, eight days |
| **Salih** | Thamud | Assumed permanence; killing the she-camel | Earthquake and Sayhah |
| **Lut** | Sodom | Transgression against created order | Rain of baked-clay stones |
| **Shu'ayb** | Al-Aykah / Madyan | Fraud in weights and measures | The Day of the Shadow |

**Nuh and the class objection.** They would follow him if he dismissed the poor believers first. His reply is one of the noblest in the Qur'an: ***"And I am not going to drive away the believers."*** Ibn Kathir: *`whoever obeys me and believes in me, then he belongs to me and I to him, whether he is noble or common, upper-class or lower-class.`* He also refuses to audit their pasts — *what does it have to do with me what they used to do? Their account is only with my Lord.*

**'Ad and the architecture of ego.** Hud's charge — *do you build on every Ri' a landmark for amusement, and take for yourselves fortresses as if you will live forever?* Ibn Kathir denounces it as **a waste of time that exhausted people's bodies for no purpose**, built not from need but to display strength. Their punishment was calibrated to their pride: the strongest, fiercest people on earth were destroyed by something stronger and fiercer — a freezing wind that lifted men and dropped them on their heads, leaving them *like hollow trunks of date palms* (69:7). Their mountain fortresses and ditches did nothing.

> **A note on "Iram of the pillars."** Ibn Kathir rejects the popular idea that Iram was a lost city. The verse says *the like of which were not created in the land* — created, not built. Had a city been meant, it would have said *the like of which was not built.* The city theory, he says, comes from Israelite narrations via Ka'b and Wahb, and **has no real basis**.

**Thamud and the illusion of security.** Salih's question is the sharpest in the section: ***"Will you be left secure in that which you have here?"*** — gardens, springs, ripe date-clusters, houses carved from mountain rock with great skill. Ibn Kathir reconciles two readings of that skill (mastery vs. extravagance): both are true, since **they carved dwellings they did not need, as a form of play**.

**Lut.** Ibn Kathir locates Sodom precisely — the putrid lake in Al-Ghur near the Jerusalem mountains — and notes the sin *was unknown on earth before their time*. The response to a warning was expulsion: *if you cease not, you will be one of those driven out.* Only Lut's wife stayed behind, and was destroyed with those who remained.

**Shu'ayb and the ethics of the scale.** The command is stated four ways to close every loophole: give full measure, weigh with the true balance, don't shortchange, don't corrupt the land. Ibn Kathir's summary is a whole business ethic in seven words: ***`Give as you take, and take as you give.`***

He also settles a scholarly dispute here: **Al-Aykah and Madyan were one nation**, described differently. He is not called *their brother* Shu'ayb, because Al-Aykah was the tree they deified, and the name carried their idolatry — so Allah did not attach him to it, though by blood he was their brother.

**The Day of the Shadow — punishment as exact recompense.** They demanded *a piece of the heaven to fall on us*, so Allah gave them exactly that, in a form they never anticipated. Ibn Abbas's account: seven days of intense heat drove them from their houses into the fields; then a cloud came and shaded them; they called each other to gather beneath its coolness; and when all were assembled, the fire came down.

> Ibn Kathir draws a broader lesson from comparing all three Qur'anic accounts of Shu'ayb's people: **the punishment is always shaped to the crime.** In Al-A'raf they threatened to drive him out, so an *earthquake* seized them. In Hud they mocked his prayer, so the *Sayhah* silenced them. Here they demanded a falling sky, so *the sky fell.*

## 5. Where the Qur'an Came From — and Where It Did Not (26:192–223)

The final movement drops the storytelling and addresses Quraysh's three theories about Muhammad ﷺ directly.

**It is revelation** (26:192–195): brought down by **Ar-Ruh Al-Amin — Jibril** (a point Ibn Kathir says is beyond dispute, citing Ibn Abbas, Qatadah, As-Suddi, Az-Zuhri and many more), *upon your heart* — *`free from any contamination, with nothing added or taken away`* — in **plain Arabic**, so that no one could claim they hadn't understood.

**It was foretold** (26:196–197): mentioned in the *Zubur* (Books) of earlier peoples. The honest scholars among the Children of Israel found his description in their own scriptures — Abdullah bin Salam, Salman Al-Farisi and others confirmed it by converting.

**And the denial was never about evidence** (26:198–201). Ibn Kathir's assessment of Quraysh is brutal: if this Book had been revealed to a non-Arab who couldn't speak a word of Arabic, *they still would not have believed*. Open a gate in heaven and let them climb it, they'd call it an illusion (6:111). **`They will not believe in it until they see the painful torment.`** And by then it is too late — as it was for Fir'awn, who believed only while drowning.

**Not from the Shayatin** (26:210–212). Three reasons, laid out in order: they **wouldn't want to** (their nature is corruption; this Book commands good and forbids evil), they **couldn't** (had it come down on a mountain, the mountain would have split in fear — 59:21), and they **weren't allowed near it** (the heavens were filled with guards and shooting stars during its revelation, so not one letter could be intercepted).

**Warn your nearest kin** (26:214). The Prophet ﷺ climbed As-Safa and called out the clans by name: *if I told you cavalry was about to attack from behind this mountain, would you believe me?* They said yes. *"Then I warn you of a great punishment close at hand."* Abu Lahab's sneer — *may you perish, is this why you called us?* — brought down Surah 111.

And to his own daughter, in the version A'ishah narrates: *"O Fatimah, daughter of Muhammad... **I cannot help you before Allah.** Ask me for whatever you want of my wealth."* The one man who could intercede on the Day of Judgement telling his own child that proximity to him is not a plan.

**Then: "be kind and humble to the believers who follow you"** — the same instruction Nuh lived out, appearing again at the end of the surah, now as a command.

## 6. The Poets, and Why the Surah Bears Their Name (26:221–227)

Quraysh had two explanations for the Qur'an: he's a *soothsayer*, or he's a *poet*. The surah answers both in six verses.

**On soothsayers:** the Shayatin descend on *every lying, sinful person who gives ear* — and Ibn Kathir explains the mechanism with the hadiths from Bukhari. The Jinn eavesdrop on the angels, catch a true word, pass it down the chain, and the fortune-teller **adds a hundred lies to it**. People believe him because of the one true thing. *"That is a word of truth which the Jinn snatches, then he gabbles it like the clucking of a chicken into the ear of his friend."*

**On poets:** *they speak about every subject* — Ibn Abbas: *`they indulge in every kind of nonsense`* — **and they say what they do not do.** That last charge is the surah's definition of the problem. Not that verse is forbidden, but that the poets of the time were **eloquence without accountability**: performers who could argue any side and lived none of it.

**Then the exception, and it matters** (26:227): *except those who believe, do righteous deeds, remember Allah much, and vindicate themselves after being wronged.* Hasan bin Thabit, Abdullah bin Rawahah and Ka'b bin Malik came to the Prophet ﷺ **weeping** — *"Allah knew when He revealed this that we are poets"* — and he recited them the exception and said: *this means you.*

> Ibn Kathir flags an honest problem here and refuses to paper over it: **the surah is Makkan, so how can the Ansar's poets be the reason for its revelation?** *"This is something worth thinking about."* The narrations about it are all *Mursal* and can't be relied on. His conclusion is broader and better: the exception covers *any* poet who turns — including the Jahiliyyah poets who had attacked Islam, then repented and used the same tongue to defend it. Abdullah bin Az-Zab'ari, on becoming Muslim: *"my tongue will try to make up for things it said when I was bad."*

And the Prophet ﷺ to Hasan: ***"Ridicule them in verse, and Jibril is with you."*** To Ka'b: *"The believer wages Jihad with his sword and with his tongue — it is as if you are attacking them with arrows."*

**The talent was never the problem. The disconnection between the words and the life was.**

## If You Remember Nothing Else

**Allah could have forced everyone to believe, and chose not to.** The whole drama of the surah exists inside that deliberate restraint. *If We will, We could send down a sign to which they would bend their necks* — and He will not, *because He does not want anyone to believe except by choice.*

**Seven prophets, one script.** *Will you not have Taqwa? I am trustworthy. I ask no reward.* If the message never changes and the rejection never changes, then rejection tells you nothing about the truth of the message. That is the consolation offered to a grieving Prophet ﷺ, and it works in reverse too: your being disbelieved is not evidence you are wrong, and your being believed is not evidence you are right.

**Eight times: *the All-Mighty, the Most Merciful.*** Every account of annihilation closes on mercy. Ibn Kathir's reading — mighty in punishing, merciful in delaying — means the time you are currently living in is itself the mercy, and it is not unlimited.

**What arrives on the Day is a heart, not a portfolio.** *Neither wealth nor sons will avail — except him who brings to Allah a **qalb salim**.* Ibrahim couldn't save his father; Muhammad ﷺ told Fatimah he couldn't save her. Nothing transfers.

**And say what you do.** The surah is named for the poets and their single named flaw — *they say what they do not do* — with the exemption reserved for those who believe, act, remember Allah much, and defend the truth with the gift they were given. **The tongue is not condemned. The gap between the tongue and the life is.**
