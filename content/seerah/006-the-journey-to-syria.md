Abu Talib joined a Quraysh trading caravan bound for Syria, and his nephew, then about twelve, would not be left behind.

At Busra there lived a monk called Bahira. The caravans had passed his cell for years and he had never once come out to them. This time he did, and he had prepared food, and he asked for all of them.

He searched the party until he found the boy, took his hand, and said what he had been waiting to say: this is the master of the worlds, the Messenger of the Lord of the worlds. Allah will send him as a mercy to the worlds.

The elders asked how he could possibly know. He told them what he had watched on the road: as the caravan came down from the direction of al-Aqabah, no tree and no stone had failed to bow, and they bow to no one but a prophet. He had also seen, below the shoulder, the seal of prophethood, shaped like an apple.

Then he gave Abu Talib advice that was not about honour but about danger: take him back to Makkah, and do not let the Byzantines learn who he is. Abu Talib sent him home.

Two things are worth holding here. The first is that the description Bahira was working from existed in writing, in the hands of the People of the Book, before the boy was old enough to trade, which is precisely the Qur'an's own claim in 7:157 and 61:6.

The second is quieter. The signs were legible to a stranger on a trade road decades before they were accepted by the men who had watched him grow up.
