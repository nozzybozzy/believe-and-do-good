The Fijar war — the sacrilegious war, fought in the months in which fighting was forbidden — had run for years between Quraysh and Hawazin and cost a great many lives. The Prophet ﷺ, then a young man, took no part in the fighting.

When it ended, Makkah had to confront what it had become. A merchant from outside the city could be robbed of his goods by a Makkan and have no recourse at all, because he had no clan in the valley to threaten anyone on his behalf. Tribal law protected members; it did nothing for strangers.

Representatives of the Makkan clans gathered in the house of Abdullah ibn Jud'an and swore a pact: they would stand together on the side of anyone wronged in Makkah, whoever he was and whoever had wronged him, until his right was restored. It was called **Hilf al-Fudul**.

He was about twenty, and he was there. Long afterwards, as a Prophet with a community and a state behind him, he said he had been present at that pact, that he would not trade his part in it for the finest wealth, and that if he were called to it in Islam he would answer.

That endorsement is doing real work. The pact was sworn by idolaters, in a city of idols, with no revelation behind it. Islam did not therefore discard it. What was just in it was just, and justice does not become void because the people who reached it were wrong about God.

The standard he would spend his life teaching — that creed is tested on conduct toward those who cannot repay you — he had already put his name to at twenty.
