Before the Hijrah, the Aws and the Khazraj had finally agreed to end their wars by appointing a single leader over the city. The man they had settled on was **Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul**. The crown was, by his own reckoning, days away.

Then the Prophet ﷺ arrived, and the city gave itself to him instead.

Ibn Ubayy was too shrewd to oppose that openly. He read the balance of power correctly and took the only route left: profess Islam publicly, and work against it privately. Those who followed him became known as the **Munafiqun**.

They are a constant presence for the next ten years. They withdrew three hundred men from the army on the way to Uhud. They built a rival mosque as a base for sedition. They spread the slander against A'ishah. They said openly on the road back from a campaign that the noble would drive out the contemptible from Madinah. When they were asked to come and seek forgiveness, they turned their heads away.

An entire surah is named for them, and the Qur'an gives them more sustained attention than it gives the idolaters of Makkah — because a declared enemy outside the walls is a simpler problem than a man in the prayer row behind you.

Allah informed the Prophet ﷺ of exactly who they were. He confided the list to one companion, **Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman**, and to no one else — and never used it. He did not purge them, expose them, or execute them.

The restraint was deliberate, and he gave the reason: so that people would not say that Muhammad kills his companions.

He judged by what people declared, and left what was in their hearts to Allah. That principle — that we rule on the outward and do not open people's chests — has governed Islamic law ever since.
