The city emptied into the streets. Men and women stood to see him; children sang. Yathrib was never Yathrib again after that day — it became **Madinat an-Nabi**, the City of the Prophet.

Then came a problem that could have started a feud on day one. Every clan wanted him as their guest, and several took hold of his camel's reins to make the claim. In a city whose recent history was inter-clan warfare, choosing one household over another was not a small matter.

He would not choose. **Leave her**, he said — *she is under command.* Wherever she stops is where I stay.

The camel walked on through the city, and knelt on an open plot of land, and then rose and went a little further, and returned and knelt again in the same place.

The nearest house belonged to **Abu Ayyub al-Ansari**, of Banu an-Najjar — the clan of the Prophet's own great-grandmother, so his maternal relatives in the city. Abu Ayyub had already carried the saddle inside before anyone could argue.

He stayed there while the mosque was built. Abu Ayyub insisted on giving him the ground floor and living above him — then could not bear the thought of walking over the Prophet's head, and begged him to swap.

The same instinct that solved the Black Stone thirty years earlier solved this: find the answer that leaves no clan defeated.
