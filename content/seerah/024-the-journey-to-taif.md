With Abu Talib dead and Makkah hardening, he took the message elsewhere. Ta'if lay about sixty kilometres away, a town of gardens and another tribe.

They did not merely refuse him. They mocked him, and then they lined the streets with their youths and slaves and had him stoned out of the town. He bled until the blood filled his sandals and dried there. Zayd ibn Harithah, who was with him, took blows meant for his head.

He got clear of the town and stopped in a garden, and there, exhausted, bleeding, rejected by two cities, he made the dua that is the closest the seerah comes to letting us hear him alone. He complains of his weakness and his helplessness and his lowness before people. He asks whose hands He is entrusting him to. And then comes the turn the whole dua exists for: *if You are not angry with me, I do not care.*

Not *make it stop*. Not *punish them*. Only: as long as You are not displeased with me, none of this matters.

The owners of the garden sent grapes to him by their Christian slave, **Addas**, who found himself talking to a man in a Makkan garden about Yunus ibn Matta, and kissed his head.

Then Jibril came, with the angel of the mountains, who offered to crush Ta'if between the two hills of the valley, the word was his to give.

He refused. Perhaps, he said, Allah will bring from their descendants those who worship Him alone.

He was right. The people who threw the stones did not accept Islam. Their grandchildren did.

This is what *We sent you only as a mercy to the worlds* (21:107) means in practice, on the worst day, with the authority to do otherwise sitting in his hand.
