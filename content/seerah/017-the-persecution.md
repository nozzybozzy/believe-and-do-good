The message drew people, and that was the problem. It did not merely offend Makkan religion. It threatened the position of the men who ran Makkan religion. So they forbade it, and when forbidding failed they turned to force.

The violence sorted itself by social protection, as violence does. A man with a strong clan was insulted. A man with no clan was tortured.

**Bilal**, the Abyssinian slave of Umayyah ibn Khalaf, was dragged through the streets with a rope around his neck, starved for days, and laid out on the burning sand at midday with a boulder on his chest while his master told him to renounce or die. What came out of him was one word, repeated: **Ahad. Ahad.** *One. One.* He was being tortured for the sake of Allah's oneness, and the only thing he would say was the oneness.

**Ammar ibn Yasir** was tortured with his mother and father. His mother **Sumayyah** was killed with a spear, the first person to be killed for Islam. Ammar, under torture, was eventually forced to say what they wanted. He came to the Prophet ﷺ in pieces about it, and the verse came down: unless it is one *whose heart is at rest in faith* while he is compelled (16:106). The law made room for what the human body cannot carry.

**Khabbab ibn al-Aratt**, **az-Zubayr** and others endured the same.

They did not spare the Prophet ﷺ either. Once, praying in the Haram, a rope was put round his neck and he was throttled. Another time, while he was in sujud, the entrails of a camel were dropped on his back, so heavy he could not rise until his daughter Zaynab came and pulled it off him, crying.

The Qur'an's answer to all of this was not a promise that it would stop. It was 29:2: *Do people think they will be left to say "we believe" and not be tested?*
